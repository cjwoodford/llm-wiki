# Charles Taylor

**Charles Margrave Taylor** (born 1931) is a Canadian philosopher and Professor Emeritus at McGill University. Internationally renowned for his monumental contributions to political philosophy, philosophical anthropology, hermeneutics, and the history of ideas, Taylor is the recipient of the Kyoto Prize, the Templeton Prize, the Berggruen Prize, and the Kluge Prize.

His magnum opuses—*Sources of the Self* (1989), *The Malaise of Modernity* (1991), *A Secular Age* (2007), and *The Language Animal* (2016)—provide a foundational critique of modern disembodied individualism, instrumental reason, and the mechanistic reduction of human consciousness.

## 💡 Key Contributions & Philosophical Frameworks

### 1. The Porous Self vs. The Buffered Self (*A Secular Age*, 2007)
- **The Porous Self (Pre-Modern)**:
  - The self was experientially open and permeable to the world. Meaning, spiritual entities, demons, and moral forces existed in external objects and landscapes (*enchanted cosmos*). The boundary between mind and world was fluid.
- **The Buffered Self (Modern)**:
  - The Cartesian-Lockean revolution erected a rigid psychic boundary. Meaning and intentionality are quarantined exclusively inside the detached human skull, while the external world is rendered as inert, inanimate matter governed by mechanical laws.
- **The Immanent Frame**:
  - Modern secular culture operates within a self-contained cosmic and social architecture where all explanations are confined to natural, impersonal, and computable processes—closing off transcendent openness.
- **Critique of "Subtraction Stories"**:
  - Taylor refutes the Enlightenment narrative that secularization is simply the passive loss or "subtraction" of religious illusion, demonstrating that modernity represents the active, historical construction of a new existential condition and moral order.

### 2. The Malaise of Modernity & Instrumental Reason (1991)
- Identifies three central malaises plaguing modern civilization:
  1. **Individualism & Loss of Heroic Horizon**: The dark side of individual liberty is atomism, self-absorption, and the flattening of shared heroic and cosmic purpose.
  2. **The Primacy of Instrumental Reason**: The relentless calculation of maximum efficiency and cost-benefit optimization, reducing human beings, ecological webs, and living bodies to raw standing-reserve (*Gestell*).
  3. **Loss of Political Freedom**: Atomized, inward-turned individuals surrender civic agency to soft, technocratic paternalism and bureaucratic control.

### 3. Sources of the Self & Strong Evaluation (1989)
- Formulates the concept of **Strong Evaluation**: human agents do not merely possess desires; they qualitatively evaluate desires as higher/lower, noble/base, meaningful/trivial.
- Human life is lived within inescapable **moral frameworks** and "horizons of significance"—structures of meaning that cannot be reduced to utility functions, behavioral conditioning, or computational optimization.

### 4. Philosophy of Language: Expressivism vs. Computational Designativism (*The Language Animal*, 2016)
- Distinguishes two rival traditions in the philosophy of language:
  - **The H-L-C Tradition (Hobbes-Locke-Condillac)**:
    - *Designative-Instrumental*: Views words merely as arbitrary signifiers attached to pre-existing mental objects or external entities. This is the foundational philosophical premise of the [[Machine Functionalism and Language of Thought|Computational Theory of Mind]] and Large Language Model tokenization.
  - **The H-H-H Tradition (Hamann-Herder-von Humboldt)**:
    - *Constitutive-Expressivist*: Language does not merely point to things already conceived; it **constitutes** new modes of awareness, interpersonal communion, ethical sensitivity, and human world-disclosure.
- Proves that mechanistic, algorithmic systems cannot replicate human language because they lack the expressive embodiment, bodily attunement, and existential concern that give symbols meaning.
