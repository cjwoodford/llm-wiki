# Herbert Simon

**Herbert A. Simon** (1916–2001) was an American polymath whose pioneering contributions spanned computer science, cognitive psychology, economics, philosophy of science, and sociology. Simon co-created the **Logic Theorist** with [[Allen Newell]], formulated the concept of **Bounded Rationality** (winning the 1978 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics), co-won the 1975 ACM Turing Award, and received the APA Distinguished Scientific Contribution Award.

---

## 💡 Key Contributions & Philosophical Positions

### 1. Bounded Rationality & "Satisficing" (1955)
- Dismantled classical economics' assumption of *Homo economicus* (the perfectly rational agent with infinite computational capacity and complete information).
- Introduced **Bounded Rationality**: real human decision-makers face severe cognitive constraints, incomplete information, and time limits.
- Coined **"Satisficing"** (satisfy + suffice): agents use heuristics to find a "good enough" solution rather than searching for the globally optimal solution.

### 2. The Logic Theorist & The 1956 MIT Breakthrough
- Co-developed the **Logic Theorist** (1956) with [[Allen Newell]] and J.C. Shaw, presented on **[[The 1956 Foundations of AI and Cognitive Science#The MIT Symposium on Information Theory (September 10–12, 1956)|September 11, 1956, at MIT]]**.
- Proved 38 theorems of Whitehead and Russell's *Principia Mathematica*, showing that human heuristics could be formalized and run on digital machinery.
- Co-created the **General Problem Solver (GPS)** (1959) and the **Physical Symbol System Hypothesis (PSSH)** (1976).

### 3. Bridging Computer Science & Cognitive Psychology
- Simon famously held appointments in both Computer Science and Psychology at Carnegie Mellon University.
- Used computer simulation programs (*EPAM*, *BACON*) as empirical psychological models to explain human memory, verbal learning, scientific discovery, and chess mastery (*Human Problem Solving*, 1972).

### 4. The Architecture of Complexity & Near-Decomposability (1962)
- In *"The Architecture of Complexity"*, argued that complex evolving systems (biological, social, and computational) naturally organize into hierarchical, **nearly-decomposable** modular subsystems, deeply influencing both software engineering and the philosophy of biology.
