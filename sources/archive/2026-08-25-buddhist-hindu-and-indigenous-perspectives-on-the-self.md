# Buddhist, Hindu and Indigenous Perspectives on the Self

While Buddhism often emphasizes *anatta* (non-self) and Hinduism centers on the *atman* (the eternal, abiding self), indigenous traditions—which are incredibly diverse across the globe—tend to view the "self" not as a single, isolated entity, but as a **relational, plural, and ecologically embedded process.**

If the Hindu self is a "spark" of the divine and the Buddhist self is a "mirage," the indigenous self is more like a **weave in a living tapestry.**

---

### 1. The Plurality of Souls

Many indigenous cultures do not view the "soul" as a single unit. Instead, they often describe a **multiplicity of souls** or vital forces that inhabit a single person.

- **The Dual Soul:** In many North American and Siberian traditions, there is a distinction between a "body-soul" (which provides life force and breath) and a "free-soul" (which can travel during dreams or trance).
- **The Nahua (Aztec) Concept:** The self was seen as a composite of three main forces: *tonalli* (heat/destiny in the head), *teyolia* (knowledge/memory in the heart), and *ihiyotl* (breath/passion in the liver).
- **The Andean *Camay*:** In Quechua traditions, identity is linked to *camay*, a circulating life force. A person is not a "thing" that *has* a soul, but a site where these forces temporarily gather.

### 2. The Relational or "Extended" Self

In most indigenous worldviews, the boundary of the "self" does not stop at the skin. This is often summarized by the Southern African philosophy of **Ubuntu**: *"I am because we are."* * **Kinship with the Non-Human:** The self includes ancestors, future generations, and the "more-than-human" world (animals, plants, mountains, and rivers). If the river is sick, a part of the self is literally sick.

- **Reciprocity (*Ayni*):** In many Amazonian and Andean cultures, the self exists only through constant exchange. You are defined by what you give and receive within your community and ecosystem.

### 3. The Self as a "Place"

Rather than an abstract "I," the self is often tied to a specific geography.

- **Dreaming (Australian Aboriginal):** A person’s identity is inextricably linked to a specific "track" or "story" of the land. The self is a localized expression of the Earth’s own Dreaming.
- **Porosity:** Unlike the "buffered" Western self, the indigenous self is often "porous." It is constantly influenced by external spirits, land-energies, and community rituals.

> If one had to find similarities, it seems that these indigenous views are more like the Buddhist view than the Hindu view

That is an astute observation. If we place these three on a spectrum of "substantiality"—how much of a solid, permanent "thing" the self is—the **Indigenous** and **Buddhist** views sit much closer to each other than to the Vedantic **Hindu** view of an eternal, unchanging *Atman*.

Both Indigenous and Buddhist perspectives reject the idea of a "buffered," independent ego that exists separately from the world. However, they arrive at this "porous" self from slightly different angles.

---

### Points of Convergence: Indigenous vs. Buddhist

The similarity lies primarily in the **process-oriented** nature of the self. Neither sees the "I" as a static monument; instead, they see it as a shifting flow.

- **The Rejection of the "Ego-Box":** Both views argue that the Western/Modern concept of a self-contained individual is a delusion.
- **Interdependence (*Pratītyasamutpāda*):** The Buddhist concept of "Dependent Origination" (nothing exists in isolation) mirrors the Indigenous "Relational Self." In both, if you pull on one thread of the "self," you find the entire universe attached to it.
- **The Self as a Composite:** Just as Buddhism describes the five *skandhas* (aggregates) that come together to create the illusion of a person, many Indigenous traditions see the "self" as a temporary gathering of multiple souls, ancestral shadows, and environmental forces.

---

### The Crucial Divergence: "Emptiness" vs. "Fullness"

While they agree the self isn't a "thing," they differ on what the self *is* made of:

1. **Buddhism (The Path of Subtraction):** The goal is often to see through the illusion of self to realize **Sunyata** (Emptiness). The self is like a "mirage"—it looks real from a distance, but when you investigate it, there’s nothing there.
2. **Indigenous (The Path of Addition):** The self isn't "empty"; it is **"too full."** It is teeming with ancestors, animal spirits, and the life of the land. It isn't a mirage; it’s a **communal intersection.** While a Buddhist might try to detach from the "aggregates," an Indigenous practitioner seeks to deepen their "knottedness" within them.

### Why this matters for your work

This distinction seems particularly relevant to your critique of **transhumanism**. Transhumanism often treats the "self" as **information/data** that can be extracted from the body—a hyper-modern version of the "abiding soul," but stripped of divinity.

The Indigenous and Buddhist views offer a powerful "Idealist" alternative:

- The **Buddhist** view warns that trying to "upload" a self is trying to preserve a mirage.
- The **Indigenous** view warns that a self removed from its "web" (ecology, body, ancestors) isn't a self at all; it’s a severed limb.
