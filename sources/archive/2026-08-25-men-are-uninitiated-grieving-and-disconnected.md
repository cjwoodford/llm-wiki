# Men are uninitiated, grieving, and disconnected

These thinkers, spanning from the 1990s mythopoetic movement to current ecological philosophy, share a central thesis: **Men are not "toxic" by nature; they are uninitiated, grieving, and disconnected. #masculinity** 

While the "symptoms" they identify are similar (violence, depression, passivity, addiction), their diagnoses of the *root cause* differ slightly, moving from the psychological (Bly) to the cosmic (Fox/Plotkin) to the ecological (Strand).

Here is a summary of their assessments.

### 1. Robert Bly: The Grief of the "Fatherless" Son

- The Diagnosis: "Father Hunger" and the Industrial Revolution.

    Bly’s core argument in Iron John is that the Industrial Revolution broke the "vertical line" of masculinity. Before this, fathers and sons worked together (on farms, in guilds). When fathers left the home for factories and offices, they became "demons" or ghosts—distant providers who returned home exhausted and emotionally empty.

- **The Result:** The "Soft Male." Bly argued that in the absence of fathers, boys were raised almost exclusively by mothers. To please the mother, men became "nice," agreeable, and disconnected from their aggression.1 However, because they never accessed their "Wild Man" (primitive, distinct masculine energy), they lack vitality and resolve.
- **The Missing Piece:** **Initiation.** In tribal cultures, older men forcefully pull the boy away from the mother to initiate him into the world of men. **Modern society has no elders and no rituals, leaving men in a state of perpetual, uninitiated boyhood.**

### 2. Matthew Fox: The Exile of the "Sacred Masculine"

- **The Diagnosis: Spiritual lobotomy via "Empire."**

    Fox, a theologian, argues that Western religion and culture have committed "deicide" on the masculine soul by stripping God of nature and replacing Him with a distant, punishing "Sky God." He believes men are suffering from a dualistic worldview that tells them matter, bodies, and the Earth are dirty or sinful.

- **The Result:** Masculinity becomes defined by "Power Over" (Empire/Dominion) rather than "Power With" (Creativity/Generativity). Men feel they must be soldiers or CEOs—destroyers or accumulators—because they have lost the archetypes of the "Green Man" (protector of the Earth) and the "Blue Man" (the cosmic, expanding consciousness of Shiva or the Cosmic Christ).
- **The Missing Piece:** **Awe.** Fox argues men are bored and violent because they are spiritually starved. They need to reconnect with the "Cosmic Christ"—the idea that the masculine is sacred not because it dominates, but because it generates life.2

### 3. Bill Plotkin: The Trap of "Patho-Adolescence"

- The Diagnosis: Egocentricity and Nature-Deficit.

    Plotkin takes a developmental view. He argues that a true "Adult" is someone who has discovered their unique "mythos" or soul-purpose through a descent into nature. Our current consumer society, however, is designed to keep people in a state of "patho-adolescence"—focused on social conformity, material security, and ego-gratification.

- **The Result:** Most "older" men are not Elders; they are just aging teenagers guarding their pile of toys. They feel lost because they are trying to fit into a *social* slot (job, status) rather than a *ecological* niche. They have never encountered their own Soul.
- **The Missing Piece:** **The Descent.** Plotkin argues you cannot think your way into manhood. You must wander in the wild (Vision Quest) until the "more-than-human world" tells you who you are. Without this "ego-death," a man remains psychologically stunted.

### 4. Sophie Strand: The Rot of the "Hero" Myth

- The Diagnosis: The "Hero's Journey" is a trap.

    Writing more recently, Strand offers a radical critique of the very models Bly and Campbell popularized. She argues that the "Hero’s Journey" (the lone man conquering obstacles, slaying the beast, and returning victorious) is an isolate, sterile model of masculinity that leads to burnout and ecological collapse.

- **The Result:** Men feel crushed by the pressure to be impenetrable, singular "individuals." They are cut off from the web of life. Strand uses the metaphor of **mycelium** (fungi). The "Hero" is a single mushroom; the true masculine is the vast, underground network that connects things.
- **The Missing Piece:** **Porosity.** Strand suggests men don't need to be "Iron" (rigid/hard); they need to be "compost." She looks to archetypes like Dionysus—a god who is fluid, torn apart, and intimately connected to fermentation, rot, and rebirth. The modern man is lost because he thinks he has to stand *above* nature, when his salvation lies in rotting *into* it—becoming an ecological participant rather than a conqueror.

### Summary of the Evolution

- **Bly (1990):** Men are lost because they are **separated from their Fathers** and the "Wild" energy. (Solution: Reconnect with older men/mentors).
- **Fox (2000s):** Men are lost because they are **separated from the Sacred/Earth**. (Solution: Mysticism and creativity).
- **Plotkin (2000s-2010s):** Men are lost because they are **stuck in the Ego**. (Solution: Vision quest and ego-death in nature).
- **Strand (2020s):** Men are lost because they are **trying to be Heroes**. (Solution: Drop the armor, become porous, and rejoin the ecological web).

# Other Thinkers

The thinkers you mentioned (Bly, Fox, Plotkin, Strand) form a specific lineage: they are all trying to solve the "Problem of the Empty Modern Man" by looking *outside* of standard psychology—turning instead to myth, nature, and mysticism.

Here are the other essential thinkers who fit this specific "Venn diagram."

### 1. The "Structural" Analysts (The Architects)

**Robert Moore & Douglas Gillette**

- **The Book:** *King, Warrior, Magician, Lover1*
- **The Assessment:** While Bly provided the poetry of the men's movement, Moore and Gillette provided the **blueprint**. As Jungian analysts, they argued that the "Boy Psychology" of modern men isn't just a vague feeling; it is a structural failure to access four specific "hard-wired" archetypes.2
- **The Difference:** They are more clinical than Bly or Fox. They argue that most men are possessed by the "Shadow" versions of these energies (e.g., the "Tyrant" instead of the King, or the "Violent Soldier" instead of the Warrior). Their work is the standard "operating system" manual for men's work today.

### 2. The "Indigenous" Initiator

**Malidoma Patrice Somé**

- **The Book:** *Of Water and the Spirit3*
- **The Assessment:** Somé was a Dagara elder from West Africa who held PhDs from the Sorbonne.4 He bridged the gap between African indigenous wisdom and the Western men's movement. His assessment was blunt: **"There is no word for depression in my village; we call it 'unacknowledged gifts'."5**
- **The Difference:** He didn't just talk about initiation metaphorically (like Bly); he described *literal* life-or-death rituals.6 He argued that Western men are dangerous because they have never had the "child" burned out of them by the community. They are "un-initiated elders" running the world with the emotional maturity of teenagers.

### 3. The "Grief" Specialist

**Francis Weller**

- **The Book:** *The Wild Edge of Sorrow7*
- **The Assessment:** Weller is a direct spiritual successor to Bly and Plotkin. His central thesis is that **grief is the doorway to vitality**. He argues that modern men are "sedated" because they have suppressed their grief over the loss of community, the destruction of the planet, and their own unlived lives.
- **The Difference:** While Plotkin focuses on the "Vision Quest" (going out), Weller focuses on the "Descent" (going down). He argues that men cannot be "upwardly mobile" forever; they must learn the "skills of the descent" into sorrow to become real human beings.

### 4. The "Post-Activist" Mystic

**Bayo Akomolafe**

- **The Concept:** *The Indeterminacy of Manhood*
- **The Assessment:** Akomolafe is a contemporary Nigerian philosopher who (like Sophie Strand) questions the very foundations of the "Man" as a fixed identity.8 He argues that "Manhood" is a colonial construct designed for productivity and war.
- **The Difference:** He offers a "trickster" perspective. Instead of trying to "heal" masculinity (which implies restoring it to a previous "whole" state), he suggests we might need to "compost" it entirely. His famous phrase, **"The times are urgent; let us slow down,"** speaks to the exhausted modern male who thinks the solution is *more* action.

### 5. The "Christian" Elder

**Richard Rohr**

- **The Book:** *Adam’s Return* or *Falling Upward*
- **The Assessment:** A Franciscan friar who became a central father figure for the men's movement. Rohr argues that the modern male script fails because it only teaches "First Half of Life" skills (building the container, making money, ego success). It fails to teach "Second Half of Life" skills (surrendering, failing, falling into God).
- **The Difference:** He translates the "Wild Man" concepts into Christian mysticism. He argues that men need "necessary suffering" to break the ego, otherwise they become "elderly toddlers."
