# Global Workspace Theory (GWT)

**Global Workspace Theory (GWT)**, developed by Bernard Baars and later computationally elaborated by Stanislas Dehaene and others, is a cognitive architecture model. The basic picture: the brain runs many specialized, parallel, unconscious processes (visual processing, memory retrieval, motor planning, etc.). Consciousness, on this view, is what happens when information from one of these processes gets "broadcast" into a shared workspace — a kind of limited-capacity stage — making it available to many other cognitive systems at once (attention, memory, language, decision-making, planning). What's "in the workspace" is what you're conscious of; everything else is unconscious processing happening in parallel, unbroadcast.

The appeal of GWT, especially for cognitive science and AI, is that it's functionally tractable. It doesn't ask "what is experience," it asks "what does information have to do — how does it have to be routed and made globally available — for a system to report on it, reason about it, and act on it flexibly." That's a question you can build architectures around, and it's exactly why GWT has become a leading contender in the AI-consciousness debates — Dehaene, and more recently a number of the "indicator properties" papers on AI consciousness, treat global broadcast/workspace architecture as a candidate marker.

**Ned Block's A/P distinction** is the crucial fork here. Block (1995, "On a confusion about a function of consciousness") argued that "consciousness" is doing two different jobs that get run together:

- **Access-consciousness (A-consciousness):** information is A-conscious if it's poised for use — available to reasoning, reporting, and the control of action. It's a functional/relational property. A-consciousness is about *availability for downstream cognitive use*.
- **Phenomenal-consciousness (P-consciousness):** the qualitative, felt character of experience — what it's like to see red, feel pain, taste coffee. This is Nagel's "what it is like to be," Chalmers' explanandum of the hard problem. It's not defined functionally at all; it's defined by the presence of qualia.

Block's whole point was that these can, in principle, come apart. You can imagine (he argued, using cases like blindsight and certain neuropsychological syndromes) information that's globally accessible and driving behavior without any accompanying phenomenal feel, and conversely you can imagine phenomenal experience that's *not* globally broadcast — feels like something but isn't reportable or integrated (some readings of peripheral or "overflow" perception, à la Ned Block's later work with Lamme on iconic memory).

**Where GWT lands relative to this distinction** is the crux of the issue for your project: GWT is, almost by construction, a theory of *A-consciousness*. Global broadcast just *is* a story about access — about information becoming available to memory, report, planning, and control. Baars and Dehaene do talk about GWT as an account of "consciousness" full stop, but critics (Block chief among them, also Chalmers) have pointed out that this either:

1. Quietly identifies P-consciousness with A-consciousness by fiat — assuming that once information is globally available, phenomenality just comes along for free, without argument; or
2. Simply doesn't address P-consciousness at all, leaving the hard problem exactly where it was, dressed in the language of "broadcasting" and "workspace."

This is precisely the move that shows up in a lot of contemporary AI-consciousness talk: a system exhibits global-workspace-like information integration and broadcast, and this gets glossed as evidence of "consciousness," when what's actually been shown is A-consciousness-style functional integration — nothing about whether there's anything it is like to be that system. Dehaene himself has been fairly careful about this in places, describing GWT as addressing consciousness in the "access" sense and leaving phenomenality to the side or treating it as an illusion/epiphenomenon — which is itself a substantive (materialist, often eliminativist) position, not a neutral finding.

For an idealist framing — where consciousness is ontologically primary rather than an emergent byproduct of information integration — GWT's success as a theory of access is basically orthogonal to (and can't settle) the question of phenomenality. A system could implement a perfect global workspace and, on an idealist or panpsychist view, still be an open question — or on a strict analytic idealist view (Kastrup et al.), the "workspace" itself would need to be understood as a pattern of *dissociated* mental activity within a more fundamental field of experience, not as the *generator* of experience from non-experiential parts. That's a very different explanatory direction than GWT/Dehaene assume, and it's worth being precise in the book about exactly where GWT's explanatory ambition stops — it's a theory of what gets broadcast and used, not a theory of why or whether broadcasting feels like anything.
