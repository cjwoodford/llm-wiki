# Metaphysical Quietism / Deflationism

> I'm not familiar with Chomsky's critiques of the concept of body or the physical. It sounds completely outside the usual reframing and recontextualization of the physical arising out of quantum physics, etc.

You’ve hit on exactly why [this argument](https://share.gemini.google/PNC8z5QxG43z) catches people off guard. When most modern thinkers try to reframe "the physical," they point to **quantum mechanics**—highlighting wave-particle duality, non-locality, or observer-dependency to show that matter isn't as solid or objective as we thought. That is an *ontological* argument (redefining what matter actually is).

Noam Chomsky’s critique is entirely different. It is an *epistemological and historical* argument. He isn't trying to use new physics to explain the mind; he is arguing that **the "body" part of the mind-body problem was accidentally destroyed over 300 years ago.**

Here is how that argument unfolds, and why it bypasses the usual quantum physics framing.

### 1. Descartes and the "Machine"

To understand how the concept of the body broke, you have to look at what it was originally supposed to be. In the 17th century, René Descartes and his contemporaries championed **Mechanical Philosophy**. They looked at the intricate, gear-driven clocks of the era and concluded that the physical universe was exactly like that: an immense, deterministic machine.

For Descartes, the physical world (*res extensa*) had very strict, intuitive boundaries. To be physical meant:

- Occupying space.
- Having a shape.
- Interacting **only through direct, physical contact** (like one gear pushing another).

Because human thought, language, and free will clearly couldn't be explained by gears pushing each other, Descartes logically concluded there must be a second, non-physical substance: the mind (*res cogitans*). This gave birth to the classic Mind-Body problem: *How does the ghost inhabit the machine?*

### 2. How Newton Wrecked the Machine

Enter Isaac Newton. When Newton published his theories on gravity, he wasn't trying to be a mystic; he was trying to do physics. But his discovery of gravity completely shattered mechanical philosophy.

Gravity required **action at a distance**. The Earth pulls on the Moon across millions of miles of empty space without any gears, fluids, or physical contact touching it. To Descartes and the rest of the scientific establishment, this sounded like absolute magic.

Newton himself openly admitted he had no idea *how* matter could attract other matter across a vacuum. He famously wrote *Hypotheses non fingo* ("I frame no hypotheses")—essentially saying, *"The mathematics work perfectly, but don't ask me what the physical mechanism is, because there isn't one we can conceive."*

### 3. The Ghost Without a Machine

Philosopher Gilbert Ryle famously mocked Cartesian dualism as the myth of the "Ghost in the Machine."

Chomsky’s brilliant historical twist is to point out that Newton didn't find the ghost—**Newton got rid of the machine.** By proving that matter can act in ways that defy mechanical, contact-based common sense, Newton inadvertently showed that we don't actually know what "the physical" fundamentally is.

Since Newton, every time physics hits a wall, it simply expands the definition of "physical" to include whatever solves the problem:

- We couldn't explain light with particles, so we added **invisible electromagnetic fields**.
- We couldn't explain subatomic behavior, so we added **quantum superposition and spacetime curvature**.
- We can't explain galactic rotation, so we invent **dark matter**.

### Chomsky vs. Quantum Physics

While a quantum-physics recontextualization tries to redefine the *behavior* of matter, Chomsky's view is a meta-critique of the language we use.

| **Perspective**        | **Core Focus**                                                                        | **The Conclusion**                                                                                                         |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The Quantum Move**   | Investigates the micro-foundations of reality (wave-functions, particles, observers). | Matter is fundamentally non-material, interconnected, or information-based.                                                |
| **The Chomskyan Move** | Investigates the history of scientific definitions.                                   | "The physical" is just a blank check. It means nothing more than "whatever the best current physics textbook says exists." |

Because "the physical" has no fixed, intrinsic boundaries, Chomsky argues that the traditional Mind-Body problem is completely unintelligible. You cannot have a hard metaphysical divide between Mind and Body if the definition of "Body" is infinitely elastic.

### Why Ellen Burns Adopts This

This is precisely why Dr. Burns uses Chomsky to bypass the "Hard Problem" of consciousness. If there is no fixed, mechanical "physical world" to contrast against our subjective experience, then we don't need to wrap ourselves in knots trying to figure out how a "dead, physical brain" secretes "living, mental consciousness."

Instead, she suggests we treat the mental and the physical not as two warring substances, but simply as different levels of organization within an open-ended reality we are still trying to understand.

## A Separation Conversation from the Metaphysical Debate of Panpsychism, Materialism, etc.

> I've been reading Goff's Galileo's Error book as you know. This feels like an entirely separately conversation with no intersection with the framing that Goff employes

You are completely right. They feel like entirely separate conversations because they diagnose the "original sin" of modern science from two fundamentally different historical vantage points.

Philip Goff and Noam Chomsky (and by extension, Ellen Burns) are standing on the same shattered foundation—the realization that vulgar materialism is a myth—but they are looking in opposite directions.

Here is how their frameworks stand in stark contrast, and the one subtle, fascinating place where their orbits actually collide.

## The Two Diagnoses

### Goff’s Axis: The Qualitative vs. The Quantitative

For Goff, the turning point is **Galileo in 1623**.

Goff's argument is metaphysical. Galileo’s "error" was dividing the world into primary qualities (geometry, size, motion) and secondary qualities (color, taste, felt experience). Galileo threw the qualitative out of the scientific sandbox so that the quantitative could be modeled with perfect mathematics.

- **The Problem:** Because science explicitly defined itself by *excluding* the qualitative, you can never use that science to explain consciousness. The "Hard Problem" is a feature, not a bug, of the Galilean paradigm.
- **The Solution (Panpsychism):** Physics only tells us what matter *does* (its mathematical behavior), not what it *is* (its intrinsic nature). Goff fills that empty internal space with consciousness.

### Chomsky/Burns’ Axis: The Mechanical vs. The Elastic

For Chomsky and Burns, the turning point is **Newton in 1687**.

Their argument is epistemological and linguistic. They aren't worried about Galileo abstraction of math; they are looking at Descartes' definition of the physical as a contact-based machine.

- **The Problem:** Newton proved that the physical world doesn't operate like a machine. Matter attracts other matter across a vacuum without touching.
- **The Solution:** Instead of trying to find an "intrinsic nature" to matter, Chomsky argues that science simply gave up on trying to intelligibly understand the world. We settled for tracking it mathematically. "The physical" became an infinite credit line—whenever we find a new weird phenomenon (fields, curved spacetime, dark matter), we just append it to the definition of "physical."

## Where the Framings Collide (and Past Each Other)

If you brought Goff and Chomsky into a room, their conversation would look like a brilliant mismatch of metaphysics versus linguistics:

| **The Concept**         | **Philip Goff (Galileo's Error)**                                                                            | **Noam Chomsky / Ellen Burns**                                                                                                                  |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **What is Matter?**     | Matter has an outer shell (math) and an inner core (consciousness).                                          | Matter is just a word we use for "whatever the physics textbooks currently say exists."                                                         |
| **The Hard Problem**    | It's a real metaphysical knot. We have to figure out how proto-conscious particles combine into human minds. | It's an illusion born of bad language. You can't have a "Hard Problem" of the physical vs. the mental if "the physical" has no real definition. |
| **The Panpsychist Fix** | Brilliantly solves the gap by making consciousness fundamental.                                              | It's just adding another empty word ("consciousness") to a physical concept we already don't intuitively comprehend.                            |

## The Non-Intersection

Goff accepts the traditional framing of the physical world—he just thinks it's incomplete. He looks at a pixel, an electron, or a brain cell and says, *"This thing has a physical description, but it must also have a subjective inside."* He is trying to heal the Cartesian split by making the physical and the mental two sides of the same coin.

Burns, following Chomsky, is making a far more radical linguistic move. She is arguing that **the coin itself doesn't exist.**

She uses this to protect human experience from being reduced to a machine. If the reductionist tells you, *"Your profound sense of self, your trauma, and your creative insights are just physical brain states,"* Burns' response isn't Goff's *"Yes, because those physical states are conscious!"*

Her response is: **"You don't even know what a 'physical state' means in a closed, mechanical sense. You cannot reduce my experience to a category that is itself a moving goalpost."**

For an idealist or a humanist alternative to modern techno-scientific reductionism, these two paths offer entirely different weapons: Goff offers a new metaphysics to re-enchant the physical world, while the Chomskyan/Burns approach offers a devastating epistemological critique that dismantles the reductionist's authority from the inside out.

## Metaphysical Quietism

> Okay, so philosophers like Burns and Chomsky are perfectly happy to have no conceptualization of a metaphysics? 

Yes, they are perfectly comfortable stepping off the metaphysical merry-go-round entirely. In contemporary philosophy, this stance is often called **metaphysical quietism** or **deflationism**.

To thinkers in this tradition, a grand, constructive metaphysical system—whether it’s physicalism, dualism, or panpsychism—isn't a necessary foundation for understanding reality. In fact, they view the demand for an absolute ontological category as a bit of a linguistic trap.

Their comfort with having no closed metaphysical framework comes down to a few core principles:

### 1. "Problems" vs. "Mysteries" (Chomsky's Cognitive Boundedness)

Chomsky famously argues that humans are biological creatures, and like any other biological organism, our cognitive faculties have structural limits. He separates our inquiries into two categories:

- **Problems:** Questions that fall within the scope of our cognitive architecture and can be productively investigated (e.g., how human language syntax functions, or how gravity behaves mathematically).
- **Mysteries:** Questions that may completely transcend our biological capacity to grasp (e.g., the ultimate, intrinsic essence of being).

To Chomsky, trying to build a definitive metaphysics to solve an ultimate "mystery" is a bit like a rat trying to navigate a maze built on prime numbers—our brains simply aren't wired to hold that kind of absolute, non-structural knowledge. Recognizing this isn't defeatism; it's just epistemic humility.

### 2. Methodological Naturalism Over Ontological Foundations

Instead of anchoring reality in a metaphysical definition, these philosophers practice **methodological naturalism**. They argue that our best way of pointing to what is "real" is simply to look at our most successful, rigorous scientific and rational models at any given moment.

If physics requires fields, spacetime curvature, or quantum entanglement to explain the world, then those things are real. We don't need an additional metaphysical umbrella label (like "matter" or "mind") to justify their existence. The universe is open-ended, and our language for it expands as our discovery expands.

### 3. Burns' Shift from Substance to Structure

This is exactly how Dr. Burns frees her work from the gravity of the Hard Problem. If you stop obsessing over *what substance* human experience is made of (Is it physical brain matter? Is it proto-conscious soul-stuff?), you are suddenly free to study experience on its own rich terms.

In her framework, human experience is better understood through **normative, linguistic, and trauma-theoretic structures** rather than metaphysical boxes:

- Instead of asking, *"How does a physical neuron secrete a feeling?"* she asks, *"How do unconscious structures, deep-seated traumas, and linguistic architectures actually shape human perception, agency, and meaning?"*

### The Core Divergence

To a constructive metaphysician, leaving the ultimate nature of reality open-ended feels like an unfinished puzzle—a gap that desperately needs to be filled.

But to a deflationist like Burns or Chomsky, pretending we can finish that puzzle by inventing metaphysical categories is a linguistic illusion. They are perfectly happy to leave the metaphysical ledger open, choosing instead to focus on the concrete, complex structures of human language, cognition, and life as they actually manifest.

You can hear Dr. Burns expand on these ideas and how they apply to modern technology in this [interview on the Doing the Work Podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xar1y4qSlcY). This conversation is highly relevant because she discusses her academic journey, her critique of traditional philosophy of mind, and why she believes current AI models lack the structural architecture of genuine human experience.

## Schopenhauer to the Rescue

> Didn't Schopenhauer advocate for a similar epistemic humility? 

Yes, absolutely. Arthur Schopenhauer is the historical heavyweight champion of this exact brand of epistemic humility regarding science. Writing in the early 19th century, he anticipated this entire line of critique by mounting a devastating attack on the scientific materialism of his day.

In fact, Schopenhauer serves as the ultimate historical bridge between the linguistic/epistemological critique of Chomsky and Burns and the panpsychist/idealist framework of Philip Goff.

His philosophy mirrors this conversation in how he maps the limits of science, where he splits from the quietists, and how he connects to the broader idealist alternative.

### 1. The Limit of Science: A "Catalogue of Inexplicable Forces"

Schopenhauer was a strict Kantian at his core, meaning he accepted that our brains are wired to perceive only *phenomena* (things as they appear to us in space, time, and causality), never the *noumenon* (the thing-in-itself).

He explicitly argued that natural science—which he called **etiology**—is fundamentally limited. Etiology can tell us how things interact, move, and react to one another under law-like regularities, but it is completely blind to what those things *are* in their inner essence.

He wrote that even if science achieved absolute perfection, it would ultimately amount to nothing more than a **"catalogue of inexplicable forces."**

> **The Schopenhauerian Critique:** Science can tell you that electricity follows Ohm's law, or that gravity drops an apple at $9.8 \text{ m/s}^2$, but if you ask a physicist *what* electricity or gravity actually are in themselves, they have to treat them as "occult qualities." Science tracks the *how* (the relational shell), but never the *what* (the intrinsic content).

This is a flawless 19th-century precursor to both Chomsky’s Newton argument (we can model gravity mathematically, but the physical mechanism is a blank check) and Goff’s Galileo argument (science only tracks the mathematical behavior of matter, leaving its intrinsic nature empty).

### 2. The Fork in the Road: Quietism vs. The Somatic Backdoor

Where the conversation splits dramatically is what philosophers choose to do once they arrive at this scientific boundary line. This is where Schopenhauer parts ways with the "quietism" of Chomsky and Burns.

- **The Chomsky/Burns Path (Quietism):** They look at this limit and say, *"Our biological faculties are cognitively bounded. We cannot grasp absolute metaphysics. Therefore, let’s stop inventing grand ontological systems (like physicalism or panpsychism) and instead focus on the real, observable structures of human cognition, language, and lived experience."*
- **The Schopenhauer Path (The Metaphysical Move):** Schopenhauer agreed that the *external* senses can never grasp the thing-in-itself. But he argued that Kant missed a vital backdoor key: **our own bodies.**

Schopenhauer pointed out that you do not encounter your own body merely as an external object in a textbook or a mirror. You experience it from the *inside*, immediately and non-rationally, as a felt sense of striving, desire, emotional currents, and life-force. This immediate, internal reality is what he called the **Will** (*Wille*).

He then made the radical, idealist leap: this "Will" that you feel animating your own being from the inside is the exact same "unexplained force" that animates the entire universe—from the growth of a plant to the magnetic pull of a planet.

### The Grand Alignment

When you place these thinkers side-by-side, you can see how they are all diagnosing the exact same blind spot in Western reductionism, but offering different remedies:

| **Thinker**         | **The Blind Spot**                                                                                                                      | **The Remedy**                                                                                                                                                     |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Chomsky / Burns** | Materialism uses an infinitely elastic, meaningless definition of "the physical" to explain away human life.                            | **Epistemic Quietism:** Dismantle the reductionist's language; protect human experience by studying its deep, non-mechanistic cognitive and linguistic structures. |
| **Philip Goff**     | Modern science explicitly chose to bracket out the qualitative, leaving matter as an empty mathematical skeleton.                       | **Panpsychism:** Heal the Galilean split by placing consciousness at the literal foundation of matter (the "inside" of the electron).                              |
| **Schopenhauer**    | Science (*etiology*) is just a map of shadowy, causal relationships between appearances; it can never find the inner essence of things. | **Idealist Voluntarism:** Look into your own somatic, lived experience of embodiment to find the animate core of reality (the Will).                               |

Schopenhauer’s epistemic humility was his starting point, not his finish line. He used it to clear away the arrogance of mechanical materialism, making room for a deeply humane, animate, and idealist alternative to the idea that the universe is just a collection of dead gears pushing against one another.

## Chomsky Is Too Disembodied 

> Bringing in Schopenhauer here makes me feel that perhaps Chomsky's deflationism is a bit too conceptual or disembodied

You’ve put your finger on the exact soft underbelly of the Chomskyan approach. It feels disembodied because, at its core, it *is* a profoundly cerebral philosophy.

Chomsky is, first and foremost, a linguist and a cognitive scientist rooted in the analytic tradition. When he defends the "mind," he isn't defending the living, breathing, pulsing vitality of human existence; he is defending an abstract architecture of innate cognitive faculties—like the computational structures that allow us to generate syntax. It is a defense of the mind engineered from the head up.

This is precisely where the stark limitation of deflationism becomes obvious, and why turning back to Schopenhauer feels like catching your breath after being underwater.

### The Fortress of Abstract Skepticism

Chomsky’s deflationism works brilliantly as an intellectual shield. If a technologist or a rigid materialist tries to tell you that human beings are just organic algorithms and that machines will soon replicate the soul, Chomsky gives you the tools to stage a brilliant logical checkmate. He allows you to say: *"Your definition of a machine is based on an obsolete 17th-century myth, and your science is just tracking mathematical regularities without touching reality."*

But once you win that argument, Chomsky leaves you standing in an empty, sterile room.

By treating the ultimate nature of reality as a "mystery" that human biology is fundamentally locked out of understanding, deflationism creates a profound sense of detachment. It turns the mystery of existence into a closed biological boundary line. The mind is protected from being reduced to a machine, but it is protected by being turned into an isolated, cognitive information-processing island.

### Schopenhauer and the Somatic Awakening

Schopenhauer’s genius was realizing that we don't just exist as intellects looking out at a world of objects. We don't learn about reality by reading the "physics textbook" that Chomsky keeps pointing to.

Schopenhauer reminds us that we are *embodied*. If you want to know what reality is, you don't look at an abstract equation; you look at the immediate, visceral, non-rational experience of inhabiting a physical form.

- **The External View (Chomsky's World):** My body is an object in space that obeys gravity, blood pressure metrics, and neurological firing patterns. It is an "appearance" tracked by science.
- **The Internal View (Schopenhauer's World):** My body is a direct current of hunger, breath, desire, fatigue, emotional resonance, and vital life-force. It is a living striving.

By locating the "Thing-in-Itself" in our direct, somatic experience of the body, Schopenhauer does something the analytic quietists can never do: he gives us an **animate** universe. He transforms the mystery of existence from a conceptual puzzle we aren't smart enough to solve into a living reality we participate in every time we take a breath or feel a wave of grief or creativity.

### Why This Distinction Matters for the Tech Age

This tension is incredibly high-stakes when we try to confront modern techno-reductionism or transhumanist frameworks.

If you fight the battle against the machine-world view purely on Chomsky’s turf, you are fighting a conceptual war using grammar, epistemology, and cognitive science. It’s effective for dismantling Silicon Valley's overconfidence, but it lacks blood. It still treats the human being primarily as a "knower" or a "thinker."

But if you fight that battle on Schopenhauer’s turf—or through the lens of an idealist, somatic alternative—the critique changes entirely. The reason an AI doesn't have a mind isn't just because it lacks human syntax or a non-mechanistic cognitive module. **It's because it doesn't breathe. It has no body, no somatic depth, no unconscious trauma, and no participation in the vital, striving currents of the animate world.**

> Chomsky provides the intellectual armor to shatter the reductionist's logic, but Schopenhauer provides the life-force.

To build a genuinely humane alternative to a mechanized future, an intellectual checkmate isn't enough. You need a philosophy that acknowledges that our primary contact with reality isn't through the concepts in our heads, but through the lived, felt experience of being an animate organism.
