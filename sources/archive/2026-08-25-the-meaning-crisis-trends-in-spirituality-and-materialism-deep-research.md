# The Meaning Crisis + Trends In Spirituality and Materialism (Deep Research)

# The Architecture of Meaning in a Secular Age: From the Crisis of Sense-Making to the Rise of Rational Spirituality

## Part I: The Anatomy of the Meaning Crisis

### 1.1. The Collapse of the Sacred Canopies

In the early decades of the 21st century, Western civilization finds itself navigating a profound psychological and sociological dislocation, a phenomenon increasingly categorized by cognitive scientists, philosophers, and cultural theorists as the "Meaning Crisis." This crisis is not merely a byproduct of economic instability or political polarization, though it exacerbates both; rather, it is a fundamental degradation of the "machinery of meaning making" itself.1 As the sociologist Peter Berger famously theorized, human societies erect "sacred canopies"—overarching frameworks of interpretation—to shield the individual from the terror of chaos. **The contemporary meaning crisis represents the shredding of this canopy, leaving the modern subject exposed to a cognitive environment where information is abundant, but wisdom is scarce**.2 #meaning 

The crisis is characterized by the simultaneous disintegration of two foundational ordering principles that have historically sustained human psychological health: the *nomological order* and the *narrative order*.3 The nomological order refers to the sense that the cosmos is governed by intelligible laws (logos) and reason, providing a stable, predictable arena for human action. The narrative order, or *mythos*, provides the overarching stories—whether religious, nationalistic, or cultural—that allow individuals to situate their personal struggles within a larger, purposeful drama. When these orders collapse, the result is "domicide," a term repurposed by cognitive scientist John Vervaeke to describe the destruction of the psychological and cultural "home".3

Historically, domicide was a physical act—the razing of cities by conquerors like Alexander the Great—which necessitated the invention of therapeutic philosophies like Stoicism and Epicureanism to help the displaced cope with their loss of belonging.4 Today, domicide is internal. The cumulative effects of the Protestant Reformation, the Industrial Revolution, and the Scientific Revolution have eroded the "worldview attunement" that once connected the self to the cosmos.2 The modern individual is no longer a participant in a cosmic drama but an observer of a mechanical universe that appears indifferent to human flourishing. This estrangement creates a "sapiential obsolescence," where the traditional institutions of wisdom—religion, ritual, and elderhood—are no longer viewed as legitimate sources of guidance.2

### 1.2. The Zombie and the Crisis of Agency

A potent symbol of this crisis in the cultural imagination is the zombie. Unlike the monsters of antiquity, which often represented external threats or moral failures, the zombie represents the "creature most unfit to reverse the status of apocalypse".5 The zombie is pure consumption without consciousness, a body without a soul, moving relentlessly but without narrative or purpose. Vervaeke argues that the zombie serves as a grim prognosis for a culture where the "agency-arena" relationship has broken down.5

In a functioning meaning system, there is a harmonious fit between the agent (the person) and the arena (the world). The agent knows how to act, and the arena makes sense of those actions. In the meaning crisis, this relationship is severed. The world becomes an "absurd" place where actions feel futile, and the agent retreats into the "zombie" state of mindless consumption or the "automaton" state of algorithmic predictability. This loss of agency is compounded by the "perennial problems" of the human condition—self-deception, internal conflict, and the temptation of nihilism—which traditional "psychotechnologies" (prayer, meditation, confession) were designed to ameliorate.1 Without these technologies, the modern subject is left vulnerable to "parasitic processing," where the mind's own adaptive heuristics are turned against it, leading to anxiety, depression, and a sense of "existential vacuum".2

### 1.3. The Meta-Crisis: When Meaninglessness Becomes Existential

The Meaning Crisis does not exist in isolation; it is the psychological substrate of the "Meta-Crisis" (or polycrisis), a term used to describe the interlocking ecological, economic, and technological threats facing global civilization.6 The inability of humanity to coordinate effective responses to climate change, nuclear proliferation, or the alignment of artificial intelligence is rooted in a failure of collective sense-making.7 #crisis 

The historical record offers a chilling warning regarding the relationship between meaning crises and meta-crises. The totalitarian movements of the 20th century—Nazism and Stalinism—can be understood as "pseudo-religious" attempts to solve the meaning crisis of the Weimar era and early industrialization.9 These ideologies offered a restoration of the narrative order (the Thousand-Year Reich, the Worker’s Paradise) and a clear, albeit horrific, nomological structure. They provided a "tsunami of bullshit" that swept up the "lost" individuals of a collapsing society into a unified, destructive purpose.9

Today, the risk persists that in the absence of a "religion of no religion"—a secular, rational integration of wisdom practices—new, destructive pseudo-religions may emerge to fill the void. The rise of conspiracy theories (QAnon), polarized political identities, and radical fundamentalisms are symptomatic of a desperate grasping for certainty in an uncertain world.9 The "Meta-Crisis" thus demands not just technological solutions, but a "comprehensive change in our consciousness, cognition, character, and culture".9 #conspiracies 

## Part II: The Demographic Landscape of Disaffiliation

### 2.1. The Great Decoupling: From "Nones" to "Neithers"

For the past two decades, the dominant narrative in the sociology of religion has been the "rise of the Nones"—those claiming no religious affiliation. However, detailed analysis of data from 2024 and 2025 reveals a significant bifurcation within this group. While the decline of Christianity continues—with Protestants dropping to 38% and Catholics to 26% of the U.S. population in 2024—the "Nones" are not a monolith.10

The category of "Spiritual But Not Religious" (SBNR), long considered the primary landing pad for disaffected Christians, appears to be plateauing. The most dynamic and rapidly growing cohort is the "Neither Religious Nor Spiritual" (NRNS). #spiritual #nones #NRNS

This data suggests that the "secular transition" is entering a new, more radical phase. The "SBNR" identity, which often served as a transitional "soft secularism," is no longer capturing the youngest cohorts. Instead, a "hard secularism" or "indifference" is taking hold. Among adults aged 18-29, nearly half report no religious affiliation, and a significant portion of these reject the "spiritual" label entirely, viewing it as synonymous with superstition or anti-scientific "woo".12

### 2.2. The Politics of Meaninglessness

The "Neither" phenomenon is heavily skewed by political affiliation, creating a "meaning gap" that aligns with the partisan divide. Democrats have seen a precipitous 23-point drop in religious identification since 1999, with the "Neither" category tripling within the party.11 Conversely, Republicans have remained relatively stable in their religious self-identification.11 #politics 

This polarization has profound implications for the Meaning Crisis. Vervaeke notes that in the absence of cosmic participation, "perspectival knowing has been reduced to your political viewpoint".9 Political identity becomes the primary container for meaning, turning policy debates into existential battles between good and evil. This "confusing of mythology for politics" is a hallmark of the meta-crisis, preventing pragmatic problem-solving.9

Furthermore, the "Neither" demographic is characterized by civic withdrawal. Unlike religious communities or even enthusiastic SBNR networks, the "Neither Religious Nor Spiritual" group exhibits very low rates of joining groups, praying, or engaging in communal rituals.16 This "atomization" exacerbates the crisis of loneliness and social fragmentation, as this growing segment of the population lacks the "thick" social ties that religious institutions traditionally provided. #sangha 

### 2.3. The Gender Gap in Secularism

The data also reveals a gendered dimension to the meaning crisis. The "Neither Religious Nor Spiritual" group is majority male (55.5%), whereas the "Spiritual But Not Religious" group is heavily female (62.4%).17 This divergence suggests that men and women may be navigating the collapse of the religious order differently. Men are more likely to drift toward a materialist/indifferent stance (the "Neithers"), while women are more likely to retain a connection to the transcendent through non-institutional spirituality (SBNR). This creates a "spiritual disconnect" in the dating market and family life, further destabilizing the "domicile" or home unit, contributing to the broader sense of domicide. #masculinity #feminity 

## Part III: The Rise of "Spiritual But Not Ridiculous"

### 3.1. Defining SBNR 2.0: The Quest for Rationality

In the chasm between the "Neither/Nor" materialists and the often-caricatured "New Age" spiritualists lies a distinct, intellectual movement: the "Spiritual But Not Ridiculous" (SBNR 2.0). The phrase, popularized by the podcast of the same name (now titled *Cosmic Intelligence*), captures the ethos of a demographic that seeks the *benefits* of spiritual practice—insight, emotional regulation, ethical grounding—without the "ridiculous" baggage of dogma, superstition, or anti-scientific claims.18

The "Spiritual But Not Ridiculous" movement is explicitly post-secular. It acknowledges the validity of the scientific worldview but refuses to accept the "disenchantment" of the world that often accompanies it. It seeks to explore "technologies of the sacred" and "consciousness" while maintaining "discernment" and grounding in philosophy.19 It positions itself against two opponents:

1. **Fundamentalist Religion:** Which demands belief without evidence.
2. **Reductive Materialism:** Which denies the reality of subjective experience and meaning.

### 3.2. The Role of Podcasts and Digital Sanghas

The movement is largely decentralized, organizing itself through digital platforms, podcasts, and apps rather than brick-and-mortar institutions. The *Cosmic Intelligence* podcast (formerly *Spiritual But Not Ridiculous*), hosted by Chad Woodford, exemplifies this trend. It features discussions on "Vedic astrology" and "plant medicine" not as supernatural magic, but as tools for exploring archetypes and consciousness, interpreted through the lens of Western and Eastern philosophy.19

Guests like philosopher Matt Segall help articulate a "worldview attunement" that moves from materialism to idealism, arguing that consciousness, rather than matter, may be the fundamental substrate of reality.18 This intellectual rigor separates the SBNR 2.0 movement from the vague "love and light" spirituality of the 1990s. It is a spirituality for the "information age," deeply concerned with the implications of Artificial Intelligence and the "soul sickness" of Silicon Valley.7

## Part IV: Methodologies of Rational Spirituality

To address the Meaning Crisis without reverting to pre-modern mythology, several "operating systems" for rational spirituality have gained prominence. These movements share a commitment to what Vervaeke calls an "ecology of practices"—a set of techniques to cultivate wisdom and insight.21

### 4.1. Secular Buddhism (Buddhism 2.0)

Secular Buddhism, championed by figures like Stephen Batchelor, represents a radical reinterpretation of the Dharma for a secular age. It posits that Buddhism's effectiveness does not require belief in reincarnation, karma (in the cosmic sense), or nirvana as an afterlife.22 #buddhism 

**The Core Shift: From Truths to Tasks**

Batchelor reframes the Four Noble Truths not as metaphysical propositions to be believed, but as pragmatic tasks to be performed. He utilizes the acronym ELSA:

- **E**mbrace life (dukkha/suffering).
- **L**et go of reactivity (craving).
- **S**ee the cessation of reactivity.
- **A**ct (cultivate the path).23

This approach, sometimes termed "Buddhism 2.0," views the Dharma as a "post-metaphysical vision" focused on human flourishing in *this* life.24 It operates on a "logic of care" rather than a "logic of transcendence".25 In traditional Buddhism, the goal is to escape the cycle of rebirth (Samsara). In Secular Buddhism, the goal is to care for the suffering of this world.

**Critiques and Tensions**

Traditionalists argue that this "decontextualization" risks turning Buddhism into "McMindfulness"—a mere stress-reduction tool for corporate efficiency that neglects the deep ethical and soteriological (salvation-oriented) goals of the tradition.22 Critics like Bhikkhu Bodhi have argued that without the "long view" of rebirth, the ethical imperative of Buddhism is weakened. However, for the "Spiritual But Not Ridiculous" demographic, the stripping away of metaphysics is precisely what makes the practice accessible.27 It allows them to engage in deep contemplative practice without experiencing "cognitive dissonance" with their scientific worldview.

### 4.2. Modern Stoicism

Parallel to Secular Buddhism is the revival of Stoicism, often dubbed "Modern Stoicism." Like its Buddhist counterpart, Modern Stoicism adapts ancient techniques—such as the "dichotomy of control"—for contemporary psychological resilience.28

**The Trichotomy of Control**

Modern Stoics have refined the classical dichotomy (things under our control vs. things not) into a "trichotomy," adding a third category: things over which we have partial control (e.g., the outcome of a tennis match).28 The goal shifts from winning to "playing to the best of one's ability," a cognitive reframe that reduces anxiety in a "liquid" modern world where job security and relationships are often precarious.28

**The Silicon Valley Adoption**

Stoicism has found a particularly strong foothold in the tech industry and entrepreneurial circles, where it is often marketed as a "life hack" for emotional invincibility. This has led to the critique that Modern Stoicism is "sour grapes philosophy" or a tool for "social media brand managers" rather than a genuine path to virtue.29 Critics argue that by focusing so heavily on individual resilience ("It's not what happens to you, but how you react"), Modern Stoicism can breed political quietism and a lack of compassion for systemic injustice.29

**Virtue as the Sole Good**

Despite these critiques, the core of the movement remains the radical Stoic assertion that "Virtue is the only good".32 This proposition offers a powerful antidote to the consumerist narrative of the Meaning Crisis. It suggests that a meaningful life is not dependent on external success, wealth, or fame, but on the cultivation of character (wisdom, justice, courage, temperance). For the secular individual, this provides a "normative order" that is independent of divine command.33

### 4.3. Sam Harris and the "Waking Up" Project

Sam Harris's *Waking Up* project exemplifies the "Spiritual But Not Ridiculous" ethos in its most technologically integrated form. It explicitly attempts to decouple "spirituality" from "religion," defining the former as the experiential investigation of the nature of the mind.34

**The Philosophy of the Illusory Self**

Harris argues that self-transcendence—the realization that the "self" is an illusion—is a verifiable empirical experience accessible through mindfulness and Dzogchen-style practice, without requiring faith in "pseudoscientific metaphysics".34 The Waking Up app functions not merely as a meditation tool but as a "secular guide to living a better life," combining practice with philosophical theory.35

**Beyond Stress Reduction**

The platform emphasizes that the goal is not just "stress reduction" (a common criticism of secular mindfulness) but a fundamental shift in understanding consciousness.36 By focusing on the "nature of mind," Harris attempts to solve the "perennial problem" of suffering caused by identification with thoughts, offering a rational path to what religious traditions might call "liberation".34

**Critiques of the Rationalist Approach**

However, the "Waking Up" approach is not without its detractors. Some argue that removing the "religious" cultural elements (such as the devotional aspects of Buddhism) leads to a "shallow" or "dry" practice.39 A commentator noted that "meditation without an element of compassion... is basically training yourself to be an asshole," highlighting the danger of separating attention training from ethical cultivation.39 Harris has attempted to address this by integrating "Metta" (loving-kindness) practices and lessons on **Effective Altruism** into the platform, linking internal awareness with external ethical action.35 #tescreal

## Part V: Secular Religion and the Failure of Community

A critical component of the Meaning Crisis is the loss of community (the *sangha* or *ecclesia*). While "Rational Spirituality" provides tools for individual meaning-making, it has struggled to replicate the communal density of traditional religion. #sangha 

### 5.1. The Sunday Assembly and Oasis Network: The "Church for Atheists"

The Sunday Assembly, launched in London with the motto "Live Better, Help Often, Wonder More," attempted to create a "church without God," utilizing congregational singing, inspiring talks, and community service to build secular community.40 Similarly, the Oasis Network established values-based secular communities focused on "reality known through reason" and "meaning from making a difference".42

**The Decline of Secular Congregations**

Despite initial media enthusiasm and rapid expansion, these movements have faced significant contraction. By 2019, roughly half of the 70 Sunday Assembly chapters had closed or gone dormant.40 In 2024 and 2025, attendance statistics remain sparse, and the momentum has visibly slowed.43

Religions bind people through "sacred" obligations and shared metaphysical narratives that transcend convenience—elements that are difficult to engineer in a strictly secular context.

### 5.2. Effective Altruism (EA) as a Meaning System

Effective Altruism has emerged as a robust, albeit controversial, secular meaning system that avoids some of the pitfalls of the Sunday Assembly. Unlike the Assembly, which mimics church *structure*, EA mimics religious *purpose*. It offers a clear soteriology (saving lives/reducing suffering), a tithe (The Giving What We Can pledge), and a community of shared values.46

**The Religion of Rationality?**

While EA proponents deny it is a religion (lacking sacred times/places) 48, it functions as a "mythos" for the rationalist class. It answers the question "What should I do with my life?" with a mathematical imperative: do the most good possible.47 It provides a rigorous "normative order" rooted in utilitarian ethics.

**The Shadow of Utility**

However, EA is critiqued for contributing to the Meaning Crisis through its intense "utilitarian" approach, which can lead to "emotional coldness" and loneliness.49 By prioritizing "distant" beneficiaries (future people, global poor) over local community, it may exacerbate the sense of alienation or "rootlessness" that Vervaeke identifies as central to the crisis.47 The movement is currently grappling with how to integrate "deepening life" alongside "extending life," acknowledging that optimization without "soul" or "song" is insufficient for human flourishing.50

## Part VI: The Meta-Crisis and the Future of Meaning

### 6.1. The "Religion of No Religion"

John Vervaeke proposes that the solution to the Meaning Crisis is not a return to traditional religion (which is often impossible for the rational mind to accept fully) nor a descent into nihilism, but the cultivation of a "religion of no religion".1 This involves re-engineering enlightenment: taking the *psychotechnologies* of wisdom (meditation, contemplation, dialogue) and situating them within a scientific worldview.1

This "religion of no religion" must address the "Meta-Crisis"—the accelerating interaction of ecological, economic, and technological crises.6 Vervaeke argues that only a comprehensive transformation of consciousness, cognition, and culture can provide the wisdom necessary to navigate these existential threats.9 This requires moving beyond "propositional knowledge" (beliefs) to "participatory knowing" (transforming the way we connect with reality).1

### 6.2. Artificial Intelligence and the Crisis of Truth

The rise of Artificial Intelligence adds a terrifying new dimension to the Meaning Crisis. As AI systems generate "bullshit" (speech indifferent to truth), they threaten to erode the nomological order further.7 The *Cosmic Intelligence* podcast highlights that in an age of AI, "discernment" becomes the highest spiritual practice.7

The danger is that AI will accelerate the disintegration of shared reality, deepening the "Zombie" apocalypse where agency is lost to algorithmic manipulation. Conversely, AI forces humanity to ask the quintessential spiritual question: "What is consciousness, and what is unique about being human?".18 This pressure may act as a catalyst, forcing the "Spiritual But Not Ridiculous" movement to mature from a collection of "life hacks" into a robust philosophical defense of human sentient value.

### 6.3. Conclusion: The Architecture of the Future

The "Meaning Crisis" is not merely an academic concept but a lived reality for millions of "disaffiliated" individuals in the West. The collapse of the Christian narrative has not been followed by a seamless transition to secular humanism, but by a fragmentation into "Nones," "Neithers," and "Rational Spiritualists."

While the "Neither Religious Nor Spiritual" cohort represents a growing void of indifference, the "Spiritual But Not Ridiculous" movement represents a vigorous attempt to construct a new "architecture of meaning." Through Secular Buddhism, Modern Stoicism, and scientific spirituality (Harris/Vervaeke), this movement seeks to salvage the "machinery of meaning" from the wreckage of theism.

However, the failure of secular *communities* like the Sunday Assembly suggests that "rationality" alone is insufficient to bind people together. The challenge for the future is whether these "rational" psychotechnologies can evolve into a genuine "ecology of practices" that fosters not just individual resilience, but the collective wisdom required to survive the Meta-Crisis. Without this evolution, the modern subject remains at risk of drifting between the "Zombie" of mindless consumption and the "Totalitarian" allure of pseudo-religious ideology. **The task of the 21st century is to build a home in the cosmos that is scientifically sound, yet spiritually habitable—a task that is, by definition, spiritual but not ridiculous.**
