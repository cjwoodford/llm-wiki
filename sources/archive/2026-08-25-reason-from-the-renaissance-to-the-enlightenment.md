# Reason from the Renaissance to the Enlightenment

> Did the emphasis on human reason begin with the Renaissance and then amplify during the Enlightenment?

Yes, that's generally an accurate summary of how the emphasis on human reason developed in Western thought. #reason #enlightenment #renaissance #Europe 

Here's a breakdown:

- **The Renaissance (roughly 14th-17th centuries):** This period saw a significant shift from the largely theocentric worldview of the Middle Ages towards **humanism**.1 Renaissance humanism emphasized the value and agency of human beings, the study of classical literature and philosophy, and a renewed interest in human potential and achievement.2 While not rejecting God, humanists championed human reason, individualism, and the ability of humans to understand and shape their world. This laid crucial groundwork for the later Enlightenment.3 Thinkers like Petrarch and Erasmus encouraged critical thinking and a return to original sources of knowledge, rather than simply accepting traditional dogma.4
- **The Enlightenment (roughly 17th-18th centuries), also known as the Age of Reason:** This movement truly amplified the emphasis on human reason to an unprecedented degree.5 Enlightenment thinkers believed that reason was the primary source of authority and legitimacy, capable of uncovering truths about the world, religion, and politics, and ultimately leading to human progress and improvement.

    Key aspects of the Enlightenment's emphasis on reason include:

    - **Scientific Method:** Building on the Scientific Revolution, the Enlightenment championed empirical observation, experimentation, and rational analysis as the means to acquire knowledge.6 Figures like Isaac Newton, whose work demonstrated the universe's predictable, calculable regularity, significantly fueled this faith in reason.7
    - **Challenging Authority:** Enlightenment thinkers questioned traditional authority, including religious orthodoxy and absolute monarchy, subjecting everything to rational scrutiny.8
    - **Individual Autonomy:** There was a growing belief in the individual's capacity to reason for themselves, leading to ideas of individual rights, self-governance, and the pursuit of knowledge, freedom, and happiness.9 Immanuel Kant's famous motto, "Dare to know!10 Have courage to use your own reason!" perfectly encapsulates this spirit.
    - **Social and Political Reform:** Reason was seen as the tool to solve societal problems, improve government, and create a more just and rational social order, influencing revolutions like the American and French Revolutions.11

While the concept of reason has a long history in philosophy, dating back to ancient Greek thinkers like Plato and Aristotle, its widespread elevation to a central principle guiding society and human endeavor truly took off with the Renaissance and then became the defining characteristic of the Enlightenment.

## Colonialism

The Renaissance, a period of profound cultural, intellectual, and scientific rebirth in Europe (roughly 14th to 17th centuries), had significant, albeit complex, connections to the rise of colonialism and contributed to a nascent sense of European superiority.1

Here's how:

**Connections between the Renaissance and Colonialism:**

- **Technological Advancements:** The Renaissance fostered innovation that was crucial for long-distance maritime travel.2 This included:
    - **Improved Cartography:** More accurate maps and sea charts were developed.3
    - **New Navigational Tools:** The compass, astrolabe, and later the sextant became more sophisticated, enabling more precise navigation.4
    - **Shipbuilding Innovations:** The development of ships like the caravel, combining speed with cargo capacity, made voyages across oceans feasible.5
    - **Advancements in Weaponry:** Cannons and muskets gave European powers a military advantage in their conquests.
- **Spirit of Curiosity and Exploration:** The Renaissance emphasized human potential, observation, and a thirst for knowledge.6 This intellectual curiosity extended to the natural world and spurred a desire to explore unknown territories, discover new lands, and understand different peoples and resources.7 The idea of the "Renaissance Man," skilled in multiple fields and eager to expand knowledge, embodied this spirit.8
- **Economic Motivations:** The flourishing trade and commerce during the Renaissance, particularly in Italian city-states, generated wealth and a desire for new trade routes and resources.9 European nations sought to bypass existing overland routes controlled by the Ottoman Empire and gain direct access to valuable goods like spices, silks, and gold from Asia and Africa. This pursuit of wealth was a primary driver for exploration and colonization.10 The rise of merchant classes and banking systems also provided the financial backing for these costly expeditions.
- **Political Competition and Rise of Nation-States:** The Renaissance saw the consolidation of political power and the rise of strong, centralized nation-states (like Spain, Portugal, England, and France).11 These emerging powers competed with each other for global dominance, leading them to sponsor voyages of discovery and claim new territories to expand their influence and prestige.12
- **Religious Zeal:** While often intertwined with economic and political motives, the desire to spread Christianity was also a factor.13 Explorers and colonizers often viewed their expansion as a mission to convert indigenous populations, legitimizing their actions through a religious lens.14

**Did this new way of thinking lead to a newfound sense of superiority?**

Yes, the Renaissance, particularly through the lens of **humanism**, played a role in fostering a sense of European superiority, which sadly became a key ideological underpinning of colonialism:

- **Emphasis on Human Achievement and Rationality:** Renaissance humanism, while celebrating human potential and a renewed interest in classical thought, often implicitly positioned European (and particularly Western European) civilization as **the pinnacle of human achievement and rationality**.15 This contrasted with the "other" cultures encountered during exploration, which were often deemed "**primitive**" or "**savage**."
- **The "Universal Man" and its Exclusions:** The ideal of the "universal man" or "ideal person" emerging from humanism, while seemingly universal, was often implicitly defined by European characteristics and values. This created a framework where those who did not conform to this ideal could be categorized as "lesser" or "non-human," thus justifying their subjugation and exploitation.
- **Justification for Domination:** The perceived intellectual, technological, and cultural advancements of Europe during the Renaissance, often attributed to their "enlightened" thinking, were used to rationalize the conquest and colonization of non-European lands and peoples. The idea that Europeans were bringing "**civilization**," "**progress**," and "**true religion**" to these "inferior" societies became a powerful justification for their actions, even as they committed atrocities.
- **"Us vs. Them" Mentality:** The Age of Exploration, fueled by Renaissance curiosity, inevitably led to encounters with diverse cultures. The emerging European worldview, shaped by its own intellectual and cultural triumphs, often struggled to truly appreciate and respect these differences. Instead, it frequently developed an "us vs. them" mentality, where European ways were seen as inherently superior.
