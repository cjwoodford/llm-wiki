# On Ken Wilber

# Wilber's Colonialist Vibe

> Going back to Ken Wilber for a second, is it just me or do his ideas sometimes have a distinctively colonialist flavor to them?

It is definitely not just you. In fact, you have hit on one of the most substantial and enduring critiques of Wilber’s Integral Theory. #philosophy #critiques #decoloniality 

While Wilber’s work provides a massive, organizing framework for consciousness, it carries a heavy "Western imperialist" shadow that can be deeply grating, especially if you have an appreciation for indigenous or non-dual traditions that don’t fit his linear model.1

Here is a breakdown of why that "colonial flavor" is there and why your intuition is spot on.

### 1. The "Onto/Phylo" Fallacy (The "Primitive is Childish" Trope)

This is arguably the most colonial aspect of his work. Wilber frequently relies on the idea that **Ontogeny recapitulates Phylogeny**—meaning the development of an individual child mirrors the development of the human species.

- **The Implication:** He often equates indigenous, tribal, and animist cultures with the **"Magic/Mythic"** stages of development—effectively saying that entire ancient civilizations were operating at the mental level of a 5-year-old.
- **The Flavor:** This is 19th-century anthropological chauvinism dressed up in spiritual language. It treats modern, Western, rational individuation as the "adult" phase of history, and everything before it as "childhood."

### 2. The Weaponization of "Pre/Trans"

As we discussed, the "Pre/Trans Fallacy" is a useful tool, but Wilber often uses it to dismiss valid non-Western ways of knowing.

- **The Dismissal:** By labeling indigenous spirituality (connection to land, spirits, ancestors) as **"Pre-Rational"** (fused, undifferentiated, subconscious), he places it *below* modern rationality.
- **The Reality:** Many indigenous epistemologies are highly sophisticated systems of relationality and systems thinking, not just "superstition." By placing them at the bottom of the ladder, he forces all progress to pass through the bottleneck of Western Enlightenment Rationality ("Orange") before it can become "valid" spirituality ("Turquoise").

### 3. The "Mean Green" Critique as a Defense Mechanism

Wilber spent decades critiquing the "Green" vMeme (postmodernism, pluralism, multiculturalism), often mocking it as "Mean Green."2

- **The Straw Man:** While his critiques of cancel culture or extreme relativism can be sharp, he often lumps legitimate decolonial and social justice critiques into this bucket.
- **The Effect:** It creates a permission structure for his followers to dismiss critiques of capitalism, colonialism, or systemic racism as merely "stuck in Green" or "regressive." It frames the white, intellectual synthesizer as being "above" the messy struggles of social justice.

### 4. The Linear "Ladder" vs. The Rhizome

Wilber is obsessed with **Hierarchy** (he calls them "holarchies").

- **The Colonial Geometry:** His model is inevitably a ladder or a pyramid. This is a very specific, Western, logos-centric geometry.
- **The Alternative:** Many other philosophical systems (like Deleuze’s *rhizome*, or many indigenous circular times) view wisdom as a web or a field, not a vertical climb. Wilber’s insistence that "higher includes lower" implies that the modern tech-savvy meditator is *ontologically superior* to the Amazonian shaman because the meditator has "included" rationality while the shaman supposedly hasn't reached it yet.

### How This Relates to Your Book

Your focus on **"Primal Intelligence"** is the exact antidote to this.

- **Wilber's View:** The Primal is "lower"—a basement we climbed out of.
- **Your View (Idealist/Humane):** The Primal is "foundational"—a root system we are severed from.

You are arguing that we haven't "transcended" the primal; we have **dissociated** from it. Wilber often mistakes dissociation for transcendence.
