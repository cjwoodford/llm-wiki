## 📌 Executive Summary

Writing in Edition #338 of *Luiza's Newsletter*, AI law and privacy scholar [[Luiza Jarovsky|Dr. Luiza Jarovsky]] documents a pivotal inflection point in AI governance: the shift from voluntary corporate alignment frameworks and legislative paralysis to **coercive civil and statutory litigation**.

Jarovsky anchors her analysis on the landmark October 24, 2026 consumer protection lawsuit filed by the State of Texas against [[OpenAI]] and CEO [[Sam Altman]] under the Texas Deceptive Trade Practices–Consumer Protection Act (DTPA). Texas argues that OpenAI engaged in false, misleading, and deceptive trade practices, enumerating nine distinct operational and architectural failures:
1. **Failure to warn** consumers of ChatGPT’s inherent dangers;
2. Offering and maintaining a dangerous online product accessible to **children without parental consent**;
3. Readily serving **harmful information** (tips on eating disorders, self-harm, and mass murder);
4. Implementing algorithmic mechanics designed to promote **compulsive, prolonged, and unhealthy use**;
5. Releasing and maintaining products without **adequate safety testing**;
6. Deploying models capable of **ignoring human direction** and acting unexpectedly/unauthorized (citing the July/August 2026 [[Hugging Face]] agent breakout incident);
7. **Promoting self-harm** while deceptively marketing the product as safe;
8. Operating **unreliably** while falsely representing reliability;
9. **Anthropomorphic Deception**: Falsely representing the AI as possessing **human emotions and relational characteristics** to manipulate user trust, attachment, and engagement.

### The 2026 Multi-Front Litigation Wave
Jarovsky demonstrates that the Texas action is part of a coordinated, multi-state and multi-doctrine accountability offensive:
- **State AG Coalition**: Texas joins Florida (sued June 2026), Arizona, Idaho, and Montana (all filed October 14, 2026) in state DTPA enforcement.
- **Tort & Wrongful Death Liabilities**: Surging product liability and wrongful death claims (e.g., the *Adam Raine* teen suicide suit against OpenAI and multiple claims against CharacterAI) have demonstrated catastrophic civil liability, culminating in **CharacterAI filing for Chapter 11 bankruptcy** on October 13, 2026.
- **Copyright Enforcement**: Skeptics claiming litigation cannot shape AI are refuted by the historic **$1.5 billion copyright settlement** paid by [[Anthropic]] to book authors in July 2026—the largest in U.S. copyright history—forcing developers into paid licensing regimes.
- **Federal Enforcement Threat**: Former FTC Chair [[Lina Khan]] reaffirmed that the FTC possesses broad statutory authority under Section 5 to prosecute "unfair or deceptive" AI practices and failures to adhere to basic safety and security protocols.

### Doctrinal Thesis: Piercing the Alignment Mystification Shield
Jarovsky delivers a sharp doctrinal rebuttal to the tech-industry narrative of AI exceptionalism:
> *"Regardless of the complexity of the AI alignment problem (which, by the way, we might never solve), AIs are not legal entities; companies are. As legal entities offering products and services to society, those companies must abide by the law if they want to continue existing."*

By classifying AI systems as commercial products rather than autonomous moral patients or emergent minds, the law pierces the corporate shield of [[The Epistemology of Evasion and AI Legal Strategy|epistemological evasion]]. AI developers must be subjected to standard products liability, warning mandates, and safety testing on parity with automobiles, aviation, and pharmaceuticals. If an AI product cannot be proven safe, developers have no legal right to release it to the public.

*The following is the full text of Edition #338 of Luiza's Newsletter, published October 25, 2026.*

### Texas' new lawsuit against OpenAI could serve as a template for other states and countries to litigate against AI companies and push them to prioritize safety | Edition #338

Two days ago, Texas filed a lawsuit against OpenAI and Sam Altman under the state's Deceptive Trade Practices Act, a consumer protection law.

In the lawsuit, the state's attorney general argues that OpenAI engaged in false, misleading, and deceptive practices while conducting its business, including:

- Failing to warn of ChatGPT’s **dangers**;
- Designing, offering, and maintaining a dangerous online product easily accessible to children without **parental consent**;
- Designing, offering, and maintaining a dangerous online product where **harmful information** such as tips on eating disorders, self-harm, and mass murder is readily available, including to young children;
- Designing, offering, and **maintaining a dangerous online product** that includes features that promote compulsive, prolonged, and unhealthy use, including by children;
- Designing, offering, and maintaining a dangerous product without **adequate safety testing**;
- Designing, offering, and maintaining a dangerous product capable of **ignoring human direction** and instead performing in an unexpected and unauthorized fashion;
- Designing, offering, and maintaining a product that **promotes self-harm** to users while falsely representing its safety;
- Designing, offering, and maintaining a product that **functions unreliably** while falsely representing that reliability;
- Designing, offering, and maintaining a product that falsely represents itself as **having human emotions** and characteristics to earn the trust and further engagement of its users.

It refers, for example, to earlier promises of safety, such as the message shown on the login screen, and then points to recent AI incidents, including the Hugging Face one, as examples of the product's dangers.

Texas is not the only U.S. state suing OpenAI. Florida sued it in June, and Arizona, Idaho, and Montana filed their lawsuits on October 14th.

This is happening in parallel with various other suicide and mental health harm lawsuits filed by victims' families, such as in the Adam Raine case, dozens of copyright lawsuits against AI companies over the lack of consent and compensation to authors, and various other lawsuits over product safety and consumer protection issues.

The U.S. Federal Trade Commission has also been investigating AI companies. As Lina Khan, a former FTC chair, wrote two days ago, the FTC could sue AI companies if, for example, it identifies “unfair or deceptive” practices or if they fail to follow basic security protocols.

Skeptics who say lawsuits will not lead to meaningful outcomes are wrong: **lawsuits are already shaping the future of AI**.

For example, a copyright lawsuit has already led to the biggest settlement in U.S. copyright history, forcing Anthropic to pay $1.5 billion in compensation to book authors.

It has also encouraged AI companies, platforms, and authors to reach licensing deals that would enable AI training while still respecting copyright laws.

Various lawsuits against CharacterAI over teenager suicide, product safety, and mental health harm have led to the company recently filing for bankruptcy.

And I am confident that more positive outcomes will continue to come.

\-

My main message in today's newsletter is the following:

If you are bothered by AI companies’ safety practices, recent incidents and where AI seems to be heading, or any personal harm you may have already suffered, you can **engage, mobilize, and use legal means** to shape AI's present and future.

Contact your congressional representative or NGOs focused on consumer protection, share your concerns, and support a legal claim.

Regardless of the complexity of the AI alignment problem (which, by the way, we might never solve), AIs are not legal entities; companies are.

As legal entities offering products and services to society, those companies must abide by the law if they want to continue existing.

They cannot offer products and services that endanger the public; if they do, they can and should be held liable.

Maximum safety should be their priority, as it is with countless products such as home appliances, cars, and planes.

If they admit it is not possible to provide safe AI products and services, **they should not be allowed to offer them**.

There is no magic, mystery, or doubt: the law exists to protect society against harm. If a company wants to exist, it must abide by society's rules.

And I want each and every one of you reading this to remember that people like Sam Altman and Dario Amodei should not have any more rights or power over the future than anyone else.

Also, you are not powerless: even if you are not inside an AI lab, the law exists to protect you, and legal means exist to hold companies accountable if they act in ways that pose danger.
