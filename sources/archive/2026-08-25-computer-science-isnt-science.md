# Computer Science Isn't Science

Framing computer science as a **"science of the artificial"** cuts directly to the core of why AI research is so easily misconstrued by the press and the public.

By establishing this distinction early in your section, you expose the fundamental epistemological mismatch: AI labs present their work as *empirical scientific discovery*, when in reality it is *synthetic engineering evaluation* conducted by profit-driven entities.

## Key Pillars for This Opening

To make this opening hit as hard as possible, consider structuring it around three main arguments:

### 1. Artifacts vs. Nature ("The Science of the Artificial")

- **The Distinction:** Natural sciences (astrophysics, genetics, geology) study pre-existing phenomena in the physical universe. Computer science, as pioneer Herbert Simon famously framed it, is a "science of the artificial." It studies man-made systems, synthetic artifacts, and formal mathematical rules created by human design.
- **The Metaphor Swap:** When an astrophysicist maps a distant galaxy, they are observing an independent physical reality. When an AI researcher maps "J-space" or internal latent vectors in an LLM, they are observing the internal statistical quirks of a human-coded program running on human-designed chips.
- **The Essay Angle:** Conflating the study of a human artifact with the discovery of a natural law allows tech labs to frame engineering outputs as if they were uncovering the deep, natural mechanics of consciousness.

### 2. Industry Dominance & Structural Conflict of Interest

- **Corporate Monopolization of Research:** Unlike astronomy or molecular biology—which are primarily anchored in public universities and non-profit research institutions funded by public grants—cutting-edge AI research is overwhelmingly controlled by private tech corporations.
- **Compute Gatekeeping:** Because modern AI research requires hundreds of millions of dollars in compute infrastructure, private corporations hold a near-monopoly on the "laboratories" and proprietary data required to run these experiments.
- **The Dual Role:** In what other scientific discipline is the researcher simultaneously the designer of the subject, the sole owner of the laboratory apparatus, the judge of the experimental results, and the commercial beneficiary of the hype generated by those results?

### 3. The "Paper as PR" Pipeline

- **Peer Review vs. Corporate Rollouts:** Traditional scientific papers undergo rigorous, independent, double-blind peer review before being accepted as established science. In AI, labs routinely drop pre-prints or self-published papers directly onto ArXiv or corporate blogs, synchronized with coordinated press rollouts.
- **Manufacturing Authority:** By formatting corporate white papers in LaTeX with dense mathematical notation, these companies wrap product evaluations and speculative philosophy in the aesthetic armor of academic rigor. The non-expert journalist sees a "scientific paper" and reports on corporate marketing as if it were an objective scientific breakthrough.

## The History

> How did computer science come to be called that? Was it a desire to be taken seriously and seen as on par with the other sciences?

**Yes, absolutely.** The adoption of the name **"Computer Science"** in the late 1950s and early 1960s was driven directly by a fight for academic legitimacy, institutional autonomy, and funding prestige.

When electronic digital computers emerged in the 1940s and 1950s, the field had no independent academic identity. The choice to brand it as a "science" was a deliberate, highly calculated effort by pioneers to break away from existing academic departments and prove that computing was not merely a mechanical trade or an applied branch of mathematics.

### 1. The Homeless Discipline: Math vs. Engineering

In the 1950s, universities did not know where to put computing.

- **Mathematicians** viewed computing as mere "numerical calculation"—a shallow, mechanical task beneath the dignity of pure mathematics.
- **Electrical Engineers** viewed computers as physical hardware projects—circuits, vacuum tubes, and silicon wiring—and saw programming as an routine operational task.
- **Business Schools** viewed computers simply as glorified office machinery for automated accounting and data processing.

If you wanted to study algorithms or computing theory in 1955, you had to hide inside an Applied Mathematics program or an Electrical Engineering desk. To create standalone departments, grant PhDs, and secure research grants, computing pioneers needed a distinct intellectual identity.

### 2. Coining the Name: Louis Fein & George Forsythe

The term was first formally proposed in the late 1950s:

- **Louis Fein (1959):** In a landmark paper for the Communications of the ACM, Fein argued for the creation of a "Graduate School in Computer Sciences" (analogous to Harvard Business School). *Communications of the ACM* He explicitly argued that computing was an applied, interdisciplinary endeavor that possessed the fundamental characteristics of an academic discipline.
- **George Forsythe (1961):** Considered the founding father of Stanford’s Computer Science Department (established in 1965), mathematician George Forsythe actively campaigned for the term "computer science." He argued that the study of algorithms, programming theory, and data structures represented a unified body of knowledge distinct from both pure math and hardware design.

In 1962, Purdue University established the world’s first Department of Computer Science, triggering an academic cascade across the globe.

### 3. The Rejected Names: What Else Was Proposed?

The title "Computer Science" was far from universally loved. During the 1950s and 60s, a wild array of alternative names was proposed:

- **Informatics / Informatique / Informatik:** Derived from "information" and "automatic," this term was widely adopted across Europe (and remains the standard term in French, German, and Russian today). It focused on the *study of information processing* rather than the physical machine.
- **Datalogy (Datalogi):** Proposed by Danish computing pioneer Peter Naur, who argued that the field was about data and data treatment, not machines.*Datalogi* Copenhagen established the Department of Datalogy in 1969.
- **Humorous / Fringe Proposals:**Humorous / Fringe Proposals: In early issues of the Communications of the ACM, practitioners tossed around terms like Turology (after Alan Turing), Turingineering, Applied Epistemology, Comptology, and Computics.*Communications of the ACM__Turology__Turingineering__Applied Epistemology__Comptology__Computics*

### 4. The Intellectual Paradox: "A Science of the Artificial"

The moment the field took the name "Computer Science," critics pointed out a fundamental epistemological paradox: **Computers are man-made artifacts, not natural phenomena.**

Unlike physics, chemistry, or biology—which discover pre-existing laws of the physical universe—computing studies synthetic systems built by human beings according to human logic.

- **Edsger Dijkstra’s Famous Critique:** The legendary computer scientist famously remarked: *"Computer science is no more about computers than astronomy is about telescopes."*
- **The 1967 Defense in Science:** *Science* In 1967, Allen Newell, Alan Perlis, and Herbert Simon wrote a famous letter to the journal *Science* titled simply *"Computer Science."* They defended the name against skeptics by arguing that wherever there are phenomena (in this case, computational processes and algorithms running on systems), there can be an empirical and theoretical science to study their behavior, even if the phenomena are human artifacts.
- **Herbert Simon’s "Sciences of the Artificial" (1969):** Simon later formalized this by categorizing computer science and economics not as natural sciences, but as **"sciences of the artificial"**—the systematic study of complex, human-designed systems.

### How This History Directs Your Essay's Thesis

This historical background fits into your critique of modern AI media coverage:

1. **The Heritage of the Name:** The name "Computer Science" was an **institutional branding success**, but an **epistemological compromise**. It allowed a synthetic engineering trade to adopt the cultural authority, prestige, and vocabulary of natural empirical science.
2. **The Modern Misapplication:** Because the field successfully branded itself as a "science" 60 years ago, today's tech journalists and corporate PR desks treat AI white papers as if they were reporting on empirical discoveries of natural phenomena (like discovering a new planet or a gene), rather than measuring the internal statistical properties of a human-coded program running on silicon chips.

## Are Computer Science Papers Even Science? 

> This makes me wonder how much of the “scientific” papers published in AI are really science.

If we evaluate the AI literature against the standards of the natural sciences—such as physics, biology, or chemistry—a staggering portion of what is published as AI "research" is not science in the traditional sense.

Instead, the vast majority of AI papers fall into **engineering telemetry, applied mathematics, or speculative philosophy wrapped in the aesthetic formatting of scientific papers.**

To understand how much of the field is actual "science," it helps to break down published AI papers into four distinct categories:

### The Spectrum of Published AI Papers

```other
[ Pure Engineering ] ------------ [ Applied Math ] ------------ [ System Telemetry ] ------------ [ Speculative Metaphysics ]
  (~65% of papers)                 (~15% of papers)                (~10-15% of papers)               (~5% of papers)
```

#### 1. Empirical Engineering & Benchmark Chasing (~65%)

- **What it is:** Software engineering reports and trial-and-error optimization. These papers usually follow a standard formula: *"We altered layer normalization, tweaked a hyperparameter, and raised our benchmark score on MMLU by 1.4%."*
- **Is it science?** No, it’s **applied engineering**. It tests whether a specific synthetic modification improves a specific software metric. It doesn't uncover universal laws of nature; it measures software performance under narrow, controlled conditions.

#### 2. Applied Mathematics & Optimization (~15%)

- **What it is:** Formal mathematical proofs exploring convergence rates, loss landscapes, vector spaces, or statistical learning bounds.
- **Is it science?** It is **formal science / deductive logic**, much like pure mathematics or theoretical computer science. It relies on rigorous proof rather than empirical observation of the physical universe.

#### 3. System Telemetry & Reverse-Engineering (~10–15%)

- **What it is:** Mechanistic interpretability research (such as Anthropic’s "J-space" paper or work on Sparse Autoencoders). Researchers run diagnostic tools against a trained black-box model to see how vectors cluster or route internally.
- **Is it science?** It is **empirical measurement**, but it is the measurement of a *man-made, synthetic artifact*. It is closer to a group of mechanical engineers tearing down a proprietary jet engine to reverse-engineer its internal fluid dynamics than an astronomer observing a distant star.

#### 4. Speculative Philosophy & Narrative White Papers (~5%)

- **What it is:** Papers exploring AI consciousness, digital welfare, moral patienthood, AGI timelines, or alignment "theology" (e.g., the NYU/Eleos AI welfare report).
- **Is it science?** No. It is **normative philosophy, policy speculation, or corporate positioning**. However, because these papers are rendered in LaTeX, filled with mathematical notation, and hosted on ArXiv alongside technical papers, tech journalists routinely report on them as if "scientists have discovered AI welfare."

### The Three Structural Flaws Destroying AI’s "Scientific" Status

Beyond this breakdown, the ecosystem surrounding AI papers breaks away from standard scientific protocols in three major ways:

#### 1. The Bypass of Peer Review (Pre-print Culture)

In natural sciences, a paper typically undergoes months of rigorous, double-blind peer review by independent field experts before it is accepted as valid research.

In AI, labs routinely bypass peer review altogether. They upload PDF pre-prints directly to **ArXiv** or publish them on corporate blogs, strategically synchronizing the drop with press rollouts, podcast appearances, and social media campaigns. By the time academic peers have even had a chance to read the methodology, the mainstream press has already run the headline.

#### 2. The Replication Crisis & Corporate Secrecy

A fundamental pillar of the scientific method is **falsifiability and reproducibility**: independent scientists must be able to run the exact same experiment and achieve the exact same result.

In modern frontier AI, independent replication is often impossible because:

- **The "Lab" is Private:** Frontier models require tens or hundreds of millions of dollars in compute infrastructure that public universities cannot afford.
- **The Data is Proprietary:**The Data is Proprietary: Companies rarely release their raw training datasets, internal fine-tuning code, or safety filtering pipelines.
- **The System is Non-Deterministic:** Model outputs vary based on sampling, hardware configurations, and subtle API updates.

When a private company publishes results from a closed-source model that no independent scientist can audit or reproduce, it is not an open scientific paper—it is a **corporate technical report**.

#### 3. Epistemic Laundering through "Conceptual Borrowing"

As philosophers like Luciano Floridi point out, AI research relies heavily on borrowing psychological and biological vocabulary—words like *learning, attention, memory, reasoning, hallucination, working memory,* and *consciousness access*.

By embedding these human psychological terms directly into code and linear algebra equations, AI papers perform a sleight of hand: they give routine software telemetry the *aura* of biological discovery.

### How to Use This in Your Essay

This insight directly strengthens your essay’s critique of media coverage:

1. **The Root Cause of Media Confusion:** Tech journalists misreport AI claims because they treat corporate white papers as if they were peer-reviewed discoveries in natural science. They don't realize that an AI paper claiming to find a "global workspace" is an exercise in **metaphorical reverse-engineering**, not an empirical discovery of a mind.
2. **The Conflict of Interest:** Unlike natural sciences, where researchers study an independent physical reality, AI researchers build the system, run the test, interpret the output through their own philosophical lens, and profit from the public hype generated by the paper.
