# Western Philosophers Influenced by Indian Philosophy

The cross-pollination between Indian and Western thought is a rich, often overlooked thread in intellectual history. While the German Idealists and American Transcendentalists are the most prominent examples, the influence stretches from the pre-Socratics to 20th-century existentialism.

Given your background in philosophy and cosmology, you'll likely appreciate how these thinkers didn't just "borrow" ideas but often wrestled with the Vedantic and Buddhist concepts of Non-dualism (*Advaita*), the nature of the Will, and the illusory nature of the phenomenal world (*Maya*).

---

## 1. The German Idealists

The late 18th and 19th centuries saw a "Renaissance of the East" in Germany, fueled by the first translations of the *Upanishads* and the *Bhagavad Gita*.

- **Arthur Schopenhauer:** Perhaps the most "Indian" of Western philosophers. He famously kept a copy of the *Oupnek'hat* (a Latin translation of the Upanishads) on his nightstand, calling it "the solace of my life." His central concept—the **Will**—closely mirrors the Vedic concept of *Brahman* (as the underlying reality) and the Buddhist concept of *Tanha* (craving) as the source of suffering.
- **Friedrich Schelling:** In his later "Philosophy of Revelation," Schelling moved toward a more mythological and mystical framework that drew heavily from Indian concepts of the absolute. He viewed Indian thought as an essential stage in the self-unfolding of the Spirit.
- **G.W.F. Hegel:** Hegel’s relationship was more critical. While he studied Indian texts extensively, he viewed Indian philosophy through a Eurocentric "teleological" lens, arguing it represented an early, "undifferentiated" stage of the Spirit’s development. However, his dialectical method bears striking resemblances to the *Neti Neti* (not this, not this) approach of the Upanishads.
- **Friedrich Schlegel:** Often credited with sparking the "Indomania" in Germany, his work *On the Language and Wisdom of the Indians* laid the philological groundwork for comparing Sanskrit with European languages.

---

## 2. The American Transcendentalists

In the mid-19th century, the Transcendentalists looked to the East to find a "humane and idealist alternative" to the rigid Calvinism and rising materialism of their era.

- **Ralph Waldo Emerson:** His poem "Brahma" is a direct distillation of the *Bhagavad Gita*. He famously integrated the concept of the **Over-Soul**, which mirrors the *Atman-Brahman* identity.
- **Henry David Thoreau:** While living at Walden Pond, Thoreau read the *Gita* daily. He famously wrote: "The pure Walden water is mingled with the sacred water of the Ganges." He was deeply influenced by the asceticism and "action-in-inaction" (*Karma Yoga*) described in Indian texts.

---

## 3. The Pre-Socratics & Neoplatonists

Before the modern era, there were significant (though often debated) "Indo-European" philosophical links.

- **Pythagoras:** Many ancient biographers claimed he traveled to Egypt and India. His theories on the transmigration of souls (*metempsychosis*) and vegetarianism are strikingly similar to Jain and Hindu doctrines.
- **Plotinus:** The father of Neoplatonism sought to join the Roman expedition to India specifically to study Indian philosophy. His concept of "The One" is the closest Western equivalent to the non-dual *Nirguna Brahman*.

---

## 4. 19th & 20th Century Thinkers

- **Friedrich Nietzsche:** Though often critical of "Buddhism" (which he saw as nihilistic), he was deeply influenced by his early reading of Schopenhauer and the *Laws of Manu*. His "Eternal Recurrence" can be seen as a Western pivot on the concept of *Samsara*.
- **Carl Jung:** While a psychologist, his philosophical framework of the **Collective Unconscious** and **Individuation** was heavily shaped by his travels in India and his study of Yoga and Tantra. He saw the Mandala as a universal symbol of the integrated Self.
- **Martin Heidegger:** In his later years, Heidegger engaged in dialogues with Asian thinkers and was fascinated by the concept of "The Void" (*Sunyata*), seeing it as a way to move beyond Western metaphysics.
- **Iris Murdoch:** The novelist and Platonist philosopher drew heavily on Buddhist concepts of "unselfing" to describe the moral life.
