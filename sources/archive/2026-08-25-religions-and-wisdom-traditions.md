# Religions and Wisdom Traditions

Of course. Here is a structured argument for the proposition that the term "religion" should be reserved for Abrahamic faiths, while traditions like Buddhism and Hinduism should be categorized differently. #religion #spirituality 

This argument is rooted in etymology, theology, and philosophy, and posits that the Western concept of "religion" is intrinsically shaped by the structure and assumptions of Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. Applying it wholesale to Dharmic traditions is a category error that obscures more than it clarifies.

Here is the argument:

### The Argument for a Narrow Definition of "Religion"

The assertion that traditions like Buddhism and Hinduism are not "religions" in the same sense as the Abrahamic faiths rests on four key distinctions: (1) the etymological and conceptual foundation of the term "religion" itself; (2) the role and nature of God or ultimate reality; (3) the ultimate goal of the spiritual path; and (4) the primary means of apprehending truth.

1. **The Foundational Concept: A Divine Covenant**

The English word "religion" originates from the Latin religio, which is most often linked to the verb religare, meaning "to bind" or "to tie fast." This etymology powerfully reflects the core of the Abrahamic traditions. Their central organizing principle is a covenant—a binding agreement between a transcendent, personal God and humanity.

- In Judaism, it is the covenant between God and Abraham, and later the Law delivered to Moses at Sinai.
- In Christianity, it is the new covenant established through the sacrifice of Jesus Christ.
- In Islam, it is humanity's submission (islam) to the will of Allah as revealed through his final prophet, Muhammad.

In all three, the human is "bound" to God through faith, obedience, and a shared historical narrative. Religion, in this sense, is a divinely initiated relationship defined by commandments, revelation, and loyalty to a singular, external, and superior being.

By contrast, Eastern traditions like Hinduism and Buddhism do not share this foundational concept of being "bound" to a creator God in a covenantal relationship. Their root concept is Dharma (from the Sanskrit root dhṛ, meaning "to hold" or "to sustain"). Dharma is not a covenant but the cosmic law, the natural order, the way things are. The goal is not to bind oneself to a divine person, but to align oneself with this impersonal, universal principle.

2. **The Theological Distinction: The Question of God**

The Abrahamic faiths are fundamentally theistic, and strictly monotheistic. The existence of one, and only one, transcendent Creator God who is separate from His creation is non-negotiable. This God is a personal being with whom one can have a relationship of prayer, worship, and petition.

The Dharmic traditions present a vastly different theological landscape:

- Buddhism is largely non-theistic. The Buddha was a human being who achieved enlightenment, not a god to be worshipped for salvation. The focus is on the nature of mind and the cessation of suffering (dukkha) through self-effort, not on devotion to a creator. While celestial beings (devas) may exist, they are also subject to the laws of karma and are not the ultimate reality.
- Hinduism is extraordinarily diverse, but its core philosophical schools are not monotheistic in the Abrahamic sense. The ultimate reality, Brahman, is typically conceived as an impersonal, all-pervading, transcendent and immanent principle—the ground of all being. While Hinduism features a vast pantheon of gods and goddesses (devas), in many schools (like Advaita Vedanta), these are understood as manifestations or aspects of the singular, impersonal Brahman. This can be described as pantheistic, panentheistic, or henotheistic, but it is not the strict creator-creature monotheism of the West. The focus is on realizing one's unity with Brahman (Atman is Brahman), not on worshipping a separate creator.
3. **The Teleological Goal: Salvation vs. Liberation**

The ultimate aim of the Abrahamic and Dharmic paths is profoundly different.

- The goal in Abrahamic religion is Salvation. This is typically conceived as being saved from sin and damnation and gaining eternal life in the presence of God (Heaven or Paradise). This is an event that occurs after death and is granted by God based on faith, grace, and righteous works performed during a single, linear lifetime.
- The goal in Dharmic traditions is Liberation (Moksha in Hinduism, Nirvana in Buddhism). This is not salvation from a deity's judgment, but liberation from the cycle of suffering and rebirth (samsara). This is not an afterlife in a heavenly realm, but the extinguishing of the causes of suffering (craving, aversion, ignorance) and the realization of one's true nature, here and now. It is a psychological and ontological transformation achieved through insight and practice, over potentially countless lifetimes within a cyclical cosmology.
4. **The Epistemological Method: Faith vs. Insight**

How is ultimate truth known and realized?

- In the Abrahamic traditions, the primary mode is faith. Truth is delivered through divine revelation to prophets and recorded in sacred scriptures. The believer's duty is to accept these revealed truths and live in accordance with them. The epistemology is top-down: from God to humanity.
- In the Dharmic traditions, while scriptures are revered, the ultimate mode is direct experience and insight (prajñā in Buddhism, jñāna in Hinduism). Scriptures are seen as maps, but they are not the territory. The ultimate truth must be verified and realized within one's own consciousness through practices like meditation, yoga, and ethical discipline. The epistemology is bottom-up and experiential: the truth is discovered within the self. The Buddha famously encouraged his followers to "see for themselves" rather than to believe on blind faith.

### **Conclusion**: A Call for New Terminology

To call Buddhism a "religion" is to force it into a theistic, covenantal framework that is alien to its core. It imposes a God where there is none, demands a faith where it asks for inquiry, and promises a salvation that is not its goal. Similarly, reducing the multifaceted philosophical system of Hinduism to the category of "religion" flattens its diverse theological stances and mistakes its ultimate goal of liberation (moksha) for the Abrahamic concept of salvation.

Therefore, for the sake of conceptual clarity and intellectual honesty, a more precise vocabulary is needed. The term "religion" should be reserved for the Abrahamic traditions of Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, which are coherently defined by their covenantal, monotheistic, and faith-based structure.

For traditions like Hinduism and Buddhism, more accurate descriptors would be "Dharmic Traditions," "Wisdom Traditions," or "Paths of Liberation." Such terms accurately center their focus on cosmic law, experiential insight, and the ultimate goal of freedom from samsara, thereby respecting their unique character rather than viewing them through a distorting Western lens.
