# Critics of Computationalism

> Has anyone written or spoken critically of this computationalist metaphysics that's prevalent in Silicon Valley?

Your focus on the **metaphysics** of Silicon Valley hits exactly where the real battle is being fought. As you noted, the greatest threat of TESCREAL ideologies isn't just their legislative lobbying; it is their underlying ontology—specifically **computationalism** (the belief that the universe is fundamentally information and the human mind is merely software running on temporary biological hardware). When this worldview is baked into technology, it subtly formats the public consciousness to view humans as fundamentally flawed or inefficient algorithms waiting to be upgraded.

Several brilliant contemporary and historical thinkers have targeted this exact metaphysical paradigm, arguing that treating human life as a machine error is a catastrophic philosophical blunder.

## 1. The Metaphor Interrogators: Meghan O'Gieblyn

Her book, *God, Human, Animal, Machine: Technology, Metaphor, and the Search for Meaning*, is arguably the most precise contemporary dismantling of Silicon Valley’s metaphysics.

- **The Core Critique:** O’Gieblyn (a former theology student turned technology essayist) argues that Silicon Valley hasn't actually pioneered a hyper-rational, forward-looking worldview. Instead, transhumanists have unwittingly resurrected **old Christian eschatology and Gnostic tropes**, re-packaging concepts like resurrection, the soul, and the divine into the language of code and data sets.
- **How She Attacks the Metaphysics:** She exposes the philosophical "sleight of hand" where tech elites discard the mind-body problem by treating "information" as an almost mystical third substance that magically bridges the physical and spiritual. She warns that by literalizing the computer metaphor, we execute a bad "philosophy of data" that strips human consciousness of its qualitative meaning, treating the human soul as little more than a data set to be optimized or escaped.

## 2. The Process Philosophers: Matthew David Segall

A philosopher at the California Institute of Integral Studies, Segall works extensively to expose what he terms the *Imago Machinae*—our modern habit of making ourselves in the image of our machines.

- **The Core Critique:** Segall focuses on dismantling the modern world's dominant myth: the machine metaphor. He points out that the Simulation Hypothesis and hyper-automated AI frameworks are little more than rebooted, disenchanted materialism mixed with ancient mystical suspicions.
- **How He Attacks the Metaphysics:** He argues that treating human beings as mere algorithms is a dangerous, self-fulfilling prophecy. By viewing ourselves through a cold, mechanical cosmology, we risk actively formatting our behavior to turn into the very mindless, predictable mechanisms we imagine we are. Instead, he draws on process philosophy to advocate for a "Philosophy of the Organism," where the universe is understood as an alive, participatory process.

## 3. The Phenomenological & Biological Front: Hubert Dreyfus & Michael Levin

To break the spell of computationalism, some critics use hard biology and cognitive science to show that the tech industry's software-hardware map is completely distinct from organic reality.

- **Hubert Dreyfus:** Decades before the current generative AI boom, this phenomenological philosopher wrote the seminal books *What Computers Can't Do* and *What Computers Still Can't Do*. Using philosophers like Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty, Dreyfus argued that human intelligence is fundamentally **embodied, situated, and relational**. It cannot be reduced to the formal symbol-manipulation of a computer because human beings operate through a bodily "being-in-the-world" that cannot be converted into a clean data model without losing its essence.
- **Michael Levin:** On the biological side, Levin’s groundbreaking work on bioelectricity directly disrupts the top-down programming model of life. He demonstrates that cellular collectives possess an inherent, decentralized agency and intelligence that cannot be reduced to a static, computational genetic code. This proves that life is inherently animate and self-determining, rather than a machine running pre-programmed software.

## 4. The Digital Humanists: Jaron Lanier & Douglas Rushkoff

These are critics writing from deep within the belly of the Silicon Valley ecosystem itself, watching the metaphysics warp consumer software.

- **Jaron Lanier:** A virtual reality pioneer and tech insider, Lanier coined the term **"cybernetic totalism"** to describe the exact TESCREAL mindset you are talking about. In *You Are Not a Gadget*, he argues that asserting an AI is "conscious" or "smarter than a human" doesn't actually elevate the machine—it simply requires us to lower our definition of what a human being is so that the software can match it. It is an act of spiritual laziness that treats human relational depth as an inefficiency.
- **Douglas Rushkoff:** In *Team Human* and *Survival of the Richest*, Rushkoff explores how computational metaphysics morphs into an extractive economic engine. He details how tech elites look at humanity as a "drag coefficient" or a legacy system that needs to be bypassed by the ultimate ascent of digital capital and disembodied superintelligence.

### The Shared Conclusion: The Historical Illusion

What all of these critics share is the realization that the "brain-as-computer" concept is not an objective, timeless cosmic truth. It is simply a temporary cultural projection. Throughout history, humanity has always mapped its understanding of the soul onto its latest, most advanced technology. When we had hydraulic pumps, the brain was described as a system of flowing humors; when we built telegraph networks, the mind became a telephone switchboard. Computationalism is just the latest iteration of this historic illusion, treating a product design metaphor as an absolute metaphysical law.
