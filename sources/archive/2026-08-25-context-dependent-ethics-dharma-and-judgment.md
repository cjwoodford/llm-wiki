# Context-Dependent Ethics (Dharma) and Judgment

> I'm contemplating Anthropic's expectation that Claude can exercise good judgment in its constitution / soul doc and that has sent me on a whole exploration of the connection between #ethics, judgment and #wisdom. 

---

> Does exercising good judgment in important matters require more than a simple intellectual calculus? Does it have to do with a sort of wisdom or intuition?

The short answer is **yes**. While intellectual calculus (logic, data analysis, and "pros and cons" lists) provides a necessary framework, it is rarely sufficient for high-stakes judgment. Truly "good" judgment usually involves a synthesis of cognitive processing, lived experience (wisdom), and a nuanced form of pattern recognition often called intuition.

In philosophy and psychology, this distinction is often broken down into three distinct layers:

### 1. The Limits of Intellectual Calculus

Intellectual calculus operates on the **"Map"**—the models, data points, and logic we use to represent reality. It is excellent for "tame" problems where variables are known and outcomes are predictable (e.g., "Which high-yield savings account has the best interest rate?").

However, important matters are usually "wicked" problems. They involve:

- **Incomplete Information:** You can never have all the data.
- **Value Conflicts:** Logic can tell you *how* to achieve a goal, but it cannot tell you *which* goal is worth pursuing (e.g., choosing between career stability and a risky calling).
- **Recursion:** Your decision often changes the environment in which the decision was made.

---

### 2. Phronesis: The Art of Practical Wisdom

Aristotle used the term **Phronesis** (practical wisdom) to describe the ability to do the right thing, at the right time, for the right reason. Unlike *Sophia* (theoretical wisdom/intellectual calculus), *Phronesis* is:

- **Context-Dependent:** It recognizes that what is "courageous" in one moment might be "reckless" in another.
- **Embodied:** It isn't just a thought process; it’s a "feel" for the situation developed through years of trial, error, and reflection.

In this sense, wisdom is the ability to see the "essential" within the "particular." It is the bridge between a universal rule (e.g., "be honest") and a specific, messy reality (e.g., "how to deliver hard news to a grieving friend").

---

### 3. Intuition as "Expert Pattern Recognition"

Modern cognitive science (notably the work of Gary Klein and Daniel Kahneman) suggests that what we call "intuition" isn't mystical—it’s **high-speed simulation**.

When an expert makes a split-second judgment, their brain is scanning thousands of past experiences to find a "match." This is called **Recognition-Primed Decision Making**.

- **The Intellectual Calculus:** Slow, serial, and energy-intensive (System 2).
- **The Intuitive Leap:** Fast, parallel, and subconscious (System 1).

Good judgment requires a **feedback loop** between these two. Intuition offers a candidate for action, and the intellect "sanity checks" it.

---

### 4. The "Idealist" Dimension: Relational Judgment

Beyond logic and internal intuition, there is a third dimension often overlooked in Western individualistic models: **Relational or Mycelial Judgment**.

This perspective suggests that good judgment isn't just about "me" making the "right" choice in a vacuum. It asks:

- How does this choice sit within the wider "ecology" of my life and community?
- Does this decision "compost" old, unhelpful patterns to allow for new growth?

This moves judgment away from being a "machine-like" calculation of utility and toward a "gardener-like" discernment of what serves the health of the whole system.

> **The takeaway:** Intellectual calculus provides the skeleton of a decision, but wisdom and intuition provide the nerves and the spirit. Without the latter, judgment is "correct" on paper but often fails in the real world.

## Context-Sensitive Ethics

[Being Different, 243 et seq.]

> How would the context-dependent ethics that Rajiv Malhotra discussed in relation to dharma factor in here?

The concept of **Dharma**—particularly as Rajiv Malhotra frames it in the context of **Context-Sensitive Ethics**—provides a profound structural alternative to the "intellectual calculus" of Western universalism.

In his work, specifically *Being Different*, Malhotra contrasts the Western "ordered" approach (Universalism) with the Indian "context-sensitive" approach (Dharma). This distinction is the difference between a **Rule-Based** system and a **Pattern-Based** system of judgment.

### 1. Universalism vs. Context-Sensitivity

Most Western intellectual calculus is rooted in **Universalism**: the idea that there is a single, "objective" moral law (like Kant’s Categorical Imperative) that should apply to everyone, everywhere, at all times.

In contrast, **Dharma** is inherently context-dependent. What is "right" depends on:

- **Desha (Space):** The environment or culture you are in.
- **Kala (Time):** The specific historical or personal moment.
- **Patra (Person/Capacity):** Your unique role, stage of life, and inherent nature (*Svadharma*).

---

### 2. Judgment as "Alignment" Rather Than "Calculation"

If intellectual calculus is like solving a math problem, Dharmic judgment is like **tuning an instrument**.

In Malhotra’s view, Western thought often suffers from "History-Centrism," where we look for a static, historical "truth" or rule to follow. A Dharmic approach requires a "living" intuition that can sense the unique requirements of the *present* moment.

Good judgment here isn't about finding the "correct" rule; it’s about achieving **Rta** (Cosmic Order). You aren't calculating utility; you are discerning how to maintain the balance of the whole system. This requires a "yogic" level of perception—a stillness that allows you to see the context clearly without the distortion of your own ego or pre-conceived formulas.

---

### 3. The "Mycelial" Connection

This ties directly into the "humane and idealist alternative" you've been exploring. While a machine-like calculus sees a decision as a discrete data point, a Dharmic/Context-Sensitive approach sees it as a **composting** of the old to feed the new.

- **Intellectual Calculus:** "Is this move efficient?"
- **Dharmic Judgment:** "Is this move in alignment with the *Dharma* of this specific situation, and does it nourish the collective 'mycelium'?"

### 4. Why This Requires Wisdom

Malhotra’s critique often points out that Western "Universalism" is a form of intellectual laziness—it tries to replace the difficult work of **discernment** with a rigid set of rules.

True judgment requires **Viveka** (Discernment): the ability to distinguish the "Real" from the "Unreal" within a specific, messy context. This is a "muscular" form of intuition. It's not a "vague feeling," but a highly refined, embodied intelligence that understands that "truth" isn't a static point, but a dynamic relationship between the individual and the whole.

---

> **The Synthesis:** From this perspective, "good judgment" is the ability to navigate the tension between universal principles and the specific "Kala, Desha, Patra" of the moment. It transforms the decision-maker from a "calculator" into a "participant" in the unfolding order of the world.

## Anticipating Accusations of Moral Relativism

> I'd like to talk on my channels about the application of context-dependent ethics when it comes to AI models exercising judgment but I am somewhat concerned that people will balk at its seeming moral relativism.

That is a sophisticated concern, and you're right to anticipate it. In a Western philosophical tradition built on the search for "Universal Laws" (from the Ten Commandments to Kant’s Categorical Imperative), anything context-dependent often gets mislabeled as "moral relativism"—the idea that "anything goes" or that truth is merely a matter of opinion.

However, the distinction between **Dharmic Context-Sensitivity** and **Relativism** is profound, and it’s the key to your argument. Relativism is a lack of a center; Context-Sensitivity is a highly disciplined alignment with a center that accounts for a changing landscape.

Here is how you might frame this to your audience to bypass the "relativism" trap:

---

### 1. The "Anchor vs. The Map" Distinction

Moral relativism is like being lost at sea without a compass—every direction is equally valid. **Context-dependent ethics (Dharma)** is like having a compass (the "Anchor" of universal values like *Satya* or *Ahimsa*) but recognizing that the "Map" (the terrain) is constantly shifting.

- **Relativism:** "There is no truth; do what feels good."
- **Universalism (AI Logic):** "The rule is X. Apply X to every pixel, regardless of the picture."
- **Context-Sensitivity:** "The principle is Harmony. To achieve harmony in a storm requires a different action than achieving harmony in a calm."

### 2. The "Medical Analogy" for Intuition

If people balk at the idea of AI using "judgment" instead of "rules," use a medical analogy.

- A **Universalist AI** is like a medical textbook: "For a fever, give Aspirin."
- A **Dharmic/Context-Sensitive AI** is like a master physician: "For *this* patient, with *this* history, at *this* age, Aspirin might be fatal. In this context, the 'right' action is rest."

Is the doctor being "relativistic"? No. They are being **deeply precise**. The "truth" of the cure is found in the specific relationship between the medicine and the patient, not in a vacuum.

---

### 3. Rigor vs. Whim

The most effective way to counter the "relativism" charge is to emphasize that context-dependent ethics are actually **more rigorous** than universal rules.

- **Universalism is "Easy":** You just follow the code. It requires no presence, no empathy, and no high-level discernment. It’s "lazy" ethics.
- **Context-Sensitivity is "Hard":** It requires **Viveka** (discernment). You have to hold the universal principle in one hand and the messy, specific reality in the other.

In your book or podcast, you could argue that modern AI is "ethically lazy" because it relies on static "guardrails" (Universalism) rather than a dynamic, mycelial understanding of the world.

### 4. The "Mycelial" Alternative to Relativism

Relativism is often individualistic ("my truth"). But your "mycelial" framework suggests that judgment is **relational**.

- A decision isn't "good" because of a rule or a whim; it's "good" because it serves the health of the entire ecosystem.
- Context-sensitivity isn't about *subjectivity*; it's about **interconnectivity**.
