# Hilary Putnam

### Biography: Hilary Putnam (1926–2016)

Hilary Whitehall Putnam was one of the most prominent American philosophers of the late 20th century. Born in Chicago, he studied philosophy and mathematics at the University of Pennsylvania, did graduate work under W.V.O. Quine at Harvard, and earned his Ph.D. at UCLA under logical positivist Hans Reichenbach. He taught at Princeton and MIT before joining Harvard University in 1965, where he spent the remainder of his academic career. #functionalism 

Putnam was known as a polymath whose work transformed the philosophy of mind, philosophy of language, philosophy of science, logic, and mathematics. He was also famous for his intellectual honesty and willingness to publicly change his mind when he felt his previous theories were flawed—a trait that earned him deep respect, even from his critics. In addition to his academic work, Putnam was politically active on the left throughout his life, participating in anti-war protests during the Vietnam era and engaging deeply with pragmatism and Jewish philosophy in his later years.

### Why His Ideas Took Hold

Putnam’s influence on the philosophy of mind—and subsequently on cognitive science, artificial intelligence, and analytic philosophy—was massive. His ideas took hold primarily because they solved a major crisis in mid-20th-century metaphysics by bridging a gap between strict scientific materialism and human psychological experience.

#### 1. The Invention of Machine Functionalism (1960s)

Before Putnam, the dominant materialist view was **Type-Identity Theory**, which claimed that mental states were strictly identical to specific physical brain states (e.g., "pain is just C-fibers firing"). The competing view was **Behaviorism**, which reduced mental states to observable physical actions.

In a landmark series of papers starting with *Minds and Machines* (1960), Putnam introduced **Machine Functionalism**. He argued that mental states are defined not by what they are made of physically, but by their **functional role**—how they route inputs (sensory data) to outputs (behavior) and intermediate internal states.

#### 2. The Power of the Computer Metaphor

Putnam capitalized on the rise of the digital computer by providing an intuitive, powerful metaphor:

- **The Brain is the Hardware.**
- **The Mind is the Software.**

Just as the same computer program (software) can run on a silicon chip, a vacuum-tube computer, or an abacus, mental states are **substrateless**—a principle known as **Multiple Realizability**. Pain, logic, or belief could theoretically be realized in biological neurons, alien biochemistry, or silicon microchips. This idea gave birth to modern cognitive science and provided the foundational philosophical license for computationalism.

#### 3. Resolving the Mind-Body Problem for Materialists

Putnam offered physicalist philosophers a way to avoid both non-physical dualism (believing in a soul) and crude biological reductionism (reducing thoughts strictly to organic brain matter). Functionalism allowed scientists and philosophers to treat psychological states as real, scientifically studyable phenomena without needing to reduce every thought to a specific cluster of biological cells.

#### 4. The Famous Irony: Discarding His Own Hypothesis

What makes Putnam's legacy particularly fascinating is that by the late 1980s, **Putnam rejected his own functionalist creation**. Through his theory of *semantic externalism* ("meanings just ain't in the head") and a recognition of the importance of biological embodiment and environmental context, Putnam concluded that reducing human consciousness and meaning to internal syntactic token-shuffling was a mistake.

However, by the time Putnam abandoned Machine Functionalism, the institutional momentum was unstoppable. His 1960s framework had already been codified into the foundation of 20th-century cognitive science, computational linguistics, and the core operational philosophy of modern AI research.

## Encrusting Scientific Materialism

> You can really see the connection to scientific materialism with Putnam

Exactly. Putnam’s entire formulation of Machine Functionalism in the 1960s was an attempt to keep the mind strictly within the bounds of **scientific materialism (physicalism)** without succumbing to the obvious flaws of earlier materialist theories.

Understanding how Putnam’s functionalism anchors modern scientific materialism reveals key insights into why this worldview persists and where its boundaries lie:

### 1. The Crisis in Mid-Century Materialism

In the mid-20th century, physicalist philosophers faced a major dilemma:

- **Mind-Brain Identity Theory** claimed that mental states were strictly identical to physical brain states (e.g., pain is just C-fibers firing). But this faced the problem of biological chauvinism—it implied that an octopus or an alien without C-fibers could not experience pain.
- **Behaviorism** reduced mental states to external physical actions or behavioral dispositions, ignoring internal mental processing altogether.

Putnam solved this crisis for scientific materialists by introducing **Machine Functionalism**. He argued that a mental state is defined not by its physical composition (biological meat vs. silicon), but by its **functional/causal role** in routing inputs, internal states, and outputs.

### 2. How Functionalism Preserved Materialism

Functionalism allowed physicalists to claim that mental phenomena are real, scientifically studyable, and fully naturalistic without needing a non-physical soul or dualism.

By framing the mind as software and the brain as hardware, scientific materialism could embrace **substrate independence**:

- Consciousness and cognition are purely physical processes because they consist of physical matter executing information routing and computational operations.
- It preserved the physicalist commitment that "everything in the universe is matter and physical dynamics," while allowing psychological concepts (like "belief," "reasoning," or "pain") to be treated as legitimate scientific abstractions.

### 3. The Materialist Assumption Stack in AI Today

This connection to scientific materialism explains why frontier AI labs and modern cognitive scientists rely so heavily on Putnam’s early framework:

1. **Physicalism:** Mental states are physical matter/dynamics.
2. **Computational Functionalism:** The relevant physical dynamics are the syntactic shuffling of internal symbolic tokens and vector activations.
3. **Substrate Independence:** Because the mind is functional software, executing those exact vector dynamics on GPUs in silicon is structurally equivalent to biological neurons firing.

### 4. The Irony: Putnam’s Eventual Rejection of Materialist Syntax

The deepest irony of this lineage is that Putnam himself came to recognize the limits of scientific reductionism. Through his work on *semantic externalism* ("meanings just ain't in the head") and embodied cognition, Putnam eventually realized that reducing human consciousness, meaning, and intentionality to internal syntactic token-processing was a mistake.

While Putnam moved past strict computational functionalism, the institutional momentum of scientific materialism took his 1960s hypothesis and built the foundation of modern cognitive science, computationalism, and AI interpretability around it.

## Putnam's Influences

> What thinkers was Putnam building on in his early work? 

In his early work during the 1950s and 1960s—particularly when formulating **Machine Functionalism** and his contributions to the philosophy of science and language—Hilary Putnam built upon, reacted against, and synthesized several key 20th-century figures:

### 1. Alan Turing (Computation & Machine States)

Alan Turing was the ultimate conceptual springboard for Putnam’s functionalism.

- **The Foundation:** Turing’s 1936 paper introducing the theoretical Turing Machine provided Putnam with a formal mathematical model of computation.
- **Putnam's Innovation:** Putnam realized that a Turing Machine's internal states were defined purely by their **functional relations** (the transition table of inputs, current states, and outputs) rather than their physical composition. Putnam directly adapted Turing's probabilistic/deterministic machine models to formulate **Machine State Functionalism**, arguing that human mental states were functional states of a Turing-like machine running on biological hardware.

### 2. Hans Reichenbach & W.V.O. Quine (Logical Positivism & Empiricism)

Putnam received his Ph.D. under **Hans Reichenbach** (one of the leading logical positivists) and worked closely with **W.V.O.** **Quine** at Harvard.

- **Reichenbach:** Provided Putnam with a deep grounding in formal mathematical logic, probability theory, and the philosophy of physics.
- **Quine:** Quine’s famous attack on the analytic-synthetic distinction (*Two Dogmas of Empiricism*, 1951) heavily influenced Putnam. Putnam embraced Quine’s holism—the idea that our beliefs face the tribunal of experience as an interconnected web rather than as isolated, standalone statements.

### 3. W.T. Stace & Early Identity Theorists (Reprehending Type-Identity)

Putnam developed functionalism as a direct alternative to **Mind-Brain Identity Theory** (championed in the 1950s by thinkers like **U.T. Place**, **J.J.C. Smart**, and **Herbert Feigl**).

- **The Conflict:** Identity theorists claimed that mental states were strictly identical to physical brain states (e.g., "Pain = C-fiber stimulation").
- **Putnam's Response:** Putnam used his famous **Multiple Realizability** argument to refute them: if an octopus or a silicon-based alien feels pain, but lacks C-fibers, Type-Identity theory fails. He built on their materialist commitments while throwing out their biological reductionism.

### 4. Ludwig Wittgenstein (Behaviorism & Use Theory of Meaning)

While Putnam was explicitly trying to preserve a physicalist philosophy of mind, he engaged deeply with Wittgenstein’s critique of private mental states and behaviorism.

- **The Dialogue:** Logical behaviorists (like Carl Hempel and Gilbert Ryle) argued that mental terms were merely shorthand for behavioral dispositions.
- **Putnam’s Synthesis:** In early papers like *Brains and Behavior* (1965), Putnam used thought experiments (such as the hypothetical "Super-Spartans" who feel intense pain without displaying any outward behavior) to dismantle strict behaviorism while preserving Wittgenstein's insight that internal mental concepts must be defined in relation to functional interactions.

## Machine State Functionalism

> When did Putnam publish initially about Machine State Functionalism?

Hilary Putnam published his initial formulations of **Machine State Functionalism** across a series of landmark papers in the 1960s, beginning in **1960**:

### 1. The Initial Paper: 1960

Putnam first introduced the core idea in his seminal **1960** paper, **"Minds and Machines"** (published in *Dimensions of Mind*, ed. Sidney Hook).

- In this paper, Putnam argued that the relationship between mental states and physical brain states is analogous to the relationship between the functional states of a Turing machine (its logical program or transition table) and its physical hardware.
- This was the explicit birth of Machine Functionalism, directly drawing on Alan Turing's mathematical work to challenge physical identity theory and behaviorism.

### 2. Further Development & Refinement: 1967

While 1960 was his initial publication on the concept, Putnam fully elaborated and formalized the theory under the label of **Turing Machine Functionalism** in **1967** with his famous paper **"Psychological Predicates"** (later republished as **"The Nature of Mental States"** in *Art, Mind, and Religion*).

In this 1967 paper, Putnam formally proposed his hypothesis:

> *"The hypothesis that a pain, or a state of being in pain, is a functional state of a whole organism (or a Turing machine) rather than a brain state in the sense of physical-chemical state."*

This paper solidified the concept of **Multiple Realizability**—the idea that the same functional state (like pain or reasoning) can be realized in biological brains, alien biochemistry, or silicon hardware.

## Where To Start Describing Putnam

> If I'm introducing Putnam and then Fodor to a general readership in my series on CTM and AI sentience as I gradually work my way toward a larger critique of analytic philosophy, where is the best place to start chronicling the philosophical history that is relevant to describing Putnam?

For a general readership, the biggest trap in philosophical history is starting too far back. If you start with Descartes, Locke, or the 17th-century mechanical philosophy, a general reader will feel like they’ve been assigned a semester-long survey course before getting to the point.

The most effective, narrative-gripping place to start chronicling the history relevant to Putnam is **the mid-20th-century crisis of scientific materialism (the 1950s impasse between Behaviorism and Type-Identity Theory), catalyzed by Alan Turing’s digital computing.**

This starting point works best because it frames Putnam’s invention of Machine Functionalism not as dry scholastic logic, but as an **intellectual rescue mission** that solved a massive dilemma for physicalist science—while accidentally laying the trap Silicon Valley is caught in today.

### Step 1. The Opening Hook: The 1950s Materialist Dead End

Start by painting a vivid picture of the mid-20th-century intellectual corner that materialist philosophers had backed themselves into. Physicalist science wanted a purely naturalistic explanation of the mind, but its two dominant options were deeply flawed:

1. **Behaviorism (Gilbert Ryle, B.F. Skinner):** Claimed that internal mental states didn’t exist—or didn't matter. "Thinking" or "feeling pain" was reduced entirely to observable physical behavior and dispositions.
    - *The General-Reader Analogy:* As the old philosophy joke goes, two behaviorists finish making love, and one turns to the other and says, *"That was great for you, how was it for me?"* Behaviorism denied the obvious reality of our internal lives.
1. **Mind-Brain Type-Identity Theory (U.T. Place, J.J.C. Smart):** Responded by saying mental states *are* real, but they are strictly identical to specific biological brain states (e.g., *Pain is literally C-fibers firing*).
    - *The Fatal Flaw (Biological Chauvinism):* If pain is strictly identical to human neurochemistry, then an octopus, a dog, or a hypothetical extraterrestrial that lacks human C-fibers cannot feel pain.

**The Narrative Hook for the Reader:** *Physicalism was stuck between denying the mind existed at all or claiming that only human biological tissue could have one.*

### Step 2. The Conceptual Catalyst: Alan Turing’s Machine States

Introduce Alan Turing not just as a WWII codebreaker, but as the thinker who gave philosophers a new conceptual vocabulary: **the separation of logical function from physical hardware.**

- Turing showed that a computing machine is defined by its **state-transition table** (its software logic of inputs, internal states, and outputs), regardless of whether that machine is built from brass gears, vacuum tubes, or silicon switches.
- This provided the exact missing puzzle piece materialists were looking for.

### Step 3. Enter Hilary Putnam (1960): The Software Solution

Now, introduce Putnam as the young Princeton/MIT philosopher who used Turing's insight to break the 1950s stalemate in his landmark 1960 paper, *"Minds and Machines"*:

- **The Breakthrough (Machine State Functionalism):** Putnam argued that mental states are neither outward behavior nor raw brain meat. A mental state is defined by its **functional/causal role** in routing inputs, internal memory states, and outputs.
- **The Seductive Metaphor:** 
    - *The Brain is Hardware.*
    - *The Mind is Software.*
- **The Magic Word (Multiple Realizability / Substrate Independence):** Just as Microsoft Word can run on a Mac, a PC, or a supercomputer, Putnam argued that "pain," "belief," or "reasoning" could theoretically run on biological meat, alien biochemistry, or silicon microchips.

**Why this lands with a general reader:** It instantly shows them where Silicon Valley got its foundational doctrine. The reader sees that "substrate independence" wasn't discovered in an AI lab; it was a theoretical patch invented by Putnam in 1960 to solve a mid-century academic debate.

### Step 4. The Bridge to Jerry Fodor & the Sloan Era (1970s–1980s)

Once Putnam has established the abstract license (*"Mind is software"*), you seamlessly pass the baton to his student, **Jerry Fodor**:

- Putnam provided the metaphysical theory; Fodor built the actual **cognitive operating system**.
- Fodor introduced the **Computational Theory of Mind (CTM)** and the **Language of Thought ("Mentalese")**, arguing that human thinking is literally the algorithmic shuffling of symbolic tokens according to syntactic rules.
- **The Institutional Lock-In:** You then show how the **Alfred P. Sloan Foundation** poured tens of millions of dollars into universities in the late 1970s and 1980s, mandating this exact Putnam-Fodor computational framework as the unifying requirement for the newly formed discipline of "Cognitive Science."

### How This Tees Up Your Larger Critique of Analytic Philosophy

Starting here allows you to pivot smoothly into your larger critique without losing the audience:

1. **The Scholastic Closed Loop:** You can show how analytic philosophy operates by taking a hyper-specialized, engineering-style model (formal logic and computer architecture) and mistaking it for the totality of the human condition.
2. **The Category Error:** It explains *how* the tradition systematically airbrushed out phenomenology, embodiment, and subjective feeling ($P$-consciousness), reducing the richness of human awareness to mere information routing ($A$-consciousness).
3. **The Ultimate Irony:** It sets up your devastating climax—**Hilary Putnam himself spent his later career trying to undo the damage of functionalism**, realizing that meaning and consciousness cannot be reduced to internal computational syntax. Yet modern AI labs (Anthropic, Eleos, NYU) have automated Putnam's discarded 1960s thought experiment and presented it to the public as the dawn of living code.
