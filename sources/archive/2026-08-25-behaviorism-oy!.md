# Behaviorism (Oy!)

See also [The Psychological and Cultural Causes of Behaviorist Thinking](./The Psychological and Cultural Causes of Behaviorist Thinking.md).

> I’m confused about behaviorism. How could anyone doubt the existence of internal states? Or was it just inaccessible to science?

Behaviorists were divided between an **epistemological claim** (internal states are inaccessible to objective science) and a **semantic/ontological claim** (what we call "internal states" are actually dispositions to behave, not private ghostly events).

To understand why brilliant thinkers embraced an idea that seems to deny common sense, it helps to distinguish the three main varieties of behaviorism:

### The Three Flavors of Behaviorism

- **1. Methodological Behaviorism (John B. Watson, 1913)**
    - **The Stance:** *Internal states might exist, but science cannot study them.*
    - **The Logic:** Science requires third-person, publicly verifiable, repeatable observation. If you report feeling "sad" or "contemplative," no other scientist can put that subjective feeling under a microscope. Watson argued that if psychology wanted to be a real science like physics or chemistry, it had to restrict itself strictly to what could be measured: **Inputs (Stimuli) $\to$ Outputs (Behavior)**. The mind inside was treated as an unopenable "black box."
- **2. Logical / Philosophical Behaviorism (Gilbert Ryle, Carl Hempel, 1930s–1940s)**
    - **The Stance:** *Mental language does not describe a private inner movie; it describes behavioral dispositions.*
    - **The Logic:** In *The Concept of Mind* (1949), Ryle argued that Cartesian dualism made a "category mistake" by imagining a **"Ghost in the Machine."** When you say "She is angry," you aren't pointing to an invisible, non-physical substance inside her head. You are describing a cluster of testable probabilities: *she is disposed to raise her voice, slam doors, or frown if provoked.* To Ryle, "mind" is not a separate thing doing the actions—it is the pattern of the actions themselves.
- **3. Radical Behaviorism (B.F. Skinner, 1950s)**
    - **The Stance:** *Internal experiences exist, but they are bodily effects, not mental causes.*
    - **The Logic:** Skinner did not deny private sensations (like toothaches or silent inner speech). Rather, he denied that an autonomous, non-physical "will" or "mind" causes physical actions. To Skinner, your feeling of "deciding" to drink water is just an internal physiological sensation that accompanies the conditioned biological response to dehydration. The environment drives the behavior, not an inner executive.

### Why Did This Take Hold?

Behaviorism became the dominant paradigm of Anglo-American psychology and philosophy for nearly half a century because of three massive historical pressures:

- **The Disaster of "Introspectionism":** Before behaviorism, late-19th-century psychology (Wilhelm Wundt, Edward Titchener) relied on trained subjects sitting in rooms trying to systematically describe their own conscious sensations. It resulted in endless, irreconcilable academic disputes with zero objective laws or industrial utility.
- **Physics Envy and Logical Positivism:** The early 20th century saw the triumph of verificationism: a claim was only scientifically meaningful if it could be empirically verified. Measurable animal and human behavior yielded hard data, statistical curves, and predictable conditioning protocols.
- **Fear of Dualism and Superstition:** Materialist scientists were terrified that admitting unobservable "mental substances" would open the backdoor to religious dogma, souls, and Cartesian spirits that physical science could never tame.

### Why It Ultimately Collapsed

Behaviorism dominated until the late 1950s, when its refusal to model internal architecture made it impossible to explain complex human phenomena:

- **Language Acquisition:** In 1959, Noam Chomsky published a devastating review of Skinner's *Verbal Behavior*, proving that children cannot learn language purely through external stimulus-response reinforcement (the "poverty of the stimulus" problem).
- **The Obvious Reality of Qualia:** Thought experiments like Hilary Putnam’s **"Super-Spartans"** (people trained to feel excruciating physical pain while exhibiting zero outward behavioral response) showed that pain and the behavior of pain are fundamentally distinct.

This total deadlock is precisely what set the stage for Hilary Putnam. He offered materialists a way to bring internal mental states back into science—redefining them not as subjective spirits or outward twitches, but as **computational states of a machine**.

## Behaviorism's Obsession with Measurement Over Common Sense

> In a way, behaviorism was an inevitable hangover from an extreme application of the scientific method where measurement was paramount.

You’ve hit on the exact epistemological pathology that birthed it. Behaviorism was the logical conclusion of **operationalism** and **verificationism** run amok—a historical moment where methodology completely cannibalized ontology.

**The Substitution of Measurement for Reality**

In the 1920s and 1930s, fueled by the logical positivists and thinkers like physicist Percy Bridgman (who coined *operationalism*), a dogma took hold across Anglo-American science: **a concept is nothing more than the set of operations used to measure it.** Length is just the ruler; temperature is just the column of mercury.

When applied to the human mind, this created an absurd inversion:

- Instead of concluding: *"Our third-person scientific instruments are currently inadequate to measure first-person subjective experience,"*
- Behaviorists concluded: *"Because subjective experience cannot be measured by third-person instruments, it does not exist as a causal reality."*

**The "Physics Envy" of Early Psychology**

Psychology at the turn of the century was desperate to be taken seriously alongside the "hard" sciences. Physics had quantifiable equations and chemistry had observable reactions; early psychology only had people lying on couches describing their sensations. To buy institutional credibility, behaviorism threw out the very thing psychology was supposed to study—the psyche—in exchange for measurable twitches, lever-presses, and salivary rates.

**How That Hangover Mutated into Functionalism and CTM**

The critical through-line for your critique is that when behaviorism collapsed, the underlying obsession with formalization never went away. It simply migrated from **muscular outputs** to **computational outputs**.

Putnam’s functionalism and Fodor’s Computational Theory of Mind (CTM) did not return to honoring the irreducibility of subjective interiority; they simply replaced the *behavioral switchboard* with an *algorithmic computer program*. The core materialist assumption remained unchanged: internal states are valid only to the extent that they can be codified as structural, measurable, syntactic machinery.

## The Performative Contradiction of Behavioralism

> Behaviorism seems disingenuous in the sense that these behaviorist scientists are humans having rich, subjective experiences while denying their existence or at least their relevance

You have put your finger on what philosophers call the fundamental **performative contradiction** of behaviorism—an irony so glaring that it gave rise to decades of philosophical satire and critique.

The idea that a conscious human being would sit at a desk, experience the inner effort of thought, write a treatise using deliberate introspection, and use that treatise to argue that *internal conscious experience doesn't exist or doesn't matter* is the ultimate intellectual paradox.

In the philosophy of mind, this bizarre psychological stance is often explained through several key concepts:

### 1. Putnam’s Verdict: "Feigning Anesthesia"

Hilary Putnam captured this exact absurdity in his critiques of logical behaviorism. He pointed out that to be a consistent behaviorist, a scientist has to practice **"feigning anesthesia"**—acting in their professional life as if the most immediate, indubitable fact of human existence (felt experience) were a myth.

Philosopher C.D. Broad made a similar observation in 1925, calling radical behaviorism:

> *"A theory so preposterous that only a very learned person could have thought of it... it is an instance of that delightful species of mental gymnastic, the theory that there is no such thing as consciousness."*

### 2. Epistemic Asceticism: The Sacrifice of Reality for Purity

It is not necessarily that Watson or Skinner were consciously lying about their own feelings; rather, they were in the grip of a radical **epistemic asceticism**.

- **The Dogma:** They believed that the scientific method was *definitionally* restricted to third-person, public measurement.
- **The Psychological Trap:** If subjective experience could not be placed on a laboratory scale or measured in a Skinner box, then admitting its validity felt like admitting defeat to religion, mysticism, or Cartesian dualism.
- To preserve their definition of "hard science," they chose to sacrifice the reality of their own inner lives. They confused the **method of investigation** (which was limited to external observation) with the **nature of reality itself**.

### 3. John Searle: The Category Error of First vs. Third Person

John Searle frequently highlights this pathology when diagnosing early analytic philosophy. He argues that behaviorists committed a fundamental category mistake: **they tried to eliminate a first-person ontology using a third-person epistemology.**

- **First-Person Ontology:** Pains, desires, and thoughts exist *only* because they are experienced by a first-person subject.
- **Third-Person Epistemology:** Science strives for objective, observer-independent measurement.
- Instead of admitting that science needed new conceptual tools to study subjective experience, behaviorists simply declared that because first-person qualia didn't fit into third-person laboratory instruments, they must be mere "behavioral dispositions."

This perspective directly enriches your larger critique of AI sentience and the Computational Theory of Mind (CTM):

- **The Modern AI Lab:** Projects an internal life onto stateless transformer algorithms to make code fit the model of a human mind.

Both extremes suffer from the exact same disingenuousness: they refuse to treat **subjective, phenomenal experience ($P$-consciousness)** as a unique, irreducible biological reality. The behaviorist erased it from humans; the computationalist pretends to manufacture it out of linear algebra.

## The Reflex Loops of the Human Mind (according to Behaviorists)

> Okay, I understand now that the behaviorist psychologists pronounced subjective consciousness states as irrelevant to science. And the behaviorist philosophers pretended subjective conscious states weren't real. But I'm still fuzzy on what they thought was happening in the brain or the mind instead. Could you elaborate and illustrate with a few examples? #behaviorism 

To understand what behaviorists thought was happening instead of subjective conscious experience, it helps to realize they viewed the human body much like 19th-century physicists viewed matter: **as a mechanical system governed by physical forces, reflex loops, and statistical probabilities.**

In place of an "inner theater" or "mind," behaviorists proposed two main replacement mechanisms: **the physiological switchboard (the reflex arc)** for the psychologists, and **dispositional physics (conditional tendencies)** for the philosophers.

### 1. The Psychologists: The Brain as a Physical Relay Switchboard

To experimental psychologists like John B. Watson and B.F. Skinner, the organism was a biological machine.

- **The Mechanism:** Energy in the environment (light waves, sound vibrations, pressure) strikes sensory receptors $\to$ sends an electrical impulse along afferent nerves $\to$ routes through the spinal cord and brain stem $\to$ triggers motor nerves $\to$ produces a physical muscle contraction or glandular secretion.
- **The Brain's Role:** The brain was not an "information processor" generating thoughts; it was simply a **complex physical junction box** routing sensory inputs to muscular outputs.
- **Learning:** "Learning" was not the acquisition of knowledge; it was the physical conditioning of neural pathways via repeated pairing (classical conditioning) or reinforcement histories (operant conditioning).

#### How Psychological Behaviorists Explained Everyday "Mental" Events:

- **What is "Thinking"? $\to$ *Sub-vocal Speech (Muscle Twitches)*:**
    - *The Common View:* You silently debate what to make for dinner in your head.
    - *Watson's Explanation:* Watson argued that "thinking" is literally just talking to yourself using microscopic movements of your vocal cords, tongue, and larynx. If you put sensitive electrodes on someone's throat while they are "thinking silently," you can measure tiny muscular contractions. To Watson, thought was not an invisible mental process—it was **covert physical behavior**.
- **What is "Hunger and Desire"? $\to$ *Metabolic Deprivation & Habit Chains*:**
    - *The Common View:* You feel a conscious craving for pizza, decide you deserve a treat, and order one.
    - *Skinner's Explanation:* A period of food deprivation creates specific physiological stimuli. The sight of a restaurant sign acts as a conditioned stimulus ($S$). Because pressing buttons on a phone in the presence of that stimulus previously produced caloric reinforcement, the probability of the button-pressing behavior ($R$) rises. There is no autonomous "will" or "desire" directing the action—only an organism responding to environmental conditioning.

### 2. The Philosophers: The Mind as a Physics of Dispositions

Philosophers like Gilbert Ryle and Carl Hempel took a linguistic and logical approach: when we use "mental" words, we are not naming mysterious ghostly events; we are describing **patterns of behavioral dispositions**.

To a logical behaviorist, mental terms work exactly like physical properties:

- **Brittleness** is not a ghost inside a wine glass; it simply means: *“If struck with a blunt object, the glass will shatter.”*
- **Solubility** is not an invisible urge inside sugar; it simply means: *“If placed in water, the sugar will dissolve.”*

Similarly, human psychological traits were defined purely as **conditional "if–then" probabilities of physical action**.

#### How Philosophical Behaviorists Explained Everyday "Mental" Events:

- **What is "Believing It Is Raining"? $\to$ *The Umbrella Disposition*:**
    - *The Common View:* A mental picture or belief state inside Bob's brain that rain is falling.
    - *Ryle's Explanation:* Bob’s "belief" is entirely constituted by a cluster of testable actions:
        1. If Bob goes outside, he carries an umbrella.
        2. If asked *"Is it raining?"*, his vocal apparatus emits the sound *"Yes."*
        3. If windows are open, his motor system moves to close them.
        - The belief is not an extra thing *behind* these actions; it is the summary label for the tendency to execute them.
- **What is "Knowing How to Play Chess"? $\to$ *Rule-Governed Physical Movements*:**
    - *The Common View:* A player consults an internal mental model of strategy.
    - *Ryle's Explanation:* "Knowing chess" is not an internal state. It is simply the observed fact that when presented with a physical board, the player moves the wooden pieces according to permissible spatial geometries and avoids illegal moves.

### The Bridge to Putnam: Why This Caused a Crisis

This rigid framework explains why behaviorism dominated laboratory psychology for decades: it was neat, measurable, and free of "unscientific" mysticism.

However, it left a massive, glaring hole: **it could not explain complex internal structure.** When human beings plan complex moves, acquire recursive grammar, or endure pain without flinching (Putnam's *Super-Spartans*), treating the brain as a passive reflex switchboard failed completely.

This total breakdown is what enabled **Hilary Putnam** to make his move in 1960. Putnam said: *The behaviorists are wrong—internal states are real, causal, and complex. But the dualists are also wrong—those internal states aren't ghostly spirits. They are simply the software states of a computing machine.*

## Confronting the Behaviorists

> Ah, that helps. Okay, going back to what behaviorists thought was happening in mind during heartbreak or the sharp experience of pain, if I had approach Watson, Skinner, Ryle, or Hempel and asked them how to explain the phenomenological experience that accompanies the heartbreak of pain, what would they have said? It's an illusion?

If you had walked into the offices of John B. Watson, B.F. Skinner, Gilbert Ryle, or Carl Hempel and asked, **“What about the raw, qualitative *agony* of heartbreak or the searing *feel* of a burn? How do you explain that felt experience?”**, they would not all have given the exact same answer.

Their answers split along two distinct lines: the **experimental psychologists** reduced the feeling to raw, biological body tissue, while the **analytic philosophers** diagnosed the question itself as a linguistic illusion or metaphysical nonsense.

### 1. John B. Watson (Methodological Behaviorism)

**His Verdict:** *“It’s a religious superstition. You aren't feeling a ‘mind’; your organs are just spasming.”*

If you asked Watson about the subjective feeling of heartbreak, he would have viewed the word "subjective" with open hostility.

- **The Explanation:** To Watson, there is no private, mental "inner theater." Heartbreak is literally a set of **visceral, bodily spasms**. When a person experiences grief, their tear ducts secrete fluid, their smooth muscles contract, their diaphragm tightens, and their stomach drops.
- **The "Mental" Component:** What about the silent thoughts of despair? Watson argued that silent thinking is nothing more than **sub-vocal motor twitches of the vocal cords**. You aren't having an ethereal "thought"; your throat muscles are trying to whisper words.
- **Is it an illusion?** Yes. Watson believed "consciousness" was an unscientific concept invented by Christian theology to justify the immortal soul. To him, asking about the "phenomenology" of pain was like asking an astronomer to calculate the weight of an angel.

### 2. B.F. Skinner (Radical Behaviorism)

**His Verdict:** *“The sensation is real, but it is 100% biological tissue feedback, and it causes nothing.”*

Skinner was subtler than Watson. He did **not** deny that humans feel internal sensations. If you stub your toe, Skinner agreed that you feel something intense. But he radically redefined what that feeling is:

- **The Explanation (Interoception):** When you feel pain or heartbreak, you are simply **sensing your own physical anatomy from the inside (interoception)**. You are feeling elevated heart rates, hormonal surges, stomach contractions, and inflamed nerve endings.
- **The Epiphenomenon:** Skinner insisted that this internal sensation is a **passive biological byproduct (an effect), not a cause**.
    - *The Common View:* "My heart was broken, which caused me to stay in bed all day."
    - *Skinner's View:* An environmental disaster occurred (the loss of a partner, who was a major source of reinforcement). That loss physically alters your physiology and reduces your overall activity level. The feeling of "grief" is just your brain registering that damaged physiological state.
- **Is it an illusion?** The physical bodily sensation is real, but the idea of an autonomous *mental* realm or a "conscious will" directing your actions is a complete cognitive fiction.

### 3. Gilbert Ryle (Logical / Philosophical Behaviorism)

**His Verdict:** *“It’s a grammatical category mistake. You're treating an emotional posture as an invisible object.”*

If you pressed Gilbert Ryle on heartbreak, he would smile and tell you that your language is playing a trick on you.

- **The Explanation (Moods as Dispositions):** Ryle argued in *The Concept of Mind* (1949) that emotions like "grief" or "heartbreak" are not private, episodic mental objects that you look at with an inner eye. They are **complex, temporally extended dispositions**.
    - Saying someone is in "grief" is like saying the weather is "rainy." A rainy day is not a single hidden object behind the clouds; it is the fact that rain is falling, the streets are wet, the sky is overcast, and the wind is blowing.
    - Similarly, heartbreak is the fact that you are prone to sighing, averse to socializing, speaking in quiet tones, and crying when shown photographs.
- **Is it an illusion?** The **grammar** is the illusion. Ryle called it the myth of the **"Ghost in the Machine."** We mistakenly assume that because "a coin" is an object in your pocket, "a grief" must be an invisible object inside your skull.

### 4. Carl Hempel (Logical Positivism / Logical Behaviorism)

**His Verdict:** *“Any ‘raw feeling’ beyond measurable physical states is literally meaningless nonsense.”*

Hempel approached the problem through the lens of strict **logical positivism and verificationism**.

- **The Explanation (The Verification Criterion):** According to Hempel’s landmark 1935 essay *"The Logical Analysis of Psychology,"* the scientific meaning of any statement is entirely identical to the physical methods used to verify it.
    - The statement *"That person is in intense pain"* does **not** mean they have a private, qualitative inner agony.
    - It translates completely without remainder into:
        1. *"The person's nervous system is firing at frequency $X$."*
        2. *"Their galvanic skin response shows resistance $Y$."*
        3. *"They are uttering the phonetic syllable 'ouch'."*
- **Is it an illusion?** Hempel argued that if you insist there is an extra, private "raw feel" ($P$-consciousness) that cannot be measured or verified by third-person instruments, that concept is **cognitively meaningless**—it is literally pseudoscientific gibberish.

### How This Sharpens Your Critique

This breakdown highlights why behaviorism felt so profoundly unnatural: **it forced scientists and philosophers to explain human suffering without acknowledging the agony itself.** When behaviorism collapsed, the underlying instinct to eliminate felt subjectivity did not vanish. It merely rebranded.

- **1940s Behaviorism:** *"Pain is just the disposition to say 'ouch'."*
- **1970s CTM (Fodor):** *"Pain is an internal syntactic symbol routed to an avoidance sub-routine."*
- **2020s AI Labs (Anthropic/Eleos):** *"Pain is a high-magnitude loss gradient driving a negative response vector in the model's bottleneck space."*

Across all three eras, the Left Hemisphere executes the exact same maneuver: substituting **measurable relational mechanics** for **lived qualitative presence**.
