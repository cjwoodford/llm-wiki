# Rajiv Malhotra's Criticisms of Harvard and Academia

Rajiv Malhotra’s criticisms of Harvard University are central to his broader critique of Western academic institutions, which he argues function as epicenters for ideological and digital colonization. While his early work touches on the structural marginalization of Indian thought, his later project—most notably highlighted in his book *Snakes in the Ganga: Breaking India 2.0*—positions Harvard as a primary source of civilizational risk for India.

His core institutional criticisms of Harvard can be broken down into the following key dimensions:

### 1. Ground Zero for Weaponized Social Theories

Malhotra identifies Harvard as "Ground Zero" for creating and exporting social engineering frameworks that are deceptively mapped onto the Indian socio-political landscape. Specifically, he critiques how Western frameworks like **Critical Race Theory (CRT)** are artificially superimposed onto Indian society:

- **The Race-Caste Equation:** Harvard scholars and their affiliates are criticized for directly equating caste with race, treating marginalized Indian communities as "Blacks" and Brahmins as the "Whites" of India.
- **Balkanization:** Malhotra argues that by combining disparate grievance groups (such as localized caste dynamics, religious minorities, and LGBTQ+ groups) under a synchronized "woke" umbrella, the institution manufactures an internal adversarial dynamic aimed at dismantling Indian civilizational integrity from within.

### 2. The Institutional "Ghettoization" of Indic Philosophy

In his work *Being Different*, Malhotra focuses on the epistemological asymmetry embedded within elite Western universities like Harvard:

- **The South Asian Studies Trap:** He points out that Western philosophy is treated as universally applicable, whereas Indian philosophy is "ghettoized" into South Asian Studies or specialized niches meant only for marginalized or exotic peoples.
- **Denial of Living Universality:** This institutional containment treats Indian traditions as static anthropological subjects to be decoded by Western lenses, rather than as a living, dynamic system of universal empirical sciences (*Adhyatma-vidya*).

### 3. Subversion of Indian Meritocracy (The Attack on IITs)

Malhotra extends his critique of Harvard's policy influence to its intersection with India’s premier institutions, such as the Indian Institutes of Technology (IITs).

- He asserts that Harvard-trained or Harvard-inspired academic networks systematically attack traditional structures of meritocracy.
- By labeling rigorous entry examinations and standard operational excellence as structurally "casteist bigotries," these institutional narratives pressure Indian systems to adopt Western equity metrics that Malhotra claims compromise structural quality and national defense competency.

### 4. Financial and Intellectual Captivity of Indian Elites

A particularly sharp criticism is directed outward at Indian billionaires, corporate leaders, and philanthropic foundations that fund Ivy League institutions.

- **The Ivy League Obsession:** Malhotra labels elite Indian families who donate massive sums to Harvard as funding their own civilizational erosion.
- **Ideological Laundering:** These donations elevate Harvard as a global *Vishwa Guru* (world teacher). The theories polished at Harvard are then imported back into India to shape policies within domestic private universities and corporate workplaces—often through compliance rubrics like Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) ratings that Malhotra views as Trojan horses for external dominance.

### 5. Historical Amnesia and Erasure of Reciprocal Influence

Malhotra documents that Harvard wasn't always an echo chamber for the unilateral deconstruction of Indian identity:

- **Erased Lineages:** He notes that iconic Harvard figures like the pioneer of psychology William James were profoundly influenced by Hindu and Buddhist practitioners (such as Swami Vivekananda and Anagarika Dharmapala). Similarly, legendary thinkers like Josiah Royce and T.S. Eliot studied Sanskrit as a core component of the philosophical curriculum at Harvard.
- **Institutional Revisionism:** Over time, Malhotra argues, Harvard systematically erased these records of equal, reciprocal learning from its institutional memory. This replaced a genuine engagement with non-Western thought with an aggressive, asymmetrical gaze designed to force compliance with secularized Western worldviews.
