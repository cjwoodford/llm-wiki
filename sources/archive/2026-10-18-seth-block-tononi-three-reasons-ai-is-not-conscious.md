# Three reasons why AI is not conscious and perhaps never will be

**Authors:** [[Anil Seth|Anil K. Seth]], [[Ned Block|Ned Block]], [[Giulio Tononi|Giulio Tononi]]  
**Published:** October 18, 2026  
**Source:** *Nature* 658, 866–868 (2026) • [doi:10.1038/d41586-026-03313-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-026-03313-7)  
**Context:** Landmark joint intervention by three of the world's leading consciousness researchers—spanning experimental neuroscience, philosophy of mind, and theoretical neurobiology—providing a definitive scientific rebuttal to claims of AI sentience and moral patienthood.

---

## 📌 Executive Summary

Writing in *Nature*, neuroscientists [[Anil Seth]] and [[Giulio Tononi]] alongside philosopher [[Ned Block]] mount a rigorous, multifaceted defense against the rising tide of claims that artificial intelligence systems could already be conscious or on a trajectory to acquire subjective experience. Rejecting arguments from prominent technologists (Geoffrey Hinton, Richard Dawkins) and frontier labs ([[Anthropic]], claiming computational signatures of consciousness in Claude), the authors set out three foundational reasons why digital, silicon-based systems are extraordinarily unlikely to ever be conscious:

1. **Intelligence is Not Consciousness (Doing vs. Being)**: Intelligence concerns functional behavior and task performance (*doing*); consciousness concerns subjective, phenomenological feeling (*being*). In the human brain, the cerebellum contains ~80% of all neurons and executes highly complex, fast, adaptive motor computations completely outside conscious awareness (and its surgical removal does not abolish consciousness). Modern Large Language Models (LLMs) appear conscious solely because of linguistic fluency—an anthropomorphic illusion, highlighted by the fact that equally complex architectures like AlphaFold evoke zero consciousness attribution because they do not "talk" to us.
2. **The Fallacy of Computational Functionalism (Substrate Dependence)**: Tracing the machine metaphor from Alan Turing (1936) and Warren McCulloch & Walter Pitts (1943), the authors dismantle the core assumption that computation alone is sufficient for consciousness. Unlike digital computers, biological brains exhibit no sharp separation between software and hardware; what they *do* is inseparable from what they *are*. Biological-substrate dependence directly refutes computational functionalism.
3. **Non-Computational Theories of Consciousness**: Leading neuroscientific and material theories preclude silicon consciousness:
   - **Integrated Information Theory (IIT)**: Requires a physical substrate with irreducible cause-effect power ($\Phi$). Deep learning architectures implemented on digital hardware possess $\Phi \approx 0$.
   - **Biological Naturalism**: Consciousness relies on specific biological materials and physical mechanisms—electrochemical synapses, broad cortical electromagnetic field integration, metabolism, and autopoiesis (living cellular self-regeneration).

Crucially, Seth, Block, and Tononi dismantle the widely invoked **"Precautionary Approach"** to AI welfare. They demonstrate that decisions under uncertainty must balance false negatives against false positives, and in digital AI, **false positives are far more likely and hazardous**: granting rights or moral standing to silicon tools actively cripples necessary oversight and governance, while diverting scarce moral and financial resources away from genuine sentient beings (humans and non-human animals).

## 📄 Verbatim Primary Source Text

Artificial-intelligence systems such as large language models (LLMs) can exhibit behaviour that seems strikingly intelligent. This has led many people, including some experts, to wonder whether these systems could have some form of consciousness — the capacity for subjective experience. However, we think that existing AI systems are not conscious, and that, along current trajectories of development, they are not likely to become conscious. Greater clarity about whether AI could become consciousness will be crucial as society grapples with the challenges posed by this increasingly powerful technology.

Several prominent scientists, including biologist Richard Dawkins and AI pioneer Geoffrey Hinton, have argued that AI systems might already be conscious. And earlier this year, AI company Anthropic in San Francisco, California, published research claiming to have identified computational signatures of a form of consciousness in its Claude model [^1].

Whether AI models can ever be conscious is not merely a philosophical question. It has practical implications. For instance, if AI systems were conscious, they might experience distress and pain, similar to how people do. Then should countless new conscious entities that might experience suffering be allowed to proliferate? Perhaps not surprisingly, some people are already calling for ‘AI welfare’ and ‘AI rights’ to be taken seriously [^2].

Conversely, if AI systems only seem to be conscious and lack actual inner experience, then granting them rights or protections could hinder efforts to govern and control them at precisely the time when oversight is urgently needed. Following this path would draw attention and resources away from beings whose consciousness is not in any doubt.

Looking beyond humans and our closest relatives in the animal kingdom, it is still hard to be certain about what does, and does not, have the capacity for consciousness — be it insects, AI or the creations of synthetic biology, such as laboratory-grown brain organoids [^3]<sup>,</sup>[^4].

This uncertainty has prompted some researchers to advocate a precautionary approach: if there is a reasonable chance that AI systems could be conscious, then society must take anticipatory action and give those systems some form of protection against potential suffering. This, the argument goes, would avoid reprising moral errors of the past — for example, the mistreatment of non-human animals, or surgery on preterm human babies without anaesthesia. An extreme version of this idea is that society should reallocate resources from addressing the welfare concerns of present-day humans to lessen the potential suffering of trillions of future AI descendants.

In our view, uncertainty is not the same as knowing nothing, and it does not mean that all possibilities are equally likely to occur. Decisions about what is moral must weigh both the consequences of false negatives — failing to recognize consciousness where it exists — and the costs of false positives — attributing consciousness where none exists. In the case of AI, we think that false positives are much more likely to occur than false negatives are. If so, a precautionary approach would be misguided.

Between us, we’ve spent decades studying consciousness from diverse scientific perspectives: experimental neuroscience, theoretical neurobiology and philosophy of mind. On the basis of this work, we feel that consciousness in AI is extraordinarily unlikely, at least in the digital, silicon-based systems that underpin today’s technologies. Here, we set out three reasons for our scepticism.

---

### Intelligence is not consciousness

What we mean by consciousness is simple enough: it is what goes away with dreamless sleep or under general anaesthesia, and what returns after waking up or while dreaming. Conscious experiences feel like something, whether it is tasting ice cream on a hot day or seeing the vibrant colours of a sunset. Philosopher Thomas Nagel, whose influential 1974 essay ‘What is it like to be a bat?’ helped to shape modern debates about consciousness, arguing that a conscious organism is one for which there is “something that it is like to be that organism” [^5].

This definition of consciousness introduces the first reason for scepticism about conscious AI. Consciousness is not the same thing as intelligence. Consciousness is about being and feeling; intelligence — very broadly — is all about doing, or performing functions. Just because something is intelligent does not mean that it is conscious.

Humans know that we are conscious, and we tend to associate consciousness with using our intellectual capacities. Yet, conscious experience and intelligent behaviour can come apart in striking ways. We know firsthand that our brain and body can carry out highly intelligent processes wholly outside awareness. For example, the cerebellum adjusts and plans motor performance in a way that is precise, fast and adaptable through ongoing learning. Yet none of what it does involves consciousness directly — we are wholly unaware of all its ‘computations’. Moreover, absence or removal of the cerebellum, which contains nearly four-fifths of the neurons in the human brain, does not affect consciousness directly [^6].

The temptation to conflate intelligence and consciousness probably reflects baked-in psychological biases — anthropocentrism, viewing the world through a human lens, and anthropomorphism, attributing human characteristics to things that merely resemble people. Consider, for example, the seductive power of language, a capacity that humans have long felt makes us special. When LLMs talk to us, it’s natural to project onto them properties that accompany our linguistic fluency, such as intelligence and consciousness.

But LLMs are trained on vast quantities of human-written text, much of it explicitly or implicitly concerned with thoughts, feelings and conscious experiences. LLMs are mirrors that reflect an image of our collective digitized past [^7]. We gaze into them and all too readily confuse the reflections with our own conscious minds [^8].

The beguiling nature of LLMs is highlighted by considering the response to AlphaFold [^9], made by Google DeepMind in London. Similar to an LLM, AlphaFold is a powerful neural network running on silicon hardware. Yet almost nobody worries that it might be conscious. That’s because AlphaFold predicts protein structures, whereas LLMs ‘talk’ to us. The compelling illusion of consciousness created by linguistic fluency just isn’t there for AlphaFold.

---

### Silicon brains

Second, the idea of conscious AI is motivated by the assumption that algorithms running on silicon are, in principle, sufficient for consciousness [^10]. In other words, that computation of the right kind is all that it takes, and that it doesn’t matter whether these computations are implemented inside carbon-based brains or in silicon-based computers. Philosophers call this assumption computational functionalism. Although computations are clearly sufficient to produce forms of intelligence — as modern AI systems demonstrate — it is much less clear that they could also be sufficient for consciousness.

Computational functionalism is widely assumed among members of the tech community and is popular among some scientists and philosophers, too, but we think it is fatally flawed because brains are not — or at least not just — computers that are made of meat.

To see why, it’s worth tracing the origin of computational functionalism to two major developments made nearly a century ago. First, in 1936, computer scientist Alan Turing showed that any calculation carried out by one sufficiently powerful computer can, in principle, be carried out by another [^11]. Such a ‘universal Turing machine’ would allow mathematicians to think about computational problems in abstract terms, independent of the physical machine that would ultimately perform the calculations. Whether a computer is made of silicon chips, gears or something else is secondary to the computation — the algorithm itself.

A few years later, in 1943, neurophysiologist Warren McCulloch and mathematician Walter Pitts created the first mathematical model of an artificial neuron and showed that networks of these highly simplified abstract ‘neurons’ could carry out basic logical operations (such as AND, OR and NOT). In effect, an artificial neural network could function like a ‘computer’ in essentially the sense introduced by Turing [^12]<sup>,</sup>[^13].

These ideas, which helped to lay the foundations of modern AI systems, have encouraged many researchers to view the brain as nothing more than an information-processing device: a network of neurons that just happens to be made of meat [^14]. This overextension of the brain-as-computer metaphor is deeply problematic. One main reason is that it is impossible to separate what biological brains do — their functional organization — from what they are — their material substrate [^15]. In other words, there is no distinct separation between the mind and living tissue in the human brain, as there is between software and hardware in a digital computer.

This biological-substrate dependence directly challenges computational functionalism. This is because the assumption that the computations themselves are sufficient for consciousness rests precisely on this sharp separation: the Turing-inspired idea that the same computations can be carried out in physical substrates as different as silicon and carbon. Evidence that the substrate of the brain matters for its function is evidence against computational functionalism, which in turn makes it very unlikely that consciousness could arise in today’s silicon-based AI systems [^16].

---

### Theories of consciousness

The third reason for scepticism comes from theories describing non-computational accounts of consciousness.

One influential theory, integrated information theory (IIT), starts from the essential properties of consciousness — those that are true of every experience we can have. For instance, we experience every visual scene as a single, integrated whole: its left and right side, colours and shapes are bound together to make the experience what it is. IIT argues that consciousness depends on a physical system, such as the brain, whose components satisfy the essential properties, requiring them to be tightly interconnected in specific ways [^17]<sup>,</sup>[^18]. These properties are satisfied by regions of the human brain that support consciousness but not, for instance, by the cerebellum. As shown by IIT, computer architectures that implement deep-learning systems cannot satisfy these properties because of the way in which they are wired. If IIT is right, then current AI systems cannot be conscious, no matter how capable they are [^19].

Another approach holds that consciousness — and perhaps other aspects of brain function — depends on specific material properties of biological brains and bodies. Candidates include electrochemical synapses (junctions at which an electrical nerve impulse is converted into a chemical signal) [^20]; electromagnetic fields that integrate neural activity across broad regions of the cortex [^21]; and fundamental biological processes such as metabolism and autopoiesis (the ability of living systems to continually regenerate themselves) [^15]<sup>,</sup>[^16]. These proposals are all forms of biological naturalism: the view that biological properties are necessary for consciousness [^22].

Pulling all this together, the balance of theory and evidence strongly suggests that, no matter how intelligent it is, silicon-based AI is unlikely to be or become conscious. The default position of the research community should therefore be that AI is not conscious and should not be treated as such.

In our view, silicon, digital AI systems should be considered objects, not subjects; they are smart tools, not smart people. Advocating that current AI systems should have rights and welfare protection is not just mistaken, it diverts attention and scarce resources away from where they belong: dedicated to the well-being of fellow sentient beings.
