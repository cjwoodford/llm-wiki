# Computation Is Not Instrinsic To Nature (Searle)

> In chapter three of his book *Mind* by John Searle, he talks about how computation and syntax are observer relative. It's a dense section that I'm having trouble fully understanding. He points to a fuller explanation of it in his book *The Rediscovery of the Mind* in chapter 9. But I'm wondering if you can give me a little bit more of an explanation of the point he's making about how computation is not discovered in nature.

John Searle’s core claim is that **computation is not an intrinsic physical process found in the world—it is an interpretation assigned by a conscious observer to physical state transitions.**

When cognitive scientists or computationalists claim the brain is literally a computer executing algorithms, Searle argues they are making a fundamental category error: mistaking an observer-relative description for an intrinsic physical property. #functionalism #computationalism 

### Intrinsic vs. Observer-Relative Properties

Searle divides all facts about the world into two distinct categories:

- **Intrinsic features:** Exist independently of human consciousness or observers.
    - *Examples:* Mass, gravitational pull, molecular composition, electrical voltage, photosynthesis. If all conscious beings disappeared tomorrow, an object would still have mass and electrical resistance.
- **Observer-relative features:** Exist only because conscious observers attach meaning, use, or interpretation to an intrinsic physical state.
    - *Examples:* Money, property rights, being a "chair," or being a "word." A piece of green paper intrinsically has cellulose and green ink; it is only a "$20 bill" relative to an agreed-upon human system of meaning.

### Why Computation Is Purely Observer-Relative

A computer chip consists solely of intrinsic physical phenomena: silicon substrates, voltage changes, electrons moving through gates, and heat dissipation.

- **Physics has no syntax:** Physics deals with mass, force, charge, and energy. It does not contain zeros, ones, symbols, algorithms, or syntactic rules.
- **The assignment of syntax:** We (the designers and observers) assign the symbol `0` to a low-voltage state (e.g., 0V) and `1` to a high-voltage state (e.g., 5V). We then design the electrical pathways so that when the circuit transitions from physical state $A$ to physical state $B$, we interpret that transition as arithmetic (e.g., $2 + 2 = 4$).
- **Syntax is not in the physics:** The circuit doesn't "know" it is adding; it is simply undergoing state changes governed by electromagnetism. Without an observer to map a syntactic system onto those electrical charges, **no computation took place—only physics occurred.**

As Searle puts it: *"Computation is not discovered in the physics; it is assigned to the physics."*

### The "Universal Realizability" Problem

Because computation is just an abstract syntactic pattern mapped onto physical state changes, Searle points out that with a complex enough mapping algorithm, **almost any physical system can be described as implementing almost any computation.**

- You could map the molecular movements of water in a pipe, or the thermal fluctuations in a wall, to the formal state transitions of a chess program.
- That does not mean the wall is intrinsically running a chess program. It means an observer’s mind has mapped a set of mathematical relations onto the wall’s physical microstates.

If computation were an intrinsic property discovered in nature (like mass or charge), you could build a device to measure "computational units" in the wild. But you cannot, because computation requires a symbolic grammar that exists only in an interpreting mind.

### The Consequence for the Brain and AI

This distinction directly attacks the Computational Theory of Mind (functionalism):

1. **The brain has intrinsic neurobiology:** The brain has real, intrinsic physical/biological processes—action potentials, neurotransmitter releases, synaptic plasticity, and conscious experiences.
2. **The brain does not intrinsically compute:** To say "the brain is a computer" is not like saying "the heart is a pump." A pump moves physical fluid (an intrinsic physical action). A digital computer processes formal syntax (an observer-relative assignment).
3. **The category mistake:** Brains cause minds through biological, neurochemical processes. Describing the brain's neural firings computationally may be a helpful mathematical model for scientists, but computation is never a *causal mechanism* discovered in the biology itself.

## Relevance to the Chinese Room Argument

> How does Searle's argument about observer-relativity in Rediscovery of the Mind expand upon his original Chinese Room thought experiment?

Searle’s argument on the **observer-relativity of syntax** in *The Rediscovery of the Mind* (Chapter 9) is essentially an admission that his original **Chinese Room argument (1980) conceded too much ground to the computationalists.**

While the Chinese Room aimed to show that computation is insufficient for understanding, the observer-relativity argument goes a step further to show that computation isn’t an intrinsic physical reality in the first place.

### The Evolution: What Each Argument Proves

| **Dimension**            | **The Chinese Room (1980)**                                  | **Observer-Relativity / Chapter 9 (1992)**                                                 |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Core Target**          | **Semantics** (Meaning / Intentionality)                     | **Syntax** (Computation / Formal Symbols)                                                  |
| **The Premise Conceded** | Assumes a computer *does* intrinsically have syntax/symbols. | Denies that physics contains syntax or symbols at all.                                     |
| **Core Maxim**           | *"Syntax is not sufficient for semantics."*                  | *"Syntax is not intrinsic to physics."*                                                    |
| **The Verdict**          | Even perfect symbol manipulation cannot produce a mind.      | A computer isn't even "manipulating symbols" unless an observer interprets it as doing so. |

### 1. The Chinese Room Concession

In the 1980 thought experiment, Searle imagined himself in a room manipulating Chinese characters according to a rulebook (the computer program):

- **The Setup:** Inputs come in (formal symbols), lookup operations occur (syntactic manipulation), outputs go out (formal symbols).
- **The Conclusion:** The man in the room does not understand Chinese. Therefore, implementing a formal program is not sufficient to produce intentionality, semantics, or understanding.
- **The Hidden Concession:** Searle took it for granted that the room/machine was *actually, objectively running a program*—that formal syntax and symbol processing were real, objective operations occurring inside the system.

### 2. The Deeper Move in *The Rediscovery of the Mind*

By 1992, Searle realized that attacking the gap between syntax and semantics left a major computationalist claim untouched: the idea that the brain is *intrinsically* an information processor executing algorithms.

Chapter 9 attacks the very concept of "syntax" itself:

- **Syntax requires an interpreter:** A transistor switching between high and low voltage is purely an electromagnetic event. It only becomes a `1`, a `0`, or an `IF/THEN` command because an external observer maps symbolic meaning onto the electrical states.
- **No "computational processes" in nature:** In the Chinese Room, the slips of paper with squiggles only count as "symbols" because human beings created them to represent language. The ink on paper has intrinsic chemistry, but its status as a syntactic token is 100% observer-relative.
- **The Radical Implication:** You cannot ask, *"Is the brain a digital computer?"* in the same way you ask, *"Is the heart a muscular pump?"* A heart pumps blood whether humans exist or not (intrinsic physics). The brain only "computes" if an outside observer decides to model neural firing as symbolic operations (observer-relative assignment).

### Why This Changes the Debate

The Chinese Room argued against **Strong AI** (the claim that running the right program creates a conscious mind).

The observer-relativity argument attacks **Cognitivism itself** (the foundational premise of cognitive science that thinking *is* computation).

If syntax is not intrinsic to the physical world, then:

1. Brain processes cannot be explained by computational algorithms, because the brain’s neurobiology doesn't come with built-in, intrinsic syntactic interpreters.
2. Computational models of the brain are useful simulations (like computational models of the weather), but a simulation of a biological process is not the duplication of its intrinsic causal powers.

## The Conceptual Borrowing of It All

> So this critique is related to conceptual borrowing as well?

Yes, exactly. The observer-relativity critique is a textbook case of **conceptual borrowing** (often running in tandem with a homunculus fallacy).

In cognitive science and computational AI, conceptual borrowing happens when a discipline quietly borrows concepts that **require an active conscious mind**, strips away the explicit mention of that mind, and then claims to have explained the mind using the borrowed concepts.

### How the Conceptual Borrowing Works

The loop follows a clear four-step pattern:

```other
Human Mind 
  └──> 1. Invents formal tools (symbols, logic, syntax, "information")
        └──> 2. Implements them in silicon (mapping voltage to 0s and 1s)
              └──> 3. Forgets the mapping requires an observer ("Silicon intrinsically computes!")
                    └──> 4. Borrows "computation" back to explain the Human Mind ("The brain is just a computer!")
```

1. **The Origin:** Concepts like *syntax, symbols, rules, decoding, representation,* and *information* were invented by conscious human agents to describe formal systems we consciously manipulate.
2. **The Projection:** We build machines (calculators, digital computers) where physical state transitions mirror these formal systems because **we** designed the physical architecture to correspond to our mathematical rules.
3. **The Erasure:** We start describing the machine's purely physical behavior using the borrowed psychological/linguistic terms: *"the circuit reads the input," "the algorithm decides," "the memory stores the symbol."*
4. **The Circular "Explanation":** Theorists then turn around, look at the biological brain, and say: *"Look, the brain is an information processor running algorithms!"*

They claim to have "naturalized" the mind through computation, but they have actually just **re-imported their own conscious concepts under the guise of objective physics.**

### The Hidden Homunculus

Searle points out that when you borrow syntactic notions to explain cognition without acknowledging the observer, you inevitably smuggle a "little person" (a homunculus) into the theory:

- If neural firings are "symbols" or "messages," **who is reading them?**
- If the brain is "executing an algorithm," **who is interpreting the syntax?**

In an actual electronic computer, the "homunculus" is the human programmer and user standing outside the box, supplying the semantic interpretation and recognizing the syntax.

When computationalists claim the brain itself is literally a computer executing algorithms intrinsically, they leave the user out of the loop—leaving an explanatory vacuum where the interpreter ought to be.

### Why the Borrowing Persists

This conceptual borrowing is persuasive because it operates as a powerful **epistemic metaphor**. It is genuinely useful for an engineer or neuroscientist to model a neural circuit *as if* it were a logic gate.

The error—what Alfred North Whitehead called the *Fallacy of Misplaced Concreteness*—occurs the moment researchers forget that "information processing" is an interpretive lens, and instead declare it to be the foundational ontology of the brain itself.
