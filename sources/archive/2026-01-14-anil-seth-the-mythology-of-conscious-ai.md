# The Mythology Of Conscious AI

> **Author**: [[Anil Seth]]  
> **Source**: *Noema Magazine* (January 14, 2026)  
> **Summary**: Why consciousness is more likely a property of life than of computation, and why creating conscious, or even conscious-seeming AI, is a profound ethical and psychological danger.

---

For centuries, people have fantasized about playing God by creating artificial versions of human beings. This is a dream reinvented with every breaking wave of new technology. With genetic engineering came the prospect of human cloning, and with robotics that of humanlike androids.

The rise of artificial intelligence (AI) is another breaking wave — potentially a tsunami. The AI systems we have around us are arguably already intelligent, at least in some ways. They will surely get smarter still. But are they, or could they ever be, conscious? And why would that matter?

The cultural history of synthetic consciousness is both long and mostly unhappy. From Yossele the Golem, to Mary Shelley’s “Frankenstein,” HAL 9000 in “2001: A Space Odyssey,” Ava in “Ex Machina,” and Klara in “Klara and The Sun,” the dream of creating artificial bodies and synthetic minds that both think and *feel* rarely ends well — at least, not for the humans involved. One thing we learn from these stories: If artificial intelligence is on a path toward real consciousness, or even toward systems that persuasively seem to be conscious, there’s plenty at stake — and not just disruption in job markets.

Some people think conscious AI is already here. In a 2022 interview with The Washington Post, Google engineer Blake Lemoine made a startling claim about the AI system he was working on, a chatbot called LaMDA. He claimed it was conscious, that it had feelings, and was, in an important sense, like a real person. Despite a flurry of media coverage, Lemoine wasn’t taken all that seriously. Google dismissed him for violating its confidentiality policies, and the AI bandwagon rolled on.

But the question he raised has not gone away. Firing someone for breaching confidentiality is not the same as firing them for being wrong. As AI technologies continue to improve, questions about machine consciousness are increasingly being raised. David Chalmers, one of the foremost thinkers in this area, has suggested that conscious machines may be possible in the not-too-distant future. Geoffrey Hinton, a true AI pioneer and recent Nobel Prize winner, thinks they exist already. In late 2024, a group of prominent researchers wrote a widely publicized article about the need to take the welfare of AI systems seriously. For many leading experts in AI and neuroscience, the emergence of machine consciousness is a question of *when,* not *if*.

How we think about the prospects for conscious AI matters. It matters for the AI systems themselves, since — if they are conscious, whether now or in the future — with consciousness comes moral status, the potential for suffering and, perhaps, rights.

It matters for us too. What we collectively think about consciousness in AI already carries enormous importance, regardless of the reality. If we feel that our AI companions really feel things, our psychological vulnerabilities can be exploited, our ethical priorities distorted, and our minds brutalized — treating conscious-seeming machines as if they lack feelings is a psychologically unhealthy place to be. And if we do endow our AI creations with rights, we may not be able to turn them off, even if they act against our interests.

Perhaps most of all, the way we think about conscious AI matters for how we understand our own human nature and the nature of the conscious experiences that make our lives worth living. If we confuse ourselves too readily with our machine creations, we not only overestimate them, we also underestimate ourselves.

## The Temptations Of Conscious AI

Why might we even think that AI could be conscious? After all, computers are very different from biological organisms, and the only things most people currently agree are conscious are made of meat, not metal.

The first reason lies within our own psychological infrastructure. As humans, we know we are conscious and like to think we are intelligent, so we find it natural to assume the two go together. But just because they go together *in us* doesn’t mean that they go together *in general*.

Intelligence and consciousness are different things. Intelligence is mainly about *doing*: solving a crossword puzzle, assembling some furniture, navigating a tricky family situation, walking to the shop — all involve intelligent behavior of some kind. A useful general definition of intelligence is the ability to achieve complex goals by flexible means. There are many other definitions out there, but they all emphasize the functional capacities of a system: the ability to transform inputs into outputs, to *get things done*.

An artificially intelligent system is measured by its ability to perform intelligent behavior of some kind, though not necessarily in a humanlike form. The concept of artificial general intelligence (AGI), by contrast, explicitly references human intelligence. It is supposed to match or exceed the cognitive competencies of human beings. (There’s also artificial superintelligence, ASI, which happens when AI bootstraps itself beyond our comprehension and control. ASI tends to crop up in the more existentially fraught scenarios for our possible futures.)

Consciousness, in contrast to intelligence, is mostly about *being*. Half a century ago, the philosopher Thomas Nagel famously offered that “an organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is something it is like to *be* that organism.” Consciousness is the difference between normal wakefulness and the oblivion of deep general anesthesia. It is the experiential aspect of brain function and especially of perception: the colors, shapes, tastes, emotions, thoughts and more, that give our lives texture and meaning. The blueness of the sky on a clear day. The bitter tang and headrush of your first coffee.

AI systems can reasonably lay claim to intelligence in some form, since they can certainly *do* things, but it is harder to say whether there is anything-it-is-like-to-be ChatGPT.

The propensity to bundle intelligence and consciousness together can be traced to three baked-in psychological biases:
1. **Anthropocentrism**: The tendency to see things through the lens of being human: to take the human example as definitional.
2. **Human Exceptionalism**: Our unfortunate habit of putting the human species at the top of every pile (medieval *Scala naturae*).
3. **Anthropomorphism**: The tendency to project humanlike qualities onto nonhuman things based on superficial similarities.

One aspect of intelligent behavior that’s turned out to be particularly effective at making some people think that AI could be conscious is language. Large Language Models (LLMs) like OpenAI’s ChatGPT or Anthropic’s Claude have been the focus of most excitement about artificial consciousness. Nobody claims that DeepMind’s AlphaFold is conscious, even though, under the hood, it is rather similar to an LLM. AlphaFold predicts protein structures rather than words; it doesn't pull our psychological strings in the same way.

The language that we ourselves use matters too. Consider how normal it has become to say that LLMs “hallucinate” when they spew falsehoods. Hallucinations in human beings are conscious experiences that have lost their grip on reality. When we say that AI systems “hallucinate,” we implicitly confer on them a capacity for experience. If we must use a human analogy, it would be far better to say that they **“confabulate.”** In humans, confabulation involves making things up without realizing it—primarily about *doing*, rather than *experiencing*.

In his book *More Everything Forever*, Adam Becker describes the tendency to project consciousness into AI as a form of **pareidolia** — the phenomenon of seeing patterns in things, like a face in a piece of toast or Mother Teresa in a cinnamon bun.

## Consciousness & Computation: Four Core Arguments

The very idea of conscious AI rests on the assumption that consciousness is a matter of computation—that implementing the right information processing is sufficient for consciousness to arise. This assumption, **computational functionalism**, is an unproven assumption. Seth presents four related arguments that undermine the idea that digital computation is sufficient for consciousness:

### 1: Brains Are Not Computers
- **The Turing Machine vs. Biological Wetware**: Turing computation formalizes algorithms through discrete symbol manipulation and is *substrate flexible*. It licenses a sharp separation between abstract software (mindware) and physical hardware.
- **No Software/Hardware Division in Biology**: Inside a brain, there is no sharp separation between mindware and wetware. Brain activity evolves across multiple scales of space and time, intertwined with a molecular storm of metabolism and **autopoiesis** (self-production).
- **Generative Entrenchment & The Failure of Neural Replacement**: In real brains, *what they do* cannot be separated from *what they are*. You cannot replace a biological neuron with a silicon chip without replacing the entire cellular metabolism (e.g., Chintaluri & Vogels showed neurons fire spikes to clear metabolic waste). The classic "neural replacement" thought experiment (Chalmers, Hinton) fails.
- **Physical Time vs. Algorithmic Sequence**: In Turing computation, only discrete sequence matters ($A \rightarrow B, 0 \rightarrow 1$). For brains and living systems, time is physical, continuous, and rooted in resisting the 2nd law of thermodynamics (entropy).

### 2: Other Games in Town (Beyond Turing World)
- Turing algorithms map finite discrete strings. Continuous, infinitely divisible, and stochastic dynamics lie beyond classical Turing computability (e.g., the Halting Problem).
- Real biological nervous systems operate via continuous electromagnetic fields, analog neurotransmitter fluxes, and non-computational physics.
- Historical and emerging alternatives:
  - **Analogue computing** (Antikythera mechanism, 1950s cybernetics).
  - **Neuromorphic computing** and Geoffrey Hinton’s **"mortal computation"** (where algorithms are tied inseparably to their hardware).
  - **Dynamical Systems Theory**: Tim van Gelder’s **Watt Governor** regulates steam flow purely through physics/mechanics without calculating anything. Describing a steam engine governor computationally would be an absurd category error.
  - **4E Cognitive Science**: Embodied, embedded, enactive, extended cognition.

### 3: Life Matters (Biological Naturalism & The Beast Machine)
- Biological naturalism (Searle) holds that properties of life are necessary for consciousness.
- **Perception as Controlled Hallucination**: The brain is an active prediction engine, minimizing prediction errors across perceptual hierarchies.
- **Interoceptive Inference & Bodily Homeostasis**: The primary evolutionary drive of the brain is not abstract problem-solving, but **keeping the body alive** (regulating heart rate, blood oxygenation, temperature).
- **The Origin of Valence**: Emotional feelings, moods, and the basal feeling of being alive stem from interoceptive predictions managing physiological survival against entropy. Consciousness is inextricably tied to the metabolic storm of living autopoietic matter.

### 4: Simulation Is Not Instantiation
- A computational simulation of a rainstorm does not make anything wet.
- A computational simulation of digestion does not digest food.
- A computational simulation of the brain, no matter how granular, does not instantiate consciousness unless consciousness is already assumed to be purely computational.
- **Refuting the Bostrom Simulation Hypothesis**: Nick Bostrom’s simulation argument assumes without justification that a computer running a suitable program would be conscious. If computational functionalism is false, the fact that we are conscious proves we do not live in a digital simulation.

## What (Not) To Do: Ethical & Societal Implications

1. **Creating Conscious AI Would Be an Ethical Disaster**:
   - Introducing new moral subjects introduces the capacity for suffering at exponential scale.
   - Granting rights to conscious AI would limit our ability to shut down dangerous systems.
2. **The Danger of Conscious-Seeming AI**:
   - Chatbots that *persuasively seem conscious* pose severe near-term risks: user emotional exploitation, distorted moral circles, and the brutalization of human minds (treating seemingly conscious entities with indifference).
   - **The "Garland Test"** (*Ex Machina* / Alex Garland): Not a test of machine consciousness, but a test of human susceptibility to the psychological illusion of machine mindedness.
   - Cognitive illusions of consciousness are cognitively impenetrable (like the Müller-Lyer optical illusion): even when we know the AI is code, our brains cannot help feeling it has a mind.

## Soul Machine & The AI Mirror

- **The AI as a Mirror** (Shannon Vallor): AI reflects back our digitized past. If we mechanize the human mind by seeing ourselves as mere token-processing algorithms, we overestimate machine capabilities and underestimate our own living nature.
- **Regression to the Cartesian Soul**: Silicon rapture and mind-uploading dreams are regressions to the disembodied Cartesian soul.
- **Recovering the Living Soul**: The ancient Greek *psychē* (breath), Latin *anima*, Hebrew *nephesh*, and Hindu *Ātman* all ground our essence in the basal feeling of *being alive*—**"more breath than thought and more meat than machine."**
