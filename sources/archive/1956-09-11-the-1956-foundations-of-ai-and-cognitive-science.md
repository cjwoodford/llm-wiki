# The Birth of Computer and Cognitive Science: Dartmouth, MIT, and the 1956 Big Bang

In the late summer and early fall of 1956, two back-to-back gatherings in New England—the Dartmouth Summer Research Project and the MIT Symposium on Information Theory—laid the conceptual and institutional foundations for both Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Cognitive Science. Together, these events sparked the "Cognitive Revolution" by establishing computation and information processing as the primary framework for understanding intelligence in both machines and minds.

---

## 🏛️ The Two Landmark Events

- **The Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence (June–August 1956):** Organized at Dartmouth College, this eight-week workshop was conceived to explore how machines could simulate aspects of human learning and problem-solving. It served as the official birthplace and christening event of Artificial Intelligence as a distinct field.
- **The MIT Symposium on Information Theory (September 10–12, 1956):** Held at MIT, this gathering brought together mathematicians, engineers, linguists, and psychologists. Cognitive psychologist George Miller famously designated September 11, 1956, as the exact birthday of **Cognitive Science** due to a trio of foundational papers presented back-to-back on that single day.

---

## 📄 The Three Breakthrough Papers at MIT (September 11, 1956)

- **Allen Newell & Herbert A. Simon (*The Logic Theory Machine*):** Demonstrated the *Logic Theorist* (developed with J.C. Shaw), the first running symbolic AI program. By proving 38 of the first 52 theorems in Russell and Whitehead’s *Principia Mathematica* using heuristic search trees, it demonstrated that computers could manipulate abstract concepts rather than just crunch arithmetic.
- **Noam Chomsky (*Three Models for the Description of Language*):** Introduced formal, generative grammars to linguistics, showing that human language syntax cannot be reduced to statistical sequence-matching or stimulus-response habits. This provided the theoretical basis for innate mental structures.
- **George A. Miller (*The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two*):** Framed human short-term memory as a limited-capacity information-processing channel (capable of holding roughly $7 \pm 2$ chunks of data), directly challenging behaviorism with quantifiable models of internal mental storage.

---

## 👥 Key Players and Their Dual Roles

- **John McCarthy (Dartmouth / Stanford / MIT):** Coined the term "Artificial Intelligence" in his proposal for the Dartmouth workshop to break away from Norbert Wiener's cybernetics and secure independent funding.
- **Marvin Minsky (Harvard / MIT):** Co-organizer of Dartmouth and a central MIT AI pioneer who treated AI as the engineering of synthetic mental architectures and human psychology as biological software architecture.
- **Claude Shannon (Bell Labs / MIT):** The father of Information Theory, whose mathematical definitions of channel capacity, bandwidth, and bits provided the shared quantitative language for both computer engineering and neural coding.
- **Allen Newell & Herbert A. Simon (RAND / Carnegie Mellon):** Polymaths who bridged computer science (winning the Turing Award) and psychology (winning APA awards) by formulating the Physical Symbol System Hypothesis—the principle that human problem-solving and computer execution are two implementations of the same symbol-manipulating process.
- **Nathaniel Rochester (IBM):** Co-organizer at Dartmouth and chief architect of the IBM 701, who helped anchor early AI research to digital computer architectures.
- **Other Notable Attendees:** Arthur Samuel (machine learning & checkers), Ray Solomonoff (algorithmic probability), Oliver Selfridge (pattern recognition & Pandemonium architecture).

## 💡 The Birth of a New Paradigm: Computational Functionalism

The paradigm birthed across Dartmouth and MIT in late summer and autumn of 1956 was **computational functionalism**—the foundational doctrine that **cognition is computation**, and that intelligence in both humans and machines consists of the rule-governed manipulation of physical symbols.

This shift redefined the scientific understanding of the mind around four interrelated pillars:

### 1. The Physical Symbol System Hypothesis
Before 1956, digital computers were conceived primarily as giant arithmetic calculators. The breakthrough engineered by Allen Newell and Herbert Simon—culminating in their demonstration of the *Logic Theorist*—proved that computing machines could manipulate abstract, non-numerical tokens according to formal heuristic rules.
- **Equivalence of Mind and Machine:** Human reasoning and computer execution were formally recast as two instances of the exact same phenomenon: **physical symbol manipulation**.
- **Information Over Energy:** By synthesizing Claude Shannon’s mathematical theory of communication with computer logic, the paradigm redefined mental processes in terms of bits, channel capacity, bandwidth, and noise, divorcing "information" from semantic meaning or conscious feeling.

### 2. The "Software/Hardware" Split (Substrate Independence)
The 1956 gatherings established what philosopher Hilary Putnam soon codified as **machine functionalism**: the premise that the organization of thought ("software") can be studied independently of the physical material executing it ("hardware").
- **Multiple Realizability:** Cognition was treated as an algorithmic state-transition system. Parsing a sentence or solving a theorem was seen as mathematically identical whether executed on biological wetware or silicon circuits.
- **Subordination of Biology:** The brain was relegated to an implementation substrate. Neurophysiology and organic embodiment were treated as secondary engineering details rather than defining features of mind.

### 3. The Overthrow of Behaviorism and Opening of the "Black Box"
Academic psychology in the 1940s and early 1950s was dominated by Skinnerian behaviorism, which viewed internal mental states (memories, intentions, representations) as unscientific, mystical fictions.
- **Materialist License for Internal States:** The digital computer gave cognitive psychologists an empirical, non-mystical justification to study the internal mind. Because a physical machine could possess internal memory buffers, execute stored programs, and track goals without violating the laws of physics, human brains were recognized as doing the same.
- **The Triad of Internal Representation:** At the September 11 MIT symposium, George Miller framed short-term memory as a limited-capacity storage buffer; Noam Chomsky demonstrated that syntax requires innate, internal generative rules that cannot be explained by stimulus-response habits; and Newell and Simon proved heuristic search could generate logical reasoning.

### 4. The Mechanistic Elimination of Context and Phenomenology
To render human thought computationally tractable, the new paradigm executed a radical operational substitution:
- **Exclusion of the Non-Formal:** As historian Howard Gardner and philosopher Hubert Dreyfus observed, the cognitive revolution deliberately sidelined emotion, bodily intuition, cultural context, and phenomenological subjectivity in favor of discrete, programmable architectures.
- **Conflating Function with Experience:** Qualitative, first-person conscious experience ($P$-consciousness) was effectively collapsed into third-person functional data-routing, access, and storage ($A$-consciousness).

The lasting legacy of the 1956 paradigm was the creation of a closed conceptual feedback loop: computer scientists built machines that borrowed the language of human psychology (e.g., *memory*, *learning*, *attention*), while psychologists, linguists, and neuroscientists imported computer metaphors to explain the living mind. By establishing the computational model as the sole shared currency across these fields, Dartmouth and MIT established the intellectual orthodoxy that still governs artificial intelligence and cognitive science today.
