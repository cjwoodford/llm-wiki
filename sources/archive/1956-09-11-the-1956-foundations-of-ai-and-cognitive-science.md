# The 1956 Foundations of AI and Cognitive Science: Dartmouth, MIT, and the Cognitive Revolution

In the late summer and early fall of 1956, two back-to-back gatherings in New England—the Dartmouth Summer Research Project and the MIT Symposium on Information Theory—laid the conceptual and institutional foundations for both Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Cognitive Science. Together, these events sparked the "Cognitive Revolution" by establishing computation and information processing as the primary framework for understanding intelligence in both machines and minds.

---

## The Two Landmark Events

* **The Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence (June–August 1956):** Organized at Dartmouth College, this eight-week workshop was conceived to explore how machines could simulate aspects of human learning and problem-solving. It served as the official birthplace and christening event of Artificial Intelligence as a distinct field.

* **The MIT Symposium on Information Theory (September 10–12, 1956):** Held at MIT, this gathering brought together mathematicians, engineers, linguists, and psychologists. Cognitive psychologist George Miller famously designated September 11, 1956, as the exact birthday of Cognitive Science due to a trio of foundational papers presented back-to-back on that single day.

---

## The Three Breakthrough Papers at MIT (September 11, 1956)

* **Allen Newell & Herbert A. Simon (*The Logic Theory Machine*):** Demonstrated the *Logic Theorist* (developed with J.C. Shaw), the first running symbolic AI program. By proving 38 of the first 52 theorems in Russell and Whitehead’s *Principia Mathematica* using heuristic search trees, it demonstrated that computers could manipulate abstract concepts rather than just crunch arithmetic.

* **Noam Chomsky (*Three Models for the Description of Language*):** Introduced formal, generative grammars to linguistics, showing that human language syntax cannot be reduced to statistical sequence-matching or stimulus-response habits. This provided the theoretical basis for innate mental structures.

* **George A. Miller (*The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two*):** Framed human short-term memory as a limited-capacity information-processing channel (capable of holding roughly  \pm 2$ chunks of data), directly challenging behaviorism with quantifiable models of internal mental storage.

---

## Key Players and Their Dual Roles

* **John McCarthy:** Coined the term "Artificial Intelligence" in his proposal for the Dartmouth workshop to break away from Norbert Wiener's cybernetics and secure independent funding.

* **Marvin Minsky:** Co-organizer of Dartmouth and a central MIT AI pioneer who treated AI as the engineering of synthetic mental architectures and human psychology as biological software architecture.

* **Claude Shannon:** The father of Information Theory, whose mathematical definitions of channel capacity, bandwidth, and bits provided the shared quantitative language for both computer engineering and neural coding.

* **Allen Newell & Herbert A. Simon:** Polymaths who bridged computer science (winning the Turing Award) and psychology (winning APA awards) by formulating the Physical Symbol System Hypothesis—the principle that human problem-solving and computer execution are two implementations of the same symbol-manipulating process.

* **Nathaniel Rochester:** Co-organizer at Dartmouth and chief architect of the IBM 701, who helped anchor early AI research to digital computer architectures.

---

## Core Takeaways and Lasting Influence

* **The Overthrow of Behaviorism:** By demonstrating that internal, rule-governed states could be rigorously measured (Miller), modeled formally (Chomsky), and executed on physical machines (Newell & Simon), 1956 dismantled the behaviorist doctrine that treated the internal mind as an unscientific "black box".

* **The "Software/Hardware" Split (Functionalism):** The 1956 paradigm shift paved the way for Machine Functionalism (formulated by Hilary Putnam in 1960) and the Computational Theory of Mind (Jerry Fodor). This established the premise of *multiple realizability*—the idea that cognition is algorithmic and can be studied independently of the biological wetware or silicon hardware executing it.

* **Institutional Formalization:** The momentum from 1956 catalyzed the founding of the first standalone Computer Science departments in the 1960s (such as Purdue and Stanford under George Forsythe and Louis Fein). In the late 1970s, the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation codified this convergence by funding the "Sloan Hexagon," institutionalizing the computational model across psychology, linguistics, neuroscience, and AI.

* **The Mind-Computer Feedback Loop:** The foundational link forged in 1956 created an enduring conceptual cycle: computer scientists modeled machines on human mental faculties (e.g., attention, working memory, heuristics), while cognitive scientists modeled the brain on computing hardware. This shared lineage remains the primary framework driving both contemporary cognitive neuroscience and modern discussions of artificial intelligence.
