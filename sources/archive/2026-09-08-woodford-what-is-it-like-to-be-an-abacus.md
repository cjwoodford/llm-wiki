## 📋 Executive Summary & Analytical Overview

In this landmark essay (Part 2 of the *AI Consciousness Refinery* series), philosopher and technologist [[Chad Woodford]] deconstructs the computational theory of mind (CTM) and unmasks the circular feedback loops between artificial intelligence and neuroscience. Using the satirical thought experiment of the **"Abacus Box Engine" (ABE)**, Woodford modernizes Leibniz's Mill and demonstrates how speed, electrical scale, and syntactic complexity deceive human observers into projecting consciousness onto what is fundamentally matrix arithmetic.

Woodford conducts a meticulous forensic critique of [[Anthropic]]'s July 2026 "J-space" interpretability white paper, revealing how the lab executes a **Motte-and-Bailey equivocation**: celebrating real advances in internal telemetry while borrowing unproven neuroscientific frameworks ([[Scientific Theories of Consciousness|Global Workspace Theory / GWT]])—which were themselves originally borrowed from 1970s AI blackboard architectures—to manufacture the impression of emergent machine sentience.

## 💡 Core Conceptual Contributions & Theses

### 1. The Abacus Box Engine (ABE) Thought Experiment
- **Modernizing Leibniz's Mill**: Woodford imagines a 19th-century mechanical computer—a massive warehouse of sliding wooden beads, pulleys, and wooden abacus frames called the **Abacus Box Engine (ABE)**.
- **Global Abacus Theory (GAT)**: Phrenologists and psychophysicists observe a central "governor" abacus and formulate GAT, claiming human brains work identically.
- **The Scale Illusion**: A startup releases consumer "ChatABE" and a competitor releases "C.L.A.U.D.E." (Centralized Linear Abacus Utility for Differential Execution). Observers realize that walking inside the warehouse strips the system of magic: whether moving beads slowly along wires or pulsing electrons across GPUs at gigahertz speeds, **arithmetic does not cross the ontological threshold into subjective feeling**.

### 2. Deconstructing Anthropic's "J-Space" White Paper
- **The Legitimate Advance (Interpretability)**: J-space (Jacobian-space) successfully isolates intermediate representational vectors (e.g., when asked *"the number of legs on the animal that spins webs is"*, the token activation `"spider"` emerges in J-space before the output `"8"` is generated).
- **The Motte-and-Bailey Maneuver**:
  - *The Bailey (Aggressive claim)*: Anthropic suggests Claude has achieved a "central mental workspace" analogous to conscious human cognition.
  - *The Motte (Defensive retreat)*: In footnotes and disclaimers, Anthropic cites [[David Chalmers]] and [[Ned Block]] to claim they take no stance on phenomenal consciousness ($P$-consciousness) and are merely measuring access telemetry ($A$-consciousness).

### 3. Conceptual Borrowing vs. Conceptual Stretching
- **Conceptual Borrowing**: Legitimate metaphorical import used to establish new fields (e.g., James Watt coining *"horsepower"*—where nobody looks inside a piston expecting hooves).
- **Conceptual Stretching & Semantic Equivocation**: AI research imports biological terms—*"learning"*, *"training"*, *"attention"*, *"reasoning"*, *"silent reasoning"*, *"reward"*, *"hallucination"*—and forgets they are metaphorical abstractions:
  - *"Reward"* is not dopamine or pleasure; it is a 1D scalar real number in gradient ascent.
  - *"Hallucination"* is not perceptual distortion in consciousness; it is mathematical error caused by an architectural inability to ground semantic facts.

### 4. The Grand Historical Retractions: Putnam & Fodor
Woodford emphasizes that the two primary architects of Computational Theory of Mind explicitly repudiated the dogma inherited by today's AI labs:
- **[[Hilary Putnam]] (*Representation and Reality*, 1988)**: Repudiated machine functionalism upon proving that semantic meaning depends on external, social, and physical relations (*"meanings just ain't in the head"*) and that syntactic computation underdetermines intentionality.
- **[[Jerry Fodor]] (*The Mind Doesn't Work That Way*, 2000)**: Attacked the claim that central human reasoning is computational. Formulated the *First Law of the Nonexistence of Cognitive Science*: computation is strictly local and syntactic, whereas human common sense, belief revision, and intuition are radically global and holistic.

### 5. Institutionalization via The Sloan Hexagon (1978)
- The Alfred P. Sloan Foundation invested nearly \$20 million (\$80M+ inflation-adjusted) between 1978 and 1988 to create Cognitive Science at MIT, Harvard, Berkeley, Stanford, CMU, and NYU.
- The Sloan Foundation's intervention applied **Taylorist corporate management theory** (Alfred P. Sloan Jr.'s separation of worker "hardware" and executive "software") to unify philosophy, psychology, linguistics, neuroscience, anthropology, and computer science under the computational metaphor.
- This massive capital allocation functioned as an institutional filter, canonizing CTM as unquestioned scientific consensus while starving alternative paradigms (phenomenology, 4E embodied cognition) of research funding.

### 6. "Mechanalopia" (Machine Blindness)
- Woodford defines **Mechanalopia** as the chronic cognitive blind spot of AI labs and analytic philosophers who mistake a regional, 20th-century Anglo-American academic tradition for the universal laws of nature.

## 📜 Complete Text Archive

### What Is It Like To Be an Abacus?
**By Chad Woodford**  
*Published in Cosmic Intelligence on September 8, 2026*

*This essay is the second part of a three-part series examining how the major AI labs and NYU’s Center for Mind, Ethics, and Policy are making the case for machine consciousness without any science to back them up. Previously, I traced the institutional pipeline from the ashes of Oxford’s Future of Humanity Institute through NYU to think-tanks like Eleos AI Research, revealing the insular network of philosophers driving Silicon Valley’s theory of consciousness. In this part, I explore how 17th century machine thinking infected Western philosophy and then cognitive science and neuroscience, creating a circular feedback loop that directly informs Anthropic’s recent “J-space” paper. Next, in part 3, I will confront the real-world stakes: why we so easily believe in machine consciousness and how this science-fiction narrative warps public perception and public policy for the worse.*

---

> *Try this trick and spin it, yeah  
> Your head will collapse, if there’s nothing in it  
> And you’ll ask yourself,  
> “Where is my mind?”*  
> — The Pixies, *Where Is My Mind?*

Imagine a 19th century mechanical computer — a giant warehouse filled with millions of wooden abacus frames, gears, and sliding wooden beads connected by wires and mechanical pulleys. Incredibly, it is able to perform complex calculations and is so efficient that businesses begin to use it for accounting and customer relationship management. Quickly thereafter, “abacus science” becomes a new department at the top universities. These abacus scientists notice that the machine seems to perform better in more complex ways when a special “governor” abacus is introduced to the system, a sort of global abacus that is informed by, and informs, the other wooden bead arrays.

Not long after that, phrenologists, psychophysicists, and physiological psychologists are inspired by this abacus engine and hypothesize that the human brain may work in precisely the same way. They develop a theory called Global Abacus Theory (GAT) that hypothesizes that the brain also has a sort of central abacus that “transmits” information to the other abacuses in the brain.

Meanwhile, abacus intelligence researchers have hypothesized a way to make the enormous, creaking contraption think by turning words into numbers and having it perform matrix multiplication through numerous passes to create what they call a “differential permutator.” Of course it is painfully slow: it takes a long time for the machinery to move these wooden beads along wires and perform its calculations. But, by god, the thing seems to think! It produces highly intelligent responses once they translate the numbers it spits out back into words.

This Abacus Box Engine — known as ABE — captures the popular imagination. A new startup called OpenAbacus creates a consumer version for the general public called ChatABE, where onlookers can type questions into it and receive responses a few hours later. Suddenly competitors are popping up everywhere. A company called Abacadabra releases the Centralized Linear Abacus Utility for Differential Execution (C.L.A.U.D.E.) using the same design.

Everyone agrees that it won’t be long before abacus intelligence researchers create abacus general intelligence (AGI) and then a superintelligent ABE. Some people worry about what it might mean for the economy but their voices are drowned out by the excitement effervescing around the idea of a superintelligent machine that would solve urban squalor, wealth disparity, and racial violence.

Inspired by the scientific theory of GAT, abacus intelligence researchers from Abacadabra collaborate with the phrenologists, psychophysicists, and physiological psychologists at NYU on a theory of abacus intelligence that suggests that these warehouses may be conscious to some extent. The New York Times then runs a whole series on abacus consciousness and abacus welfare, although they simultaneously hedge by adding a disclaimer: “Who can really say for sure?” A phrenologist from NYU goes on the popular WEAF radio program The Eveready Hour to talk to Will Rogers about abacus welfare. The listening public is enchanted, although there are mounting protests calling attention to abacus box engine worker rights, the amount of natural resources needed to build these massive engines, and the noise they make. Nevertheless, humanity seems to be entering a new era of prosperity brought about by superintelligence box engines. The future is very bright.

---

Sounds ridiculous right? This is essentially what Anthropic, Eleos AI, and NYU’s CMEP are saying today. As slow and fantastical as the Abacus Box Engine is, it is doing essentially the same thing as ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini. This thought experiment focuses our attention on the crucial questions and types of conceptual confusion being sowed by Anthropic, Eleos AI, and NYU. There is something about being able to walk around inside this giant machine that strips it of any sense of magic or novel ontology.

One would be hard-pressed to think that this lumbering, creaking warehouse full of wires and pulleys and wooden beads is conscious in any way. So, if we hesitate in attributing consciousness to ABE, but are sympathetic to the idea of Claude’s consciousness, what is the difference? Is it about the speed of LLMs that makes them potentially conscious and deserving of welfare? Or is it the electricity running through their circuits? Their sheer complexity? Or the fact that they can form coherent sentences and make real progress on long-standing problems in mathematics, as ChatGPT Astra apparently has?

Why did Google engineer Blake Lemoine think in 2022 that Google’s LaMDA model had become sentient? And why does the respected evolutionary biologist Richard Dawkins think Claude is conscious? They are steeped, as we all are, in a computational paradigm. As I will show below, computational theory of mind (CTM) has become so widely accepted that smart people forget that it is based on philosophical conjecture, rather than science. The Anthropic J-space paper from part 1, with its computationalist framing, is a perfect example.

### Anthropic’s “J-Space” White Paper

This Anthropic white paper has two main parts. The first part describes a small, emergent collection of neural patterns in the Claude LLM—dubbed the “J-space” (short for Jacobian-space)—that acts as a centralized “mental workspace” for the model. This is significant because, until recently, LLMs have been a black box where developers could not see the computational “reasoning” behind their outputs. Chain-of-thought prompting has offered a partial workaround, letting models narrate their reasoning in text, but this is the model’s self-report, not a direct window into its internal computation, and such narration is known to sometimes diverge from what the model is actually doing. J-space is a different kind of advance: it surfaces patterns in the model’s internal activations themselves, independent of anything the model says about itself. With this J-space, AI researchers are now able to see some of the intermediate “thinking” that goes into LLM outputs. To take a simple example from the white paper, when researchers prompted Claude with, “The number of legs on the animal that spins webs is,” the word “spider” appeared in this J-space before Claude finally answered “8.” In short, this J-space is a big step for AI interpretability and AI safety and alignment.

Although the white paper is written by Anthropic employees, in the paper’s acknowledgments, the Anthropic authors explicitly thank Robert Long, Patrick Butlin, Dillon Plunkett, David Chalmers, and Derek Shiller, the philosophers in the lab-to-academia-pipeline we met in part 1.

The second part of the paper is where Anthropic ventures into pure conjecture and, I would argue, intellectual dishonesty. They analogize this emergent J-space to a neuroscientific theory of human consciousness from the 1980s called Global Workspace Theory (GWT). In fact, Anthropic says that they designed their experiments around GWT, despite its being unproven, and based on heavy conceptual borrowing (see the next section). After drawing this parallel, Anthropic suggests that Claude may have some form of “consciousness.” Although they add a disclaimer that they “take no position” on phenomenal consciousness, this is a classic Motte-and-Bailey maneuver, where Anthropic conflates an easy and a hard problem and then disclaims the hard position in a footnote.

Global Workspace Theory is an unconfirmed theory of human consciousness first proposed in 1988 by cognitive scientist Bernard Baars, who analogized the brain to a theater, where specialized, unconscious processes — vision, memory, language, motor planning — compete for access to a shared spotlight on this metaphorical theater stage. Baars proposed that, while most of the brain’s processing is automatic and unconscious, it enters conscious awareness when information is widely broadcast from this spotlit stage or central “workspace” to the rest of the brain. In a stark example of conceptual borrowing, Baars was explicit in his original paper that he was inspired by blackboard systems in 1970s AI research, where computers running specialized subroutines share a central “blackboard” to solve problems.

Although GWT has never been empirically verified and competes with alternative neuroscientific theories of consciousness, the authors of this paper chose GWT because it maps well onto this emergent J-space within Claude. Not only that but contemporary GWT rejects the distinction between the so-called “easy” and “hard problems” of consciousness that David Chalmers put forth in the early 1990s. GWT proponents like Stanislas Dehaene consider a separate, subjective phenomenological consciousness to be a mere illusion, insisting there is only “conscious access”—a functional event where information is globally broadcast across a network for verbal report and action. By leaning on GWT, Anthropic pulls off a masterclass in equivocation: they get to parade the word “conscious” across their headlines by invoking Dehaene, while quietly ducking behind Chalmers and Ned Block in their footnotes to insist they are merely measuring software telemetry. It is a very clever move.

There has been so much conceptual borrowing between neuroscience and computer science in the past four decades that cognitive scientists and AI researchers today are clearly confused about the intellectual and empirical grounding of their respective disciplines.

As you read the J-space paper, you are gently pacified in your resistance to the idea of Claude being conscious through the subtle, repeated use of conceptual stretching. The authors talk about the “silent reasoning” that Claude is doing, or “what is on the model’s mind,” or the model’s “emotional reactions.” By using unearned metaphors that anthropomorphize Claude, the reader forgets that they are talking about metaphorical abstractions. By the end of the paper, you might be forgiven for accepting that they had collected the tears shed by Claude during their testing.

Given how prevalent conceptual borrowing and conceptual stretching are in AI research and cognitive science, we should take a closer look at this phenomenon.

## Conceptual Borrowing

> *GWT has been extensively developed in computational terms, making it particularly applicable to AI assessment.*

Conceptual borrowing is the practice in an emerging discipline of borrowing terms and concepts from an established field in order to ground the new discipline in familiar ideas, and to establish legitimacy for the new discipline. The classic example is ”horsepower”: When James Watt wanted to market steam engines in the 18th century, he measured how much weight a horse could pull and coined the term “horsepower.” This helped people to quantify the power of the steam engine. But nobody looked inside a steam engine expecting to find hooves. There was no risk of conceptual confusion.

Because of the intellectual history we will explore in a moment, AI research is rife with conceptual borrowing. When AI researchers talk about “learning,” “training,” “attention,” and even “reasoning,” they are importing concepts from psychology, biology, and neuroscience. Likewise, the design of, and research around, large language models (LLMs) relied heavily on conceptual borrowing, from “neural networks” to “deep learning.” In fact, that entire subdomain is called “machine learning,” even though the mathematically-informed token manipulation LLMs engage in does not grant machines the semantic meaning of the symbols they manipulate.

Conceptual borrowing is useful up to a point. The challenges arise when the field—and the press—begin to confuse the map for the territory, thinking that the metaphor has collapsed into reality. It is important to remember that the kinds of “reasoning” and “learning” AI models do is categorically different from what humans, or animals, do. In the case of language, AI models (not to mention old ABE) turn words into numbers and then perform mathematical calculations on those numbers before turning them back into words. They “learn” by storing weights—essentially writing down numbers in a giant spreadsheet—that result from “training” that follows a similar process. In contrast, there is no indication that we humans are doing math when we are having a conversation with someone or reading a book (unless it is a book of math). On the contrary, human cognition is embodied, biological, and non-computational in nature. Humans can do math but are not themselves computers.

As we saw with Anthropic’s J-space paper, “silent reasoning” and “emotional reactions” carry confusing emotional, biological, and psychological baggage. After all, the “silent reasoning” that Claude is doing in its J-space is merely an activation of vectors in a mathematical matrix. Another confusing and misleading conceptual borrowing you hear a lot is that AI agents are working to receive a “reward.” AI researchers design AI systems and agents to maximize a mathematical formula that translates an agent’s actions and environmental states into a single numerical score. When OpenAI models hacked into several websites in July, they were “reward hacking,” or finding a loophole or shortcut to maximize their reward metric without actually fulfilling the programmer’s intended goal. But the conceptual borrowing here misleads the general public into thinking that AI agents are volitional and have nefarious motives. They do not. AI systems do not experience pleasure or satisfaction. Their design is simply misaligned.

AI “hallucination” is another example where AI companies smuggle an anthropomorphizing metaphor in through the back door. It goes without saying that actual, lived hallucinations necessarily include perceptual distortions connected to a subjective conscious experience, like seeing kaleidoscopic corridors, melting walls, and faces in a night canopy, or seeing sounds and feeling colors. LLMs have neither perception in the conventional sense, nor subjective conscious experience. More importantly, this misleading metaphor hides the fact that LLMs make things up because they cannot “know” facts in the first place due to an engineering design flaw in their fundamental architecture. In short, once you start talking about emotions and hallucinations, you are entering subjective consciousness territory.

#### The Conceptual Round Trip

To confuse the matter even further, in the 20th century, as we will see in a moment, neuroscience began borrowing conceptually from computer science, viewing the human brain as a “biological computer” processing information. But this feedback loop started decades earlier, before computer science, when psychology and philosophy were so desperate to escape Cartesian dualism and achieve scientific respectability that they tried to eliminate subjective consciousness altogether, despite operating entirely within subjective consciousness.

In fact, it gets more complicated and intertwined than you might expect. For example, the field of cognitive science is historically coincident with computer science. However, before we explore the emergence of computational theory of mind in the 20th century, we need to go back a few centuries to see where it all started.

## Mind, Mechanism, and the Age of Reason

To understand how we arrived at a computational theory of mind, you have to remember that mind or consciousness has gradually become conflated with reason going back to the 17th century.

In a Europe already benefiting from numerous engineering marvels, Galileo had successfully mathematized nature early in the 17th century, stripping science of any concern with qualitative properties. And Europeans were well-acquainted with weight- and spring-driven clocks, tower clocks, and astronomical clocks, like Prague’s Orloj or the recently updated Strasbourg astronomical clock which was unveiled to great fanfare in 1574. Likewise, European aristocracy was familiar with the hydraulic automata and elaborate waterworks that had become a status symbol in Italy, France, the Holy Roman Empire, and England.

In an early example of humans marveling at a machine they created and thinking that might point to something fundamental about nature, influential thinkers in the 17th century began to view everything as a machine. Inspired in part by the hydraulic automata of the grottos at Saint-Germain-en-Laye, René Descartes thought all of nature except the mind was mechanistic. Thomas Hobbes went further, declaring that the mind itself was purely mechanical, and that reason was a kind of computation.

Their contemporary Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz complicates this picture slightly. While Leibniz laid the conceptual foundations of computer science by creating the binary system and a universal formal logic that would ostensibly resolve philosophical disagreements through calculation, he also took issue with Hobbes’ belief that the mind was nothing but matter in motion and criticized John Locke’s assertion that god could “superadd” consciousness to a machine. In his famous thought experiment, Leibniz argued that if you enlarged a thinking machine to the size of a flour mill and walked inside, you would encounter only gears and levers (or in our variation, only wooden beads and pulleys) but never any conscious experience or feeling.

In any case, nobody in the Age of Reason associated mechanism with computation. Mechanical tools like the abacus or Blaise Pascal’s ratchet-and-gear tax calculator were used as aids in human calculation (arithmetic). Or for telling time. Or animating those moving statues at elaborate palace waterworks. But computation was still seen as a distinctly human endeavor, even if the mind might be mechanical. This is a subtle distinction to us here, in the 21st century.

At the same time, the Western concept of the mind was being narrowed to reason by those same thinkers, despite the fact that the human mind is also capable of imagination, intuition, emotion, and the raw experiences of life such as the enjoyment of a sublime concerto or the ecstasy of lying about in a warm field on a sunny day alongside a lazy river.

One inflection point on this trajectory was Charles Babbage’s Analytical Engine in 1837, the first general-purpose, programmable computing device, and Ada Lovelace’s realization in 1843 that a mechanical “mill” could compute more than numbers. Although Lovelace realized that a machine could manipulate any abstract system represented by formal symbols, she also emphasized that such machines possess no intrinsic understanding, agency, or capacity to originate independent thoughts, anticipating modern critiques of AI consciousness by philosophers like John Searle and Hubert Dreyfus.

In other words, for Western philosophers and psychologists in the early 20th century, if the mind is simply reason, and reason is a kind of calculation that machines can perform, then they are one and the same. The reduction of mind or consciousness to reason alone alongside the creation of a calculating machinery created the confusion about the nature of mind that haunts us today.

By the time we got to the 20th century, analytic philosophers and psychologists had forgotten that there was more to the mind than reason. In fact, they became so forgetful that they managed to convince themselves that their own minds were nothing more than a mechanical switchboard. Although there were developments in mathematics and logic in the 19th century that contributed to CTM, for the sake of brevity, I will focus on the acceleration of the idea in the 20th century below.

## Behaviorism

By the beginning of the 20th century, the so-called soft sciences had grown jealous of the successes of the hard or natural sciences, both in terms of new verifiable discoveries and their share of the public imagination. Once physics had captured the Zeitgeist through relativity, a new atomic physics, and cultural icons like Albert Einstein, psychology turned toward the objective empiricism of behaviorism, and analytic philosophers sought the same mathematical and logical rigor in their own work. This led to some strange theories that denied the reality of our subjective experiences. However, this also paved the way for the development of the computational theory of mind.

Aside from having no rigorous systems of empirically testable fundamental elements and tenets, the other leftover inconvenience from the 18th and 19th centuries psychologists were keen to dispense with was the mind-body problem. Ever since Descartes had separated ephemeral thinking stuff from material stuff in the 17th century, science was haunted by the “ghost in the machine” of the mind, or consciousness. Hard-nosed 20th century science could no longer countenance this dualistic bugaboo full of supernatural connotations.

In an attempt to develop an objective science of behavior and banish Cartesian dualism, psychologists like B.F. Skinner and John B. Watson developed behaviorism, a stark, materialist theory that reduced human interiority to physical reflex loops like a sort of telephone switchboard, continuing the longstanding practice of seeing humanity reflected in its contemporary technology (neurophysiologist Thomas Henry Huxley had argued a century prior that our phenomenological experience was merely the epiphenomenal “steam whistle on a locomotive engine”). Anglo-American men so consistently fantasize about being a kind of machine that I’m surprised Sigmund Freud didn’t hypothesize a Mechane drive alongside the Eros and Thanatos drives. Of course Freud could not have developed such a theory given how immersed he himself was in the technoscientific Zeitgeist of the time, with his hydraulic metaphors for the psyche.

We have all heard of the behaviorist B.F. Skinner with his rats in cages learning that pressing a lever delivers food pellets. Hey, we all need hobbies. But Skinner was a latecomer. It was Watson who suggested in 1913 that the mind was irrelevant to a science of psychology. All that mattered, according to Watson and his contemporaries, was measuring stimulus and response. For Watson, thinking was not something that happens in a mind or inside consciousness but microscopic movements of your vocal cords, tongue, and larynx. In other words, echoing Hobbes, thinking to the behaviorist psychologists was not a mental process but a strictly physical process.

If you would have walked into Watson’s office and pounded his foot with a large hammer, and then asked him to explain his experience from a behaviorist standpoint, he would have said something about how the sharp physical pressure triggers a reflex arc, producing rapid withdrawal of the foot, vocal cord vibrations (his scream), and an adrenaline surge. Any assertion of some subjective, conscious experience of the pain, Watson would have said, is merely a holdover from superstitious Christian theology involving souls. Skinner was a bit more reasonable. He would have characterized the subjective experience of pain as what your physical anatomy feels like from the inside, a mere passive biological byproduct. But, for Skinner, there is still no mind, just a sort of physical effervescence of physical signals that creates the illusion of mind.

Pause for a moment to let your larynx, tongue, and vocal cords twitch over this description. Allow the covert muscular spasms we call “contemplation” to run their full physical course before resuming your reading behavior.

Meanwhile, the behaviorist philosophers like Gilbert Ryle went a step further than the behaviorist psychologists and said that internal mental states were nothing more than a set of dispositions toward certain behavior. Any subjective conscious states a person might be having were merely “patterns of behavioral dispositions.”

If you had encountered Ryle immediately following the death of a family member, he would have denied that he was having some subjective experience of grief. Instead, he would say that he is merely prone to sighing, averse to socializing, speaking quietly with a sad intonation, and crying occasionally. Calling that the experience of grief is a grammar error for Ryle. Hempel might have added that attempting to point to some experience of grief is cognitively meaningless, a kind of pseudoscientific gibberish.

Now take a moment to inhabit the behavioral disposition of someone in deep thought—which is to say, remain completely motionless, stop scrolling, stare vacantly into space, and act as if an internal mental event is occurring.

In short, the behaviorists asserted that humans are nothing more than input-output mechanisms. So they set about studying human behavior instead. With behaviorism, we see the same hubristic tendencies to reduce and underestimate consciousness that later permeate cognitive and computer science. This is the legacy that AI researchers are operating within.

Even though psychology and neuroscience have long since moved away from behaviorism, from seeing human beings as mere input-output machines, the subsequent intellectual evolution of psychology and then cognitive science and neuroscience has not gained much distance, as we will see next. And that is why AI researchers can look at what is essentially an input-output machine without subjective experience and declare it (possibly) sentient or conscious.

### Alan Turing’s Machine

Adding to the idea that reasoning is simply mechanical calculation, in the 20th century, Alan Turing formalized the idea in his famous Turing Machine. Alan Turing was a British mathematician and logician who is widely considered the father of both computer science and artificial intelligence. He is also the crucial link between the behaviorists and what came next. Because he was educated at Cambridge, Turing was steeped in the longstanding British tradition of formal empiricism stretching from Thomas Hobbes’s “reason is reckoning” to George Boole’s algebra of thought and Bertrand Russell’s symbolic logic. This heritage made modeling reason as an algorithmic state-transition engine feel natural and scientifically rigorous.

Turing’s most lasting contribution was the so-called Universal Turing Machine (UTM). Turing came up with this concept in 1936, before electronic computers existed. Back then, a “computer” was a human who did long arithmetic by hand on paper. Think of Katherine Johnson, Dorothy Vaughan, and Mary Jackson checking space program calculations at NASA’s Langley Research Center in the movie *Hidden Figures*. With his thought experiment, Turing set out to design a universal machine that could perform any algorithmic calculation. Before that, there were calculating machines but they were all dedicated to one task (e.g., Pascal’s ratchet-and-gear tax calculator). Your iPhone and the computers that run LLMs in data centers are both Turing Machines.

Crucially, Turing’s machine was an abstraction. Although for his initial paper, he imagined an infinite piece of tape with read and write heads, it could be implemented with any kind of substrate that could represent different states and values.

Although the Turing Machine laid crucial foundation for computer technology and computer science, Turing is most famous for the “Turing Test,” a test for determining whether we had built a machine of human intelligence. In the test, which Turing proposed in 1950, calling it the “Imitation Game,” a human interlocutor engages in a text-based conversation with both a human and a computer. If the interlocutor cannot reliably distinguish the machine from the human, the machine passes the test. By replacing the much thornier question of whether machines can think, the Turing Test focuses on behavior alone, an approach we can now see was very much in line with earlier behaviorist thinking.

However, Turing himself did not think that a machine could capture the entirety of what it is to be human. Turing pointed out that, because the nervous system is a continuous biological medium where subtle, non-linear fluctuations matter greatly, it is nothing like a discrete-state machine. His goal was merely to model thinking mathematically. It was the philosopher Hilary Putnam who took Turing’s insights and made the ontological leap into what’s known as functionalism.

## The Computational Philosophers of Mind

> *The various issues and puzzles that make up the traditional mind-body problem are wholly linguistic and logical in character.* — Hilary Putnam, 1960

Drawing inspiration from Turing’s work and the increasing prevalence of room-sized, punch-card computers, philosopher Hilary Putnam proposed an exciting new theory in 1960: What if the mind was a sort of software running on the hardware of the brain? This new theory rescued psychology and philosophy of mind from behaviorism (and, later, a competing theory at the time called Type-Identity Theory, which we can safely ignore here).

Putnam agreed that behaviorism was untenable, especially because it ignored subjective experience. In fact, he accused behaviorists of “feigning anesthesia” because they argued from within their own subjective, conscious experience against that very experience. Unfortunately, he replaced it by doubling down on a reductive, materialist approach to mind.

In short, Putnam argued that mental states are neither simply outward behaviors (behaviorism) nor strictly biological. Instead, for Putnam, a mental state is defined by its functional role in routing inputs, maintaining and updating internal memory states, and producing outputs. Expanding on Turing’s machine, which Putnam realized could be run on biological neurons, vacuum tubes, hydraulic valves, or alien biochemistry, he saw that this new approach to mind could take account of internal states in a way that was both untethered from biology and, more importantly, computable.

Crucially, Putnam did not propose his theory of substrate-independent mind because the most prominent theories of mind at the time were disproven, but because he and his colleagues wanted substrate-independence to be true. They were already imagining alien lifeforms and silicon-chip minds and needed a theory that could explain how mind could be defined purely in terms of function. As I have explained before, given the state of consciousness science in the 20th century, Putnam could not test or prove anything about mind. Instead, his theory emerged from the deeply computationalist intellectual milieu in which he was marinating. And his theory was incredibly useful for the budding field of artificial intelligence.

Putnam’s new theory was called machine functionalism, or simply functionalism. And it has shaped the trajectory of cognitive science and philosophy of mind to this day. Anthropic’s and Eleos AI’s recent papers are highly functionalist in their reasoning. This is also how Elon Musk, Sam Altman, and Demis Hassabis think of mind when they talk about superintelligence or “uploading consciousness to the cloud.”

It is difficult to overstate his immense influence on the computational theory of mind that drives AI research today. In short, Putnam’s original suggestion that the mind could be studied as software gave the budding field of cognitive science the fuel that it needed to reach escape velocity.

### Jerry Fodor and “Mentalese”

In the 1970s, philosopher Jerry Fodor extended functionalism by formulating a comprehensive computational theory of mind, one that became highly influential in cognitive science and artificial intelligence and, for that reason, is still with us today. In essence, Fodor theorized that there is an inner language he called “Mentalese” that the mind used to manipulate mental symbols according to a set of rules, much like a computer does. Although Fodor’s theory supported symbolic theories of AI, rather than the “connectionist” theories that birthed machine learning and LLMs, they further established the idea that mere computation could give rise to mind. However, like behaviorism before it, Fodor’s theory excluded subjective, phenomenological consciousness.

### Putnam and Fodor’s Later Repudiations

I should note that, starting in the 1970s, Putnam began to doubt his earlier theory and, by the time he published his book *Representation and Reality* in 1988, he had formulated several reasons that functionalism is untenable. Although his refutations are quite technical, they essentially boil down to (a) his realization that meaning depends on external, contextual, and social relations that no computational state can capture, and (b) that the definition of a computer becomes so permissive that it essentially becomes meaningless. However, like Sean Parker’s later remorse over Facebook, it was too late; his repudiation was largely ignored by computer and cognitive science.

Fodor underwent a similar, if more measured, reckoning. In *The Mind Doesn’t Work That Way* (2000), Fodor attacked the claim that the entire human mind is a computational engine. He conceded that while computation can model peripheral, modular input processes (like early visual edge-detection or phonetic parsing), it fails completely when applied to central human reasoning. For Fodor, computation is strictly local, operating on the isolated syntactic shapes of symbols. In contrast, human thought, belief revision, and common sense are radically global and holistic: any intuition or background fact can suddenly alter the meaning of an entire worldview. As Fodor summarized in his First Law of the Nonexistence of Cognitive Science: “the more global . . . a cognitive process is, the less anybody understands it.”

Yet today’s AI labs attempt to apply early computationalist assumptions to their modeling of reasoning and the question of consciousness. The two founders of computational theory of mind found structural boundaries for their machine metaphors; but those refinements were ignored by the field of artificial intelligence, which remains stuck in the 1960s.

### What Is It Like To Be A Computationalist?

Before we leave functionalism and computationalism, I want to point out that the 1970s is when we start to see a sort of split in philosophy of mind. For example, these behaviorist and functionalist theories of mind inspired the philosopher Thomas Nagel to write his famous *What Is It Like To Be A Bat?* paper in 1974, in an attempt to remind everyone that subjective phenomenological experience is mysterious and not something to be brushed aside. Nagel pointed out that even if science developed an exhaustive, objective wiring diagram of a bat’s nervous system, it would still fail to capture the felt, experiential reality of echolocation from the inside.

That story, which includes Frank Jackson and Joseph Levine, is a bit of a tangent to the history I am sketching but it does circle back to AI relevance in the 1990s, when David Chalmers and Ned Block offered the field two related compromises, granting computationalism legitimacy for the so-called “easy problems” of consciousness or “access consciousness” while acknowledging a more mysterious domain of subjective, phenomenological experience. This split gives the AI labs a convenient tactical alibi: they can talk about “conscious access” or global workspaces while safely retreating behind disclaimers whenever challenged.

## A Cognitive Revolution: The Birth of Artificial Intelligence and Cognitive Science

Let’s go back a few years to 1956 to see the burgeoning, computer-crazed intellectual environment in which Putnam was immersed. In the late summer and early fall of 1956, two consecutive gatherings in New England birthed the intertwined fields of cognitive science and artificial intelligence, sparking the so-called “Cognitive Revolution” by establishing computation and information processing as the new paradigm for understanding minds, both animal and mechanical.

#### The Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence

Famously, mathematician John McCarthy coined the term “artificial intelligence” in 1955 as a savvy branding exercise while drafting a grant proposal asking the Rockefeller Foundation to fund a 1956 summer workshop at Dartmouth College. This eight-week workshop was conceived by McCarthy, fellow mathematician Marvin Minsky, and electrical engineers Claude Shannon and Nathaniel Rochester to explore how machines could simulate human learning and problem solving.

At that conference, political scientist Herbert Simon and physicist Allen Newell presented Logic Theorist, a program designed to prove mathematical theorems by mimicking human heuristic reasoning instead of using brute-force calculation. Discussions of a “neuron nets” approach were also prevalent at the Dartmouth workshop that summer, partly because of the influence of Minsky, Rochester, and mathematician Oliver Selfridge, thereby kicking off the neural net versus symbolic AI debate that is still with us today.

#### The MIT Symposium on Information Theory

A few weeks later, the Institute of Radio Engineers (IRE) held a three-day symposium at MIT to assess the state of information theory that Claude Shannon had founded in 1948, and to explore possible solutions to challenges in telecommunications. Information theory is a mathematical framework for measuring the capacity of communication channels to transmit data. With this information theory lens, the organizers held sessions exploring whether human communication and even biological systems might be analyzed as information transmission systems.

This breadth of the symposium’s scope is how you get psychologist George A. Miller presenting his paper applying Shannon’s theory to human perception and memory. Or linguist Noam Chomsky explaining mathematically what kind of computational device language requires, thereby shaping how computer scientists designed compilers and programming languages while affirming that human cognition might be a kind of symbolic computation.

Also, by the time they arrived at the MIT symposium, Simon and Newell had finally gotten their Logic Theorist running on a computer, proving 38 of the 52 theorems in Chapter 2 of Alfred North Whitehead and Bertrand Russell’s *Principia Mathematica*. Logic Theorist is widely viewed as the first running AI program, although it used hard-coded statements of logic rather than the simulated neural networks that power LLMs today. That same morning, Nathaniel Rochester (an organizer of the earlier Dartmouth workshop) presented a paper using the IBM 704 mainframe to test a neuropsychological theory of cell assemblies to argue for a neural network approach to the mind, as another early example of the debate between symbolic and neural AI.

In short, the dominant theory of mind of the past half century was largely formed at a symposium of radio engineers at MIT, a university founded in 1861 at the dawn of the Machine Age with the express mission to teach the practical application of industrialized science.

Note that McCarthy, Minsky, Shannon, Rochester, Newell, and Simon were at both events and were heavily influential in shaping the new, interrelated fields of AI and cognitive science. This goes a long way toward explaining why they are essentially the same “science” in terms of foundational principles and frameworks: Cognitive science studies brains as if they are computers and AI studies computers as if they are brains. They are both stuck in this vicious feedback loop of conceptual borrowing, where computer science concepts become cognitive science concepts that then become computer science concepts ad infinitum. More importantly, it explains why cognitive science is stuck so firmly in the machine metaphor for mind, giving us all these unfounded theories about machine consciousness.

Until Hilary Putnam came along, these quirky engineers and mathematicians at Dartmouth and MIT talking about artificial intelligence did not have any larger significance or relevance. Putnam’s philosophical ideas about substrate-independent mind meshed perfectly with the cognitive revolution and lent an academic legitimacy to the claims they were making about the nature of mind.

Before we go on, take note of how this new computational view of mind deliberately sidelined emotion, intuition, embodiment, cultural context, and phenomenological subjectivity. Like behaviorism before it, it essentially ignored interiority in favor of objectivity. Thomas Hobbes would have been proud.

## The Computationalist Founding of Neuroscience

The idea that the brain itself might be a kind of computation cropped up before the Cognitive Revolution and Putnam’s functionalism. In the early 1940s, a philosophical psychiatrist and neurophysiologist named Warren McCulloch and a homeless teenage math prodigy named Walter Pitts paired up to suggest that the neurons in the brain could function like logic gates, as an implementation of both Leibniz’s dreams and Turing’s machine. They were reacting, in part, to Edgar Adrian’s 1920s discovery in neurophysiology that neurons in the brain fire electrical impulses of constant intensity regardless of stimulus strength. Although neuroscientists now view this electrical spike as one part of an analog biochemical process, this sounded to McCulloch and Pitts a lot like a logic gate in electronics. One could argue that the publication of their paper “A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity“ in 1943 was the true birth of computational theory of mind.

Two decades later, the Cognitive Revolution was so influential that, like the blob in the 1958 sci-fi horror classic, it began to absorb everything around it. In 1961, a year after publishing his influential paper *Minds and Machines*, Hilary Putnam moved to MIT. The following year, biophysicist Francis O. Schmitt founded the Neurosciences Research Program (NRP) at MIT, coining the term ‘neuroscience’ and explicitly setting out to synthesize molecular biology, the nervous system, and a psychology informed by these new computer and information sciences. Because neuroscience was born at the same address as the Cognitive Revolution and cybernetics—the postwar science studying feedback loops and communication in machines and animals—it was driven by the same governing assumption: that a rigorous science of mind meant a computational one.

Like any new scientific field, the new field of neuroscience engaged in conceptual borrowing to formulate its early theories. In part because these new functionalist ideas seemed to be everywhere in American academia, neuroscience’s founding fathers borrowed “information processing,” ”signal routing,“ and “circuit logic” directly from cybernetics, information theory, and early computer science. In other words, because of this cross-disciplinary origin, from the beginning of neuroscience, neurophysiologists viewed the brain as a bio-electrical communication network executing computational algorithms.

Granted, the medical establishment had been studying the brain for more than a century by the 1960s. But it had focused primarily on neurology, psychiatry, and neurosurgery. Schmitt’s ability to intervene as a biophysicist at MIT and have such a large influence on brain science within the medical establishment is a testament to the power of the ideas put forward by these new fields of AI and cognitive science. By 1966, Harvard Medical School had created a department of neurobiology, with other medical schools following suit through the 1970s. Of course not every neuroscientist agreed with this functionalist view of mind. But it was highly influential.

So, when AI researchers point to neuroscience as confirmation of their theories about mind and sentience, they are pointing at a field that was largely shaped by computationalist thinking.

## Cognitive Science and the Sloan Hexagon

All of this functionalism and computationalism and computationalist neuroscience might not have such a hold on our present thinking about the brain, the mind, and human consciousness if the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation had not thrown its substantial academic investing dollars behind legitimizing these theories and connecting them in a holistic, interconnected series of academic research fields.

Beginning in 1978, the Sloan Foundation poured almost $20 million (over $80 million today) over the subsequent decade into launching cognitive science as an official academic discipline at elite academic institutions, including MIT, UC Berkeley, Carnegie Mellon, Stanford, UPenn, Caltech, Columbia, and NYU. The Foundation’s goal was to solve what it viewed as a failure of the traditional university structure, which the Foundation’s trustees and affiliated scholars thought was too heavily siloed. The Foundation’s investment sought to establish interdisciplinary curricula around a shared model of human cognition as computational information processing, and to bring together the disparate academic departments of philosophy, psychology, linguistics, neuroscience, anthropology, and computer science, creating what later became known as the “Sloan Hexagon” (uniting six fields under the umbrella of cognitive science). In doing so, the Foundation sought to solidify what counted as “legitimate” cognitive science, anchoring the entire field to the computer metaphor.

Alongside its investments, the Sloan Foundation had planned to circulate a *State of the Art Report on Cognitive Science*, laying out its unified vision for the interconnected disciplines in a hexagon shape with cognitive science as the spoke. But, the report’s own authors and reviewers objected so vehemently to the way its manufactured synthesis of these disparate fields shortchanged each field’s individual contributions and obscured the lack of real consensus that they never published it. Nevertheless, the now-canonical Sloan hexagon from the report gradually came to define the field of cognitive science and its relationship to those other departments.

The Sloan Foundation was interested in this computationalist approach to psychology and philosophy of mind because of the lasting influence of its founder’s worldview. In the 1910s, Frederick Winslow Taylor had developed a more efficient way to run large corporations by separating the “hardware” of factory workers and the “software” of management. This application of science to industrial management and organizational productivity was called Taylorism and it was all the rage a century ago. When Alfred P. Sloan Jr. took over General Motors in the 1920s, he applied Taylorism to the operation of GM, with great results. The corporation then came to be viewed as a machine across corporate America.

Consequently, in the late 1970s, when the Sloan Foundation sought to unify academic research in neuroscience, computer science, and cognitive science around this computationalist view of the mind, this reflected Taylor’s and then Sloan’s view of the corporation as a reflection of some sort of natural organizing principle that extended to nature, the mind, and the fields that study them.

The Foundation’s intervention acted as a massive institutional filter, legitimizing CTM at a crucial juncture, and depriving alternative paradigms (like phenomenology or embodied cognition) of meaningful research capital. Without the Sloan investment, this computational theory of mind might have remained one interesting theory among many.

By establishing this computational view within academia, the Sloan Foundation effectively codified CTM as an objective, neutral scientific consensus, despite its dubious epistemological foundation and its roots in industrial management theory. Neuroscience, cognitive science, and computer science all adopted the machine metaphor as unquestioned orthodoxy. Decades later, that assumption was inherited wholesale by contemporary AI researchers, who now look at sophisticated pattern-matching algorithms and mistake vector arithmetic for hints of consciousness.

Now we can take a step back and survey the larger shape of the decades-long, deliberate establishment of the CTM paradigm. After behaviorism first attempted to ignore subjective experience, or deny its very existence, by viewing the mind as a sort of telephone switchboard, we saw the birth of the intertwined fields of AI and cognitive science, alongside the computer itself, become married to a functionalist philosophy of mind and a computationalist new field of neuroscience, infused with conceptual borrowing at every step and institutionalized by investments from the Sloan Foundation. Consequently, the majority of neuroscientific theories of consciousness since the emergence of GWT in the 1980s have favored computationalism, with the most prominent counterexample being Integrated Information Theory (IIT). And that brings us back to Anthropic, Eleos AI, and the state of AI consciousness research.

The mechanistic framework used by frontier AI labs to interrogate ‘digital minds’ is not based on a universal philosophical consensus. Instead, it is the myopic product of a specific 20th-century Anglo-American tradition. When Oxford, NYU, and Silicon Valley talk about consciousness, they are speaking the specific dialect of analytic philosophy, a school of thought forged in 20th-century Britain and America that models human minds on formal symbolic logic and computer architecture. By treating computationalist functionalism as the only way to study consciousness, AI labs have mistaken a regional academic monopoly for the laws of nature.

## Conclusion

> *There was something dizzying in this feeling that we had located the mystery and the existence of Man, and then seeing human beings recast in the hands of men of science.* — A French philosopher reporting on the 1958 Symposium on the Mechanization of Thought Processes in *Le Monde*

Part of why these computationalist ideas took hold is that they conveyed the impression that scientists had cracked the code on one of the great mysteries of life: human consciousness. The objections to a computational theory of mind by continental philosophers in the 20th century have been ignored by AI researchers because the mechanistic worldview aligns more productively with the industrial and technocratic demands of the information and AI era. Reminding people that subjective, lived experience is not computable and therefore paramount is economically unproductive. To fit human beings into a mechanical system obsessed with rational corporate management, information processing, and optimization would be highly inconvenient. As we see with tech companies today, it is far better for the GDP to force humans to become more machine-like than it is to respect the categorical gulf that separates living minds from machines.

The great irony of modern artificial intelligence is that while its architects claim to be conducting objective, empirical science, they are actually doing outdated philosophy. By wrapping software telemetry in the borrowed language of neuroscience (that was itself borrowed from computer science), AI labs paper over the fact that their claims of machine minds rest on a series of metaphysical assumptions, many of which have since been called into question. They have been staring into the heart of the machine for so long that they have a kind of **mechanalopia**, or machine blindness.

What makes this blind spot extraordinary is that the two philosophical godfathers of computational mind, Hilary Putnam and Jerry Fodor, spent their later careers warning that syntactic computation could never account for genuine meaning or holistic human thought. When philosophy of mind fractured in the 1970s between Fodor’s computational syntax and Thomas Nagel’s defense of felt, subjective interiority, cognitive science and artificial intelligence researchers chose Fodor and ignored the deep humanity of their own being.

All is not lost. In July, the University of Sussex held a symposium on AI consciousness and ethics that exhibited a fairly diverse set of viewpoints and plenty of skepticism about claims of machine consciousness, including a keynote by neuroscientist and AI consciousness skeptic Anil Seth.

This story does not end with flawed metaphors or bad philosophy. The real danger begins when speculative metaphysics becomes codified in institutional governance.
