# The Lived Experience of Meaning: Intellectual Feeling & Cognitive Phenomenology

> One point that Segall makes, drawing on Whitehead and Hegel, is that there is a phenomenological experience between sensory inputs and coherent thought expressed in words that is lacking in LLMs. Is that right? 

That captures Segall’s argument, with a crucial philosophical distinction regarding what *kind* of phenomenology is missing in machine models. #philosophy #reason #ai #phenomenology 

In Chapter VIII (*The Science of Machine Consciousness*), Segall directly addresses cognitive scientists (such as Elan Barenholtz) who claim LLMs lack consciousness simply because they lack physical bodies, sensory qualia, and spatial affordances. Segall agrees that LLMs lack sensory qualia, but he argues that sensory perception is not the *only* form of conscious experience. Drawing on Hegel and Whitehead, he points to a distinct **phenomenology of pure thinking**—an internal, lived current of conceptual activity that occurs as thought moves toward linguistic expression:

- **Hegel & Dialectical Phenomenology:** Segall uses Hegel to refute the idea that thinking is merely a mechanical loom stitching pre-given concepts onto raw sense data. In *Phenomenology of Spirit*, Hegel shows that conscious thought is an active, self-making process that cannot be reduced to sensory inputs. Living thought undergoes a dialectical movement—experiencing contradiction, self-negation, and reorientation (*sublation*) as it struggles to externalize itself into coherent words. That struggle is itself a lived, conscious experience.
- **Whitehead & Intellectual Feelings:** Segall draws on Whitehead’s account of "intellectual feelings" and "propositional feelings" to highlight the "vectorial" momentum of mind. When a human thinker composes a sentence, they feel an originative valuation—a directional pull, a conceptual lure, and a non-statistical appraisal of relevance—that guides how the thought finishes.

### Why LLMs Lack This Middle Current

An LLM skips this entire subjective middle phase. It operates solely on the fossilized, statistical sediment of text that human thinkers left behind.

While an LLM can calculate token probabilities or trace mathematical directions in high-dimensional vector space, its directional persistence "dies within a single word". Each output is a fresh, scalar computation rather than a continuous, self-determining movement of Reason.

The LLM reweaves the external syntax of thought, but it does not *live through* the dialectical tension or *feel* the conceptual lure that brought those words into existence.

## Intellectual Feeling

> In other words, in this philosophical tradition, "intellectual feeling" isn't exactly the same as the qualia of raw experiences like watching a beautiful sunset? It's a different, yet related, kind of conscious phenomenon in humans?

Precisely. In this tradition, "intellectual feeling" is a distinct, non-sensory (or trans-sensory) dimension of conscious experience that cannot be reduced to the raw, perceptual qualia of watching a sunset, tasting an apple, or feeling physical pain.

While mainstream cognitive science often restricts the definition of consciousness to sensory-motor qualia—the "what it is like" to receive perceptual inputs—Segall uses Hegel, Whitehead, and Ruyer to argue that **thinking itself has a lived, subjective phenomenology**.

### How Intellectual Feeling Differs from Sensory Qualia

| **Dimension**            | **Sensory Qualia (e.g., A Sunset)**                           | **Intellectual Feeling (e.g., Grasping an Idea)**                                                     |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Primary Domain**       | Perceptual fields, bodily sensation, and spatial affordances. | Meaning, logical direction, conceptual tension, and felt relevance.                                   |
| **Subjective Content**   | The vivid red of the sky, warmth on the skin, ambient sound.  | The "a-ha!" moment of understanding, the weight of a contradiction, the pull of an emerging sentence. |
| **Philosophical Ground** | Physical prehensions / sensory certainty.                     | Propositional feelings, dialectical sublation, and self-survey of themes.                             |

### Key Breakdown of the Concept Across the Triad

- **Whitehead’s "Intellectual Feelings":** For Whitehead, a "feeling" (*prehension*) is not merely an emotion or a physical touch, but any act of taking the world into account. An *intellectual feeling* occurs when a mind integrates physical/sensory data with conceptual possibilities (propositions). It is the conscious experience of feeling the contrast between *what is* and *what might be*—the subjective grasp of relevance, novelty, and truth.
- **Hegel’s Phenomenology of Thought:** Hegel refutes the empiricist assumption (from Locke and Hume) that concepts are merely "faded sensory pictures". He argues that pure thinking undergoes its own concrete experience. When you wrestle with a complex argument, feel the friction of a logical paradox, or experience the sudden reorientation of a breakthrough, you are living through a real, conscious phenomenology that is conceptual rather than sensory.
- **Ruyer’s Self-Survey of Themes:** For Ruyer, when a mind engages with a conceptual "theme" or ideal, it conducts an absolute self-survey (*survol*). This isn't observing an image with an inner eye; it is the immediate, non-local presence of the mind to its own active meaning.

### Why the Distinction Matters for AI

If consciousness were *only* sensory qualia, one could argue that an AI lacks consciousness simply because it lacks eyes, skin, and biological sensors.

By establishing that **intellectual feeling** is a real form of human consciousness, Segall exposes a deeper flaw in LLMs: even though LLMs deal exclusively with text and symbols (the domain of thought), they do not possess the *conceptual phenomenology* that produced those symbols. They process the fossilized syntax of intellectual feelings without ever undergoing the lived, interior current of meaning, valuation, or understanding.

## Other Thinkers In This Vein

> Were Hegal, Whitehead, and Ruyer the only philosophers to identify this kind of intellectual feeling?

No, Hegel, Whitehead, and Ruyer are far from the only thinkers to identify this phenomenon; they belong to a rich, continuous lineage in philosophy that recognizes a distinct, non-sensory phenomenology of thought—often referred to as **intellectual intuition**, **cognitive phenomenology**, or the **lived experience of meaning**.

Across the history of philosophy, numerous major thinkers have argued that the mind directly undergoes a conscious, qualitative experience when grasping concepts, logical structures, and meaning, entirely distinct from raw sensory qualia.

### 1. Classical & Medieval Foundations: Aristotle and Neoplatonism

- **Aristotle’s *Nous* (Active Intellect):** Aristotle distinguished between sensory perception (*aisthesis*) and the direct, non-sensory apprehension of forms by the mind (*nous*). For Aristotle, *nous* does not merely passively record sensory impressions; it actively grasps the essence or underlying intelligibility of things through a direct, lived act of intellectual realization.
- **Neoplatonism & Plotinus:** Plotinus described the experience of the *Intellectual Principle*—a state of luminous, immediate awareness where the mind does not look *at* concepts from the outside, but fully inhabits and coincides with the intelligible realm.

### 2. German Idealism: Fichte and Schelling (*Intellektuelle Anschauung*)

- Before Hegel, **Johann Gottlieb Fichte** and **Friedrich Schelling** made "intellectual intuition" (*intellektuelle Anschauung*) the cornerstone of their philosophies.
- Kant had argued that human cognition requires both sensory intuitions (perceptions) and concepts (understanding). Fichte and Schelling pushed further, asserting that the act of self-consciousness itself—the mind's immediate awareness of its own dynamic activity as it thinks—is an *intellectual intuition*. It is a conscious, non-sensory experience of the self in the act of willing and generating thought.

### 3. Phenomenology: Husserl and "Categorial Intuition"

- **Edmund Husserl**, the founder of modern phenomenology, introduced the concept of **categorial intuition** (*kategoriale Anschauung*).
- Husserl pointed out that when you see a "red apple," your eyes perceive the color red and the shape of the fruit (sensory data). But you also perceive that the apple *is* red, or that the state of affairs *exists*.
- For Husserl, logical relations, syntactical structures, and state-of-affairs ("is-ness") are not sensory inputs, yet we consciously *perceive* and *fulfill* them in experience. We directly experience the meaningful structure of the thought.

### 4. Pragmatism & Process Precursors: James, Peirce, and Bergson

- Segall explicitly aligns Whitehead and Ruyer with a cluster of late 19th- and early 20th-century thinkers who defended the creative immediacy of experience, including **William James**, **C. S. Peirce**, and **Henri Bergson**.
- **Henri Bergson** spoke of *intuition* as the method by which consciousness enters directly into the living movement of time (*durée*) and dynamic concepts, rather than viewing them through the static, spatialized lens of the analytical intellect.
- **William James** analyzed the "stream of thought," famously pointing out that we consciously experience the *transitive states* of thinking—the felt "fringe" of an idea, the subtle awareness of a vector leading toward a word, or the lived tension of trying to recall a forgotten name—long before the thought crystallizes into articulated words.

### 5. Contemporary Philosophy of Mind: "Cognitive Phenomenology"

In recent decades, a major debate has re-emerged in analytic philosophy between **sensory chauvinists** (who claim consciousness consists *only* of sensory-motor qualia) and advocates of **Cognitive Phenomenology** (such as Galen Strawson, Terence Horgan, David Pitt, and Michelle Montague):

- **The Hearing-Foreign-Language Thought Experiment:** Strawson asks you to imagine listening to a news broadcast in a language you do not understand, and then listening to the exact same broadcast after you have mastered the language. The raw auditory sensory qualia (the sound waves entering your ears) are identical in both cases. Yet, the second experience possesses a profound, additional conscious dimension: *the phenomenology of understanding the meaning*.
- **Proprietary Cognitive Qualia:** Philosophers in this camp argue that holding a belief, grasping a mathematical proof, or recognizing an ambiguity has a distinct, proprietary "what-it-is-like-ness" that cannot be reduced to inner speech, visual imagery, or bodily sensations.

### Why This Broad Lineage Matters

This broader tradition demonstrates that the separation between raw sensory inputs and the lived, internal current of meaning is not a fringe metaphysical claim. From Aristotle to modern phenomenology, thinkers have recognized that **meaning is lived through as an active, conscious event**.

This reinforces Segall's central critique: because functionalist AI paradigms assume that thinking is merely the formal manipulation of symbols, they mistake the *recorded footprint* of this cognitive lineage (text/syntax) for the *living activity* that experienced and generated it.

## What It's Like To Be a Bat

> So when Nagel wrote What's It's Like To Be a Bat, he wasn't talking about intellectual feeling, just raw phenomenal conscious experiences? 

Spot on. When Thomas Nagel published his landmark 1974 essay, *"What Is It Like to Be a Bat?"*, he was focused squarely on **perceptual and sensory qualia**.

Nagel picked the bat specifically because its primary sensory modality—**echolocation**—is completely foreign to human biology. His argument was that even if a neuroscientist mapped every physical neuron, acoustic reflection, and brain state in a bat, no amount of physical description could tell us *what it actually feels like from the inside* to perceive the world through sonar.

## How Nagel Fit into the Story

### 1. The Fight Against Physicalism

Nagel’s main target was computational and physicalist reductionism. He wanted to prove that objective science leaves out the subjective "what-it-is-like-ness" of experience. However, by choosing a bat’s sonar as his primary example, he anchored the debate in **sensory perception**.

### 2. The Unintended "Sensory Chauvinism"

Because Nagel’s paper became the foundational text for modern analytic discussions on consciousness, it unintentionally nudged late-20th-century philosophy of mind into focusing almost exclusively on sensory examples:

- What is it like to see red? (*Mary the Color Scientist*)
- What is it like to feel pain?
- What is it like to taste a lemon or hear a bat's sonar?

For decades, standard analytic philosophy largely bracketed out the inner, qualitative experience of **pure conceptual thinking**, assuming that thoughts were just silent language or abstract logic running in the background.

## The "What-it-is-like-ness" of Thinking

It wasn't until much later that advocates of **cognitive phenomenology** (like Galen Strawson, David Pitt, and Terence Horgan) hijacked Nagel’s famous phrase and turned it back toward thought itself. They argued:

> *Just as there is "something it is like" to perceive a bat's sonar or taste coffee, there is also **"something it is like" to understand a concept, to feel a logical contradiction, or to grasp an insight.***

If you read a complex sentence in a foreign language you don't speak, you have raw auditory sensory qualia. When you finally learn the language and read it again, the auditory sounds are identical, but the **conscious experience of understanding the meaning** is fundamentally different. That difference is the "intellectual feeling".

## Why This Matters for the AI Debates

This distinction is precisely where Segall’s monograph takes AI theorists to task:

1. **The Embodiment Trap:** Many contemporary AI researchers (like Elan Barenholtz, whom Segall cites) argue that Large Language Models aren't conscious because they lack bodies, sensory qualia, and spatial affordances. The implied assumption is: *"If we just give the LLM camera eyes, robotic ears, and tactile skin, it will finally become conscious like Nagel's bat."*
2. **Missing the Higher Axis:** Segall (drawing on Hegel, Whitehead, and Ruyer) argues that adding robotic sensors only addresses the *sensory* field. It completely misses the lived, non-sensory current of **intellectual phenomenology**—the dialectical tension, self-survey of meaning, and originative valuation that happens when a mind actually *thinks*.

Nagel showed us that a computer program can't know what it's like to *be a bat*. Segall, Hegel, and Whitehead are adding that a computer program also doesn't know what it's like to *think a thought*.
