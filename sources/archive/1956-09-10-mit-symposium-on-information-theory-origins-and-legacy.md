# The 1956 MIT Symposium on Information Theory: Origins, Institutional Backdrop, and the Catalysis of Cognitive Science

The 1956 MIT Symposium on Information Theory (held September 10–12, 1956) was originally conceived not as a forum on human psychology, but as an advanced technical summit for electrical engineering, mathematics, and telecommunications. Yet, by providing a shared formal language of signals, channels, and computation, it became the catalytic event that launched the Cognitive Revolution.

---

## 📡 Original Purpose: Codifying a New Mathematical Discipline

The symposium was organized to assess the state of **Information Theory** eight years after Claude Shannon published *"A Mathematical Theory of Communication"* in 1948.

* **Engineering Focus:** The primary objective was solving practical and theoretical problems in telecommunications: calculating channel capacity, designing error-correcting codes, optimizing data compression, filtering noise from radar signals, and securing military cryptography.
* **The "Universal Information" Ambition:** Organizers were eager to test whether Shannon’s mathematical definition of information—measuring bits and entropy regardless of semantic meaning—could serve as a universal quantitative framework. Because of this, the organizers deliberately set aside sessions to explore whether biological systems, speech, and human communication could also be analyzed as transmission channels.

---

## 🏛️ Why IRE (IEEE) Organized It and MIT Hosted It

* **The IRE Connection:** The conference was organized by the **IRE Professional Group on Information Theory (PGIT)**. (The Institute of Radio Engineers merged with the American Institute of Electrical Engineers in 1963 to form the modern **IEEE**, which is why the symposium and its published proceedings are cataloged under the IEEE name today). Formed in 1951, PGIT served as the professional society for researchers turning Shannon’s mathematical logic into applied engineering standards.
* **The MIT Epicenter:** MIT was the uncontested home of American communications research after World War II. Its **Research Laboratory of Electronics (RLE)**—the direct academic heir to the wartime MIT Radiation Laboratory—was supported by the military’s Joint Services Electronics Program (Army, Navy, Air Force).
* **Institutional Champions:** Figures like Claude Shannon (who held an appointment at MIT), Jerome Wiesner (head of RLE), and Peter Elias (chairman of PGIT and an MIT electrical engineering professor) were based in Cambridge. Hosting the event at MIT allowed the IRE to coordinate directly with RLE’s engineers and leverage MIT’s facilities, publishing the papers immediately in the September 1956 issue of *IRE Transactions on Information Theory*.

---

## ⚡ The Catalyst: September 11, 1956

The symposium changed intellectual history because the organizers invited scholars from outside electrical engineering who were applying computational ideas to human faculties. Cognitive psychologist George Miller famously designated **September 11, 1956**, as the official birth date of Cognitive Science due to three papers delivered back-to-back in MIT's halls:

* **Allen Newell and Herbert A. Simon (*The Logic Theory Machine*):** Presented the *Logic Theorist*, the first operational symbolic AI program. By demonstrating that a computer could use heuristic search trees to prove abstract mathematical theorems, they showed engineers and psychologists alike that computers were general-purpose physical symbol manipulators, not merely fast number-crunchers.
* **Noam Chomsky (*Three Models for the Description of Language*):** Chomsky mathematically demonstrated that human syntax cannot be produced by statistical Markov chains, associative transitions, or behaviorist stimulus-response loops. He proved that language generation requires innate, hierarchical, rule-governed computational grammars.
* **George A. Miller (*The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two*):** Miller applied Shannon's concept of channel capacity directly to human perception and short-term working memory. He showed that human memory exhibits a measurable processing ceiling (roughly $7 \pm 2$ "chunks"), treating the human mind as an information-processing channel with strict bandwidth bottlenecks.

---

## 🧠 Its Role in Birthing Cognitive Science

Before that afternoon, these thinkers worked in isolated disciplinary pockets: Newell and Simon were studying organizational decision-making and computer algorithms at RAND and Carnegie Mellon; Chomsky was an iconoclastic linguist at MIT; and Miller was an experimental psychologist at Harvard.

Listening to each other's presentations at the IRE symposium, they realized that they were modeling the exact same reality: human thought, language, and machine logic were all governed by the formal manipulation of internal symbols through rule-based programs. By importing communications engineering concepts (buffers, channels, noise, coding, and search algorithms) directly into the study of language and mind, the MIT symposium broke the grip of Skinnerian behaviorism and established the computational paradigm that defined the Cognitive Revolution.
