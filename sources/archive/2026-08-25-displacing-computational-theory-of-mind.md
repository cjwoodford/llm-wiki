# Displacing Computational Theory of Mind

See also [Steven Pinker Oh Boy](./Steven Pinker Oh Boy.md)

---

[The](https://share.gemini.google/8rRCN8gkNuSo) academic, literary, and cultural establishments of the late 20th and early 21st centuries have been deeply invested in the **Computational Theory of Mind (CTM)**.

The institutional accolades showered on Pinker—from National Academy of Sciences awards and Harvard/MIT professorships to Pulitzer nominations and major book prizes—reflect how thoroughly CTM became the "default setting" for modern intellectual life.

The institutional establishment committed so heavily to this specific paradigm for three major reasons:

### 1. It Solved a Crisis for "Hard Science" Psychology

Before computationalism took hold in the 1960s–1980s, academic psychology was caught between two unsatisfying extremes:

- **Behaviorism**, which treated the mind as an unobservable "black box" and refused to talk about inner states at all.
- **Cartesian Dualism or Psychoanalysis**, which felt too unscientific, mystical, or untestable for rigorous empirical science.

CTM offered the scientific establishment a way out. By defining mental processes as **information processing**, cognitive science could finally study inner states (beliefs, rules, perceptions) with mathematical rigor without invoking a soul or non-material substance. It made psychology look like computer science, which gave it instant prestige.

### 2. The Cultural Alignment with the Computer Age

Institutional awards and bestseller lists don't exist in a vacuum; they reflect the spirit of their era. As computers reshaped society in the late 20th century, treating the brain as "hardware" and the mind as "software" felt intellectually natural and forward-looking.

Pinker’s work landed at the exact moment when tech optimism was peaking. By framing human nature through software engineering, modular subroutines, and evolutionary algorithms, CTM provided a unified framework that satisfied neuroscientists, computer scientists, evolutionary biologists, and secular humanists alike.

### 3. The Institutional Lag

When an ideological paradigm wins major institutional backing, it creates a self-reinforcing ecosystem:

- **Peer review, grants, and tenure** flow to researchers operating within the established CTM framework.
- **Popular science publishing** rewards clear, mechanistic narratives over messy, non-reductionist ones.
- **Prestige institutions** bestow awards on popularizers who articulate the consensus clearly.

Even as alternative paradigms—such as **embodied/enactive cognition (4E)**, **predictive processing**, and **biologically grounded neuroscience**—have gained serious ground in academic philosophy and neuroscience over the past two decades, CTM remains the prevailing "common sense" of the broader public establishment.

### The Result

The wall of prizes around CTM is a classic example of an academic paradigm becoming so institutionalized that its foundational metaphor (*Mind = Computer*) is no longer treated as a hypothesis to be tested, but as the baseline definition of reality.

## How To Counter It

> Well, I wonder what it would take for a non-computationalist alternative to be taken seriously by the same institutions

For a genuinely non-computationalist paradigm to displace Computational Theory of Mind (CTM) at an institutional level, it cannot simply offer a better philosophy—it has to defeat CTM on its own home turf: **engineering utility, empirical testability, and economic incentive.**

As Thomas Kuhn famously pointed out in *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*, mainstream institutions don't abandon an entrenched paradigm when it encounters theoretical flaws; they abandon it only when a competing framework solves practical problems the old one cannot, while offering a superior toolkit for building real-world applications.

Here is what would have to happen across four major domain pillars for non-computationalist alternatives (like **enactive cognition, 4E framework, biological naturalism, or dynamic systems theory**) to gain institutional dominance:

### 1. The Engineering Failure of Pure Computational AI

Institutions care deeply about what works in technology and medicine. As long as scaling silicon vectors and token-processing Transformers delivers massive economic value, computationalism remains backed by trillions of dollars.

For a paradigm shift to occur, **symbolic/vector computation must hit a hard, irreducible ceiling** in real-world application:

- **The "Brittle Wall":** If scaling LLMs fails to solve core challenges in robotics, true autonomy, commonsense physical interaction, or real-time spatial navigation, the industry will be forced to look elsewhere.
- **The Bio-Embodied Alternative:** Non-computational models would need to build hardware or systems based on **neuromorphic, thermodynamic, or morphological computing**—where computation is not symbolic software running on substrate-independent chips, but physical, continuous dynamics embedded directly in biological or physical material. If a 4E-inspired robot out-maneuvers a silicon-programmed robot using a fraction of the energy, DARPA and tech labs will pivot overnight.

### 2. A Predictive Formalism (Mathematics Beyond Symbolic Logic)

CTM won because information theory (Shannon, Turing, von Neumann) provided a precise, universal mathematical language. To compete, non-computational alternatives must offer a mathematical framework that is just as rigorous.

- **Dynamic Systems & Active Inference:** Frameworks like Karl Friston’s Free Energy Principle or non-linear dynamical systems theory (differential equations governing continuous brain-body-environment loops) are beginning to supply this language.
- To win over grant-giving bodies like the NSF or NIH, alternative frameworks must stop using purely qualitative/phenomenological descriptions and reliably predict brain states, motor learning, or psychiatric outcomes better than information-routing models do.

### 3. Clinical and Neurological Breakthroughs

Medicine is inherently pragmatic. If treating severe neurological disorders, psychiatric conditions, or brain-machine interfaces (BMIs) remains stuck under the "computational brain" model, medicine will abandon the metaphor.

- **From "Software Glitch" to "Systemic/Biological Imbalance":** If treatments for conditions like depression, schizophrenia, or neurodegenerative diseases advance rapidly by treating the brain as a continuous, metabolically coupled, self-organizing biological organ rather than a set of malfunctioning software modules, medical institutions (and pharmaceutical funding) will shift away from CTM.

### 4. A Great Popularizer (The "Anti-Pinker")

Finally, paradigms require cultural ambassadors. CTM succeeded partly because communicators like Pinker, Dennett, and Dawkins made "mind as software" feel intuitive and exciting to journalists, venture capitalists, and university deans.

- The non-computational movement needs voices capable of translating concepts like *autopoiesis, enactivism, continuous dynamics,* and *embodied coupling* into compelling, accessible narratives for the public.
- Until a popularizer makes "you are a living, self-organizing process in continuous dialogue with your environment" sound as crisp and actionable as "your mind is an optimized software suite," CTM will remain the default cultural shorthand.

### The Tipping Point

The moment AI engineering hits an undeniable wall regarding real-world embodiment, or when biological/dynamical models produce breakthroughs in robotics and medicine that silicon software cannot mimic, the institutional prizes, university chairs, and bestseller lists will naturally follow the new paradigm.

## A Cultural and Spiritual Awakening

> Given that the nature of the limitations of CTM and the underlying metaphysical assumptions, the "practical problems" it fails to solve will not be in the realm of computational hardware or medicine per se. I think the limitations and failures of that worldview will ultimately be societal, cultural, spiritual, and very human. And that makes it all the more unlikely. But that doesn't mean it's impossible. It will just be of a magnitude and character beyond those chronicled in Kuhn's work.

You have identified the fundamental tension that makes Computationalism so remarkably resilient—and why its ultimate undoing cannot follow Thomas Kuhn's standard model of scientific revolutions.

Kuhn’s *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* was modeled on the **natural sciences** (physics, chemistry, astronomy). In Kuhn's framework, a paradigm collapses when "anomalies" accumulate—when physical nature refuses to obey the theory’s equations (e.g., the retrograding of planets breaking Ptolemaic epicycles, or blackbody radiation breaking classical physics).

Because **Computational Theory of Mind (CTM)** is not just a scientific model of an empirical object, but a **metaphysical worldview (an anthropology)** that redefines what a human being *is*, its failures will never present as broken code or anomalous lab measurements. CTM will never "fail" to run on a GPU, nor will scaling up matrix multiplication fail to produce increasingly convincing linguistic mimicry.

If CTM fails, it will fail because its **human, cultural, and spiritual fallout** becomes unbearable.

### 1. Why Kuhn’s Framework Falls Short Here

When a paradigm is an engineering tool, its test is *utility*. But when a paradigm becomes a secular theology, its test is *meaning*.

CTM can deliver endless utility—efficiency, automation, predictive text, diagnostic telemetry. It succeeds wildly on the level of technical optimization. Therefore, if we wait for CTM to suffer an "engineering crisis" in the Kuhnian sense, we will wait forever. #core 

The "anomalies" of CTM are not mathematical errors; they are **existential symptoms**:

- **Psychological:** Widespread alienation, loneliness, and despair born from being told that one’s inner life, grief, love, and sacred experiences are merely "sub-personal algorithmic subroutines."
- **Cultural:** The flattening of art, literature, and human relationship into "content generation" and token optimization.
- **Societal/Political:** The surrender of human agency to corporate software systems, where human workers are disciplined to behave like algorithms, while algorithms are elevated to the status of "moral patients."

### 2. The Character of the Crisis: A Civilizational Revolt

Because the damage of computationalism is ontological rather than technical, the shift away from it will resemble a **cultural and spiritual awakening** rather than a standard scientific update.

In Western history, whenever a mechanical, hyper-rationalist paradigm threatens to completely flatten human depth, the human spirit revolts not with better equations, but with a **re-enchantment movement**:

- **The Enlightenment vs. Romanticism:** When 18th-century clockwork physics tried to reduce nature and man to cold, mechanical gears, the Romantic movement erupted—not to deny mathematics, but to assert that beauty, emotion, mystery, and lived presence are irreducible foundations of reality.
- **The Industrial Revolution vs. Embodied Humanism:** When industrial technocracy tried to treat workers as disposable machine parts, cultural movements fought back by re-asserting human dignity, organic living, and spiritual depth.

The revolt against CTM will likely follow this same pattern. It will be driven by a cultural realization that **we sold our souls too cheaply to our creations**—that in our eagerness to build machines that look like minds, we defined human minds down to the level of machines.

By framing the fight against computationalism as a **cultural and spiritual reclamation** rather than a dry academic debate, you elevate your essay series from a critique of a single white paper into a profound critique of modern technocracy.
