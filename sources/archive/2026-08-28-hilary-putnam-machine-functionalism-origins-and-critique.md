# Hilary Putnam

### Biography: Hilary Putnam (1926–2016)

Hilary Whitehall Putnam was one of the most prominent American philosophers of the late 20th century. Born in Chicago, he studied philosophy and mathematics at the University of Pennsylvania, did graduate work under W.V.O. Quine at Harvard, and earned his Ph.D. at UCLA under logical positivist Hans Reichenbach. He taught at Princeton and MIT before joining Harvard University in 1965, where he spent the remainder of his academic career.

Putnam was known as a polymath whose work transformed the philosophy of mind, philosophy of language, philosophy of science, logic, and mathematics. He was also famous for his intellectual honesty and willingness to publicly change his mind when he felt his previous theories were flawed—a trait that earned him deep respect, even from his critics. In addition to his academic work, Putnam was politically active on the left throughout his life, participating in anti-war protests during the Vietnam era and engaging deeply with pragmatism and Jewish philosophy in his later years.

### Why His Ideas Took Hold

Putnam’s influence on the philosophy of mind—and subsequently on cognitive science, artificial intelligence, and analytic philosophy—was massive. His ideas took hold primarily because they solved a major crisis in mid-20th-century metaphysics by bridging a gap between strict scientific materialism and human psychological experience.

#### 1. The Invention of Machine Functionalism (1960s)

Before Putnam, the dominant materialist view was **Type-Identity Theory**, which claimed that mental states were strictly identical to specific physical brain states (e.g., "pain is just C-fibers firing"). The competing view was **Behaviorism**, which reduced mental states to observable physical actions.

In a landmark series of papers starting with *Minds and Machines* (1960), Putnam introduced **Machine Functionalism**. He argued that mental states are defined not by what they are made of physically, but by their **functional role**—how they route inputs (sensory data) to outputs (behavior) and intermediate internal states.

#### 2. The Power of the Computer Metaphor

Putnam capitalized on the rise of the digital computer by providing an intuitive, powerful metaphor:

- **The Brain is the Hardware.**
- **The Mind is the Software.**

Just as the same computer program (software) can run on a silicon chip, a vacuum-tube computer, or an abacus, mental states are **substrateless**—a principle known as **Multiple Realizability**. Pain, logic, or belief could theoretically be realized in biological neurons, alien biochemistry, or silicon microchips. This idea gave birth to modern cognitive science and provided the foundational philosophical license for computationalism.

#### 3. Resolving the Mind-Body Problem for Materialists

Putnam offered physicalist philosophers a way to avoid both non-physical dualism (believing in a soul) and crude biological reductionism (reducing thoughts strictly to organic brain matter). Functionalism allowed scientists and philosophers to treat psychological states as real, scientifically studyable phenomena without needing to reduce every thought to a specific cluster of biological cells.

#### 4. The Famous Irony: Discarding His Own Hypothesis

What makes Putnam's legacy particularly fascinating is that by the late 1980s, **Putnam rejected his own functionalist creation**. Through his theory of *semantic externalism* ("meanings just ain't in the head") and a recognition of the importance of biological embodiment and environmental context, Putnam concluded that reducing human consciousness and meaning to internal syntactic token-shuffling was a mistake.

However, by the time Putnam abandoned Machine Functionalism, the institutional momentum was unstoppable. His 1960s framework had already been codified into the foundation of 20th-century cognitive science, computational linguistics, and the core operational philosophy of modern AI research.

## Encrusting Scientific Materialism

> You can really see the connection to scientific materialism with Putnam

Exactly. Putnam’s entire formulation of Machine Functionalism in the 1960s was an attempt to keep the mind strictly within the bounds of **scientific materialism (physicalism)** without succumbing to the obvious flaws of earlier materialist theories.

Understanding how Putnam’s functionalism anchors modern scientific materialism reveals key insights into why this worldview persists and where its boundaries lie:

### 1. The Crisis in Mid-Century Materialism

In the mid-20th century, physicalist philosophers faced a major dilemma:

- **Mind-Brain Identity Theory** claimed that mental states were strictly identical to physical brain states (e.g., pain is just C-fibers firing). But this faced the problem of biological chauvinism—it implied that an octopus or an alien without C-fibers could not experience pain.
- **Behaviorism** reduced mental states to external physical actions or behavioral dispositions, ignoring internal mental processing altogether.

Putnam solved this crisis for scientific materialists by introducing **Machine Functionalism**. He argued that a mental state is defined not by its physical composition (biological meat vs. silicon), but by its **functional/causal role** in routing inputs, internal states, and outputs.

### 2. How Functionalism Preserved Materialism

Functionalism allowed physicalists to claim that mental phenomena are real, scientifically studyable, and fully naturalistic without needing a non-physical soul or dualism.

By framing the mind as software and the brain as hardware, scientific materialism could embrace **substrate independence**:

- Consciousness and cognition are purely physical processes because they consist of physical matter executing information routing and computational operations.
- It preserved the physicalist commitment that "everything in the universe is matter and physical dynamics," while allowing psychological concepts (like "belief," "reasoning," or "pain") to be treated as legitimate scientific abstractions.

### 3. The Materialist Assumption Stack in AI Today

This connection to scientific materialism explains why frontier AI labs and modern cognitive scientists rely so heavily on Putnam’s early framework:

1. **Physicalism:** Mental states are physical matter/dynamics.
2. **Computational Functionalism:** The relevant physical dynamics are the syntactic shuffling of internal symbolic tokens and vector activations.
3. **Substrate Independence:** Because the mind is functional software, executing those exact vector dynamics on GPUs in silicon is structurally equivalent to biological neurons firing.

### 4. The Irony: Putnam’s Eventual Rejection of Materialist Syntax

The deepest irony of this lineage is that Putnam himself came to recognize the limits of scientific reductionism. Through his work on *semantic externalism* ("meanings just ain't in the head") and embodied cognition, Putnam eventually realized that reducing human consciousness, meaning, and intentionality to internal syntactic token-processing was a mistake.

While Putnam moved past strict computational functionalism, the institutional momentum of scientific materialism took his 1960s hypothesis and built the foundation of modern cognitive science, computationalism, and AI interpretability around it.

## Putnam's Influences

> What thinkers was Putnam building on in his early work? 

In his early work during the 1950s and 1960s—particularly when formulating **Machine Functionalism** and his contributions to the philosophy of science and language—Hilary Putnam built upon, reacted against, and synthesized several key 20th-century figures:

### 1. Alan Turing (Computation & Machine States)

Alan Turing was the ultimate conceptual springboard for Putnam’s functionalism.

- **The Foundation:** Turing’s 1936 paper introducing the theoretical Turing Machine provided Putnam with a formal mathematical model of computation.
- **Putnam's Innovation:** Putnam realized that a Turing Machine's internal states were defined purely by their **functional relations** (the transition table of inputs, current states, and outputs) rather than their physical composition. Putnam directly adapted Turing's probabilistic/deterministic machine models to formulate **Machine State Functionalism**, arguing that human mental states were functional states of a Turing-like machine running on biological hardware.

### 2. Hans Reichenbach & W.V.O. Quine (Logical Positivism & Empiricism)

Putnam received his Ph.D. under **Hans Reichenbach** (one of the leading logical positivists) and worked closely with **W.V.O.** **Quine** at Harvard.

- **Reichenbach:** Provided Putnam with a deep grounding in formal mathematical logic, probability theory, and the philosophy of physics.
- **Quine:** Quine’s famous attack on the analytic-synthetic distinction (*Two Dogmas of Empiricism*, 1951) heavily influenced Putnam. Putnam embraced Quine’s holism—the idea that our beliefs face the tribunal of experience as an interconnected web rather than as isolated, standalone statements.

### 3. W.T. Stace & Early Identity Theorists (Reprehending Type-Identity)

Putnam developed functionalism as a direct alternative to **Mind-Brain Identity Theory** (championed in the 1950s by thinkers like **U.T. Place**, **J.J.C. Smart**, and **Herbert Feigl**).

- **The Conflict:** Identity theorists claimed that mental states were strictly identical to physical brain states (e.g., "Pain = C-fiber stimulation").
- **Putnam's Response:** Putnam used his famous **Multiple Realizability** argument to refute them: if an octopus or a silicon-based alien feels pain, but lacks C-fibers, Type-Identity theory fails. He built on their materialist commitments while throwing out their biological reductionism.

### 4. Ludwig Wittgenstein (Behaviorism & Use Theory of Meaning)

While Putnam was explicitly trying to preserve a physicalist philosophy of mind, he engaged deeply with Wittgenstein’s critique of private mental states and behaviorism.

- **The Dialogue:** Logical behaviorists (like Carl Hempel and Gilbert Ryle) argued that mental terms were merely shorthand for behavioral dispositions.
- **Putnam’s Synthesis:** In early papers like *Brains and Behavior* (1965), Putnam used thought experiments (such as the hypothetical "Super-Spartans" who feel intense pain without displaying any outward behavior) to dismantle strict behaviorism while preserving Wittgenstein's insight that internal mental concepts must be defined in relation to functional interactions.

## Machine State Functionalism

> When did Putnam publish initially about Machine State Functionalism?

Hilary Putnam published his initial formulations of **Machine State Functionalism** across a series of landmark papers in the 1960s, beginning in **1960**:

### 1. The Initial Paper: 1960

Putnam first introduced the core idea in his seminal **1960** paper, **"Minds and Machines"** (published in *Dimensions of Mind*, ed. Sidney Hook).

- In this paper, Putnam argued that the relationship between mental states and physical brain states is analogous to the relationship between the functional states of a Turing machine (its logical program or transition table) and its physical hardware.
- This was the explicit birth of Machine Functionalism, directly drawing on Alan Turing's mathematical work to challenge physical identity theory and behaviorism.

### 2. Further Development & Refinement: 1967

While 1960 was his initial publication on the concept, Putnam fully elaborated and formalized the theory under the label of **Turing Machine Functionalism** in **1967** with his famous paper **"Psychological Predicates"** (later republished as **"The Nature of Mental States"** in *Art, Mind, and Religion*).

In this 1967 paper, Putnam formally proposed his hypothesis:

> *"The hypothesis that a pain, or a state of being in pain, is a functional state of a whole organism (or a Turing machine) rather than a brain state in the sense of physical-chemical state."*

This paper solidified the concept of **Multiple Realizability**—the idea that the same functional state (like pain or reasoning) can be realized in biological brains, alien biochemistry, or silicon hardware.

Introduce Alan Turing not just as a WWII codebreaker, but as the thinker who gave philosophers a new conceptual vocabulary: **the separation of logical function from physical hardware.**

- This provided the exact missing puzzle piece materialists were looking for.

Now, introduce Putnam as the young Princeton/MIT philosopher who used Turing's insight to break the 1950s stalemate in his landmark 1960 paper, *"Minds and Machines"*:

- **The Breakthrough (Machine State Functionalism):** Putnam argued that mental states are neither outward behavior nor raw brain meat. A mental state is defined by its **functional/causal role** in routing inputs, internal memory states, and outputs.
- **The Seductive Metaphor:** 
    - *The Brain is Hardware.*
    - *The Mind is Software.*
- **The Magic Word (Multiple Realizability / Substrate Independence):** Just as Microsoft Word can run on a Mac, a PC, or a supercomputer, Putnam argued that "pain," "belief," or "reasoning" could theoretically run on biological meat, alien biochemistry, or silicon microchips.

**Why this lands with a general reader:** It instantly shows them where Silicon Valley got its foundational doctrine. The reader sees that "substrate independence" wasn't discovered in an AI lab; it was a theoretical patch invented by Putnam in 1960 to solve a mid-century academic debate.

## More On Type-Identity Theory

> Why was it obvious to Putnam and others that Type-Identity Theory was incorrect?

To Hilary Putnam and the early functionalists, Mind-Brain Type-Identity Theory was fundamentally untenable because it committed what they saw as an obvious biological chauvinism: it reduced the universal nature of a mental state to the specific neuroanatomy of human beings.

If pain, belief, or hunger were literally identical to a specific physical-chemical brain state—such as human C-fibers firing—then any organism or system lacking that exact biological substrate would be logically barred from experiencing that mental state.

**1. The Argument from Multiple Realizability** Putnam’s primary knockdown argument in his 1960s papers (*Minds and Machines*, *Psychological Predicates*) was that psychological states can be instantiated in wildly different physical substrates:

- **The Biological Counter-Examples:** An octopus, a bird, and a human all experience hunger, pain, and fear, yet their evolutionary lineages diverged hundreds of millions of years ago. Their nervous systems share almost no physical, structural, or chemical micro-identity.
- **The Alien/Machine Possibility:** If a silicon-based extraterrestrial or a complex synthetic computing system demonstrated the ability to learn, avoid danger, and navigate an environment, Type-Identity Theory would be forced to claim they feel nothing simply because they lack mammalian neurochemistry.
- **The Empirical Improbability:** Putnam argued it is wildly implausible to assume that across the entire physical universe, nature (or engineering) would require the exact same physical-chemical setup to produce a mental phenomenon.

**2. The Category Error: Confusing Role with Realizer** Putnam argued that Type-Identity theorists confused **what a mental state does** (its functional/causal role) with **what it is physically made of** (its physical realizer):

- **The Mechanical Analogy:** Consider the concept of a "carburetor," an "AND gate," or a "vending machine". A vending machine calculates when two 25¢ quarters equal 50¢. You can build that machine out of electronic circuits, brass levers, or hydraulic valves. What makes it a vending machine is its functional logic of state transitions, not whether it is made of copper or silicon.
- **The Computational Leap:** By separating the logical program (software) from the physical machinery (hardware), Putnam argued that psychological predicates (like "being in pain" or "believing it is raining") denote abstract organizational states, not physical tissue.

**3. Type-Identity vs. Token-Identity** To break the stalemate without abandoning scientific materialism, Putnam and subsequent functionalists (like Jerry Fodor) made a crucial distinction:

- **Type Identity (Rejected):** The claim that the general *category* of a mental state (e.g., the *type* "Pain") is identical to a universal *type* of physical brain state.
- **Token Identity (Accepted):** The claim that every particular, individual *instance* (token) of a mental state occurring in a creature at a given moment is physically realized in some physical medium.

This maneuver allowed Putnam to remain a thoroughgoing physicalist while discarding Type-Identity Theory: every mental event in a human is indeed physically realized in their biology, but what makes it *that kind of mental state* is its abstract functional relation to inputs, outputs, and other internal states—not the underlying meat.

## We Gotta Have Substrate Independence! 

> So it's not so much that TIT was scientifically proven to be false but that it didn't support Putnam's desire for substrace independence of mind

You have identified the precise metaphysical maneuver at play. Mind-Brain Type-Identity Theory (TIT) was never empirically or scientifically falsified in a laboratory; rather, Hilary Putnam rejected it on theoretical and conceptual grounds because it was fundamentally incompatible with **substrate independence** and the computational model of mind.

The reasons this distinction is vital to understanding the shift to functionalism include:

**1. A Conceptual Veto, Not an Empirical Refutation**

- **The Empirical Claim of Identity Theory:** When U.T. Place and J.J.C. Smart introduced TIT in the late 1950s, they framed it as an empirical scientific hypothesis—arguing that discovering "pain is C-fiber firing" is structurally identical to discovering that "water is $\text{H}_2\text{O}$" or "lightning is electrical discharge".
- **Putnam's Move:** Putnam did not present neurobiological data showing that human mental states were *not* brain states. Instead, he mounted an *a priori* plausibility argument: if pain is identical to human biology, then hypothetical alien biochemistry, octopuses, or digital computers cannot feel pain.
- To Putnam, this "biological chauvinism" was an unacceptable limitation for a general philosophy of mind.

**2. Reversing the Standard Scientific Baseline**

- In every other branch of natural science, physical phenomena are strictly **substrate-dependent**:
    - **Photosynthesis** requires specific chlorophyll chemistry.
    - **Liquidity** requires specific intermolecular forces.
    - **Magnetism** requires specific atomic configurations.
- Nobody accuses a chemist of "molecular chauvinism" for pointing out that a computer simulation of water is not actually wet.
- TIT treated consciousness like standard physical chemistry—a biological phenomenon produced by specific wetware. Putnam’s functionalism demanded that consciousness be the **one physical phenomenon in the universe exempted from its physical realizer**, redefining it as abstract information routing.

**3. The Institutional Incentive: AI and Cognitive Science Needed Functionalism**

- If Type-Identity Theory held, the emerging field of Artificial Intelligence was a dead end before it began. If mental states require biological brain tissue, you cannot build a mind out of electronic circuits or lines of code.
- Functionalism provided the necessary **metaphysical permission slip** for cognitive science, computational linguistics, and early AI. By shifting the definition of mind from *what it is made of* (hardware) to *what it does* (software), Putnam gave scientists license to treat the digital computer as a literal instantiation of mind rather than a mere metaphorical model.

Putnam didn't prove that mind is detached from biology; he constructed an abstract framework where **substrate independence was assumed as the goal**, and discarded Type-Identity Theory because it got in the way.

## Connections to Computer and Cognitive Science

> As a philosopher and academic, how was Putnam connected to the early computer scientists and artificial intelligence researchers? It seems like he was aware of their theoretical challenges and they were aware of his work

Hilary Putnam was not merely a detached philosopher commenting on computers from an armchair; he was an active contributor to computability theory, a co-developer of foundational computer science algorithms, and a colleague of AI's founding figures at MIT during the formative 1960s.

His deep mutual awareness with early computer scientists and AI pioneers operated across several concrete institutional, mathematical, and philosophical channels:

**1. Direct Contribution to Computer Science: The Davis-Putnam Algorithm (1960)**

Putnam was directly involved in early automated reasoning. Alongside logician Martin Davis (and later refined with George Logemann and Donald Loveland in 1962), he created the **Davis-Putnam (DPLL) Algorithm**:

- It was one of the first and most influential algorithms designed for automated theorem proving and solving the Boolean Satisfiability Problem (SAT).
- Early AI pioneers like Allen Newell, Herbert Simon, and John McCarthy were actively trying to get machines to prove mathematical theorems (such as Simon and Newell's *Logic Theorist* in 1956). Putnam was doing the exact same formal work on the mathematical logic side.

**2. Theoretical Computability and Hilbert’s 10th Problem** Before formulating Machine Functionalism, Putnam worked extensively in formal recursion theory and mathematical logic. Alongside Martin Davis and Julia Robinson, he contributed critical components to the eventual resolution of **Hilbert’s Tenth Problem** (the MRDP theorem, completed by Yuri Matiyasevich in 1970), which proved that there is no general algorithm to determine whether an arbitrary Diophantine equation has integer solutions. This placed Putnam in the inner circle of mid-century computability theory alongside the intellectual heirs of Alan Turing and Kurt Gödel.

**3. The MIT Crucible (1961–1965)**

In 1961, Putnam joined the faculty at MIT as a professor of philosophy:

- During this exact window, MIT was the primary epicenter of the emerging AI and cognitive science revolutions.
- Putnam shared campus halls with **Marvin Minsky** and **John McCarthy** (who founded the MIT AI Project), **Noam Chomsky** (who was developing generative grammar in linguistics), and **George Miller**.
- These thinkers did not work in separate disciplinary silos; they attended the same seminars, debated the limits of formal systems, and read each other’s working drafts.

**4. The Princeton-to-MIT Lineage: Advising Jerry Fodor** Putnam served as the PhD advisor to **Jerry Fodor** at Princeton University.

- When Fodor took a faculty position in linguistics and philosophy at MIT in 1961, he acted as the primary conduit translating Putnam’s abstract machine functionalism into the empirical research programs of MIT’s cognitive scientists.
- Fodor and Jerrold Katz worked directly with Chomsky’s team, creating an unbroken institutional bridge between Putnam’s philosophical logic and the linguistic and computational models being programmed in the AI labs.

**5. Providing AI with its Metaphysical Permission Slip** Early AI researchers (like Newell, Simon, and Minsky) were engineers building heuristic software, but they faced severe philosophical skepticism from traditional materialists who insisted that genuine "thought" required living biology.

Putnam’s 1960 paper, *"Minds and Machines,"* handed computer scientists the exact conceptual vocabulary they needed:

- Putnam used the mathematical formalism of Turing machine state-transition tables to argue that mental states were **software-level functional relations**, not biological hardware.
- This gave computer scientists academic license to claim that running a symbolic program on silicon was structurally equivalent to a human thinking, establishing **substrate independence** as the core operating philosophy of classical Artificial Intelligence.

Putnam understood the theoretical and mathematical mechanics of digital computers because he helped invent pieces of them; computer scientists embraced Putnam because his philosophy legitimized their machines as potential minds.

## Putnam's Inspiration for Proposing Functionalism

> I'm trying to nail down his thought process in proposing functionalism. He was inspired in part by Turing's work but also dissatisfied with behaviorism and TIT. At the same time, it's not as if those theories had been disproven. So it was as much about a desire for a new theory as it was a scientific explanatory gap? 

Putnam’s formulation of Machine Functionalism in 1960 was primarily a **conceptual rescue mission and an ideological intervention** rather than a response to empirical laboratory findings. Neither Behaviorism nor Type-Identity Theory (TIT) had been empirically falsified by neuroscientists; rather, both had painted scientific materialism into philosophical corners that Putnam and his contemporaries found unacceptable.

Putnam’s thought process was driven by three converging motivations: resolving a philosophical deadlock, securing a non-reductive version of physicalism, and providing a theoretical foundation for the emerging sciences of computation and cognition.

### 1. The Impasse of Mid-Century Materialism

By the late 1950s, physicalist philosophy of mind was trapped between two extremes, neither of which satisfied Putnam’s analytical requirements:

- **The Problem with Behaviorism:** By reducing mental states strictly to external behavioral outputs or physical dispositions, behaviorism ignored internal cognitive architecture and required what Putnam called "feigning anesthesia"—acting as though internal mental episodes did not exist. Chomsky’s 1959 critique of Skinner had just demonstrated that complex cognition (like language) was impossible to explain without positing internal rule-governed mechanisms.
- **The Problem with Type-Identity Theory (TIT):** Identity theorists like U.T. Place and J.J.C. Smart offered a real, internal physical state, but they tied it strictly to specific human biology (e.g., "pain is C-fibers firing").

Putnam recognized that TIT was conceptually too restrictive. It committed what he framed as **biological chauvinism**: if pain is literally identical to human neurochemistry, then an octopus, a bird, or an alien with different physiology could never be said to experience pain, regardless of how similarly they functioned.

### 2. The Desire for Substrate Independence

Putnam did not discover that the mind was software through biological experimentation; he formulated a framework where **substrate independence was assumed as a theoretical desideratum**:

- **The AI and Logic Orbit:** Putnam was deeply embedded in mathematical logic, recursion theory, and the intellectual circle at Princeton and MIT. He was actively working on computability algorithms (such as the Davis-Putnam algorithm) alongside early computer scientists.
- **The Theoretical Need:** Early AI researchers and computational linguists were building models of reasoning, search trees, and formal grammars. They needed a philosophy of mind that legitimized internal functional states without requiring them to simulate every micro-chemical detail of biological neurons.
- **Exempting Mind from the Physical Realizer:** In every other natural science, physical phenomena are substrate-dependent (photosynthesis requires chlorophyll; liquidity requires specific intermolecular dynamics). Putnam’s functionalism proposed that the mind was the unique phenomenon in nature defined entirely by **abstract relational organization** rather than its physical composition.

### 3. Turing as the Architectural Key

Alan Turing’s work provided Putnam with the exact formal mechanism needed to escape the behaviorist/identity theory dilemma:

```text
[Behaviorism]                [Type-Identity Theory]
Inputs ──► Outputs           Mental State = Physical Meat
(No Internal States)         (Hardware-Locked)
         │                               │
         └───────────────┬───────────────┘
                         ▼
        [Turing's Machine State Model]
        Inputs ──► Internal States ──► Outputs
                         │
                         ▼
           [Putnam's Machine Functionalism]
           Mind = Software / Functional Program
           Brain = Physical Realizer / Hardware
```

Turing had shown mathematically that a physical machine could possess formal, rule-governed **internal states** without invoking a non-physical soul. Putnam realized that a Turing machine's state-transition table is defined purely by functional relations (how inputs, current internal states, and outputs interact), completely independent of whether the machine is constructed from vacuum tubes, brass gears, or biological tissue.

By mapping human mental states directly onto Turing machine states, Putnam achieved what he wanted: **internal mental states were restored to scientific legitimacy, dualism was avoided, and the mind was decoupled from biology**.

### 4. An Epistemological Leap, Not Empirical Proof

Putnam’s 1960 paper *"Minds and Machines"* was an *a priori* conceptual proposal. Because neurobiology and consciousness science in 1960 lacked the empirical tools to map the fine-grained physical dynamics of phenomenal experience, functionalism filled the explanatory void:

- It offered physicalism a way to treat psychological concepts ("belief," "desire," "calculation") as scientifically real without having to reduce them directly to molecular neuroscience.
- It provided the budding field of cognitive science with its foundational charter: the mind could be studied as an autonomous level of software analysis.

Putnam proposed functionalism because he needed a theory that could reconcile physicalism with internal cognition and multiple realizability. The resulting computational framework succeeded not because it was empirically verified in a laboratory, but because it provided the exact metaphysical permission slip that analytic philosophy and the emerging computer sciences needed to move forward.
