# John Vervaeke's Integrated Approach to Resolving the Meaning Crisis (Gemini Deep Research)

# The Components of the Meta-Crisis

Vervaeke argues that the Meaning Crisis acts as a **"cognitive bottleneck"** that degrades our ability to solve *any* complex problem. Because we have lost the "wisdom machinery" to process reality together, we become paralyzed in the face of existential threats.

Here are the specific challenges Vervaeke (often in dialogue with thinkers like Daniel Schmachtenberger and Iain McGilchrist) identifies as being unsolvable until we address the meaning crisis:

### 1. The A.I. Alignment Problem (The "Wisdom Gap")

This is likely most relevant to your work as an AI philosopher. Vervaeke argues that we are rapidly increasing our technological **power** (our ability to manipulate the world) while our **wisdom** (our ability to realize what is valuable and good) is crashing.

- **The Trap:** We are building A.I. systems based on "propositional processing" (logic, computation, efficiency) without "relevance realization" (the embodied sense of what actually matters).
- **The Meaning Crisis connection:** If we don't know what it means to be human—if we cannot define "the good" beyond mere utility or GDP—we cannot program "the good" into our machines. We risk creating a super-intelligence that optimizes for the wrong variables because we ourselves are confused about what is meaningful.

### 2. The Ecological Crisis (Loss of "Participatory Knowing")

Vervaeke argues you cannot solve climate change simply with better solar panels; you need a fundamental shift in worldview.

- **The Trap:** We view the world through a "utilitarian" lens—nature is just dead matter to be exploited for resources. This is what he calls the "having mode" (from Erich Fromm) rather than the "being mode."
- **The Meaning Crisis connection:** We have lost the sense of the "Sacred." For Vervaeke, "sacred" doesn't necessarily mean supernatural; it means viewing reality as having intrinsic value that demands our respect.1 Because we view the earth as an object rather than a system we participate in, we are psychologically incapable of making the sacrifices necessary to save it.

### 3. Political Polarization (The Death of "Dialogos")

Democracy relies on the assumption that conflicting viewpoints can come together to find a higher truth.

- **The Trap:** Current discourse has devolved into "adversarial processing." We view the other side not as a partner in truth-seeking, but as an enemy to be destroyed.
- **The Meaning Crisis connection:** We have lost the art of **Dialogos**—the practice of thinking *together* to reach a collective insight that neither side had alone. Without this, politics becomes pure power struggle (Will to Power) rather than a collective search for wisdom, making coordination on complex issues impossible.

### 4. The Epistemic Crisis ("Post-Truth" and Bullshit)

We are drowning in information but starving for wisdom.2

- **The Trap:** The internet is an "adversarial information environment." Algorithms amplify what is *salient* (what grabs attention/outrage), not what is *true*.3
- **The Meaning Crisis connection:** Vervaeke distinguishes between **Truth** (connection to reality) and **Bullshit** (speech that is unconcerned with truth, only influence). Because we are disconnected from a shared "Nomological Order" (a shared map of reality), we have no immunity to bullshit. We retreat into tribal bubbles where we can't even agree on basic facts, making collective problem-solving impossible.

### 5. The Mental Health & Addiction Crisis

This is the internal cost that drains the energy needed to solve external problems.

- **The Trap:** Modern capitalism exploits our dopamine systems (parasitic processing). We medicate our sense of meaninglessness with consumption—social media, porn, junk food, and endless entertainment.
- **The Meaning Crisis connection:** When people feel their lives are meaningless (the "Zombie" state), they are easily hijacked by super-stimuli. A population that is addicted, depressed, and anxious does not have the cognitive surplus or emotional resilience to tackle climate change or AI safety.

### Summary

Vervaeke’s ultimate argument is that **you cannot solve these problems at the level of symptoms.**

- If you fix the climate but keep the "exploitation mindset," we will just destroy the planet in a different way later.
- If you fix the economy but keep the "addiction mindset," we will just consume ourselves to death.

He believes we need a **"Transformation of Consciousness"**—a shift from the "Having Mode" to the "Being Mode"—to act as the foundation for solving these material threats.

# Vervaeke's Meaning Crisis Video Series Summarized

**Awakening from the Meaning Crisis** is a 50-episode video lecture series by **John Vervaeke**, a Professor of Psychology and Cognitive Science at the University of Toronto. #sensemaking #meaning 

The series is an interdisciplinary tour-de-force that weaves together history, philosophy, cognitive science, and religion to explain why modern society is suffering from a pervasive sense of meaninglessness—and how we might scientifically "reverse engineer" a solution.

You can watch the full series here: **[Awakening from the Meaning Crisis Playlist](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLND1JCRq8Vuh3f0P5qjrSdb5eC1ZfZwWJ)**

### **Core Thesis: What is the "Meaning Crisis"?**

Vervaeke argues that we are in the midst of a mental health and cultural crisis characterized by rising rates of suicide, anxiety, depression, and a general sense of "drift."

- **The Problem:** We have lost the "machinery" or "cultural grammar" that previous civilizations used to connect themselves to reality. Historically, religion and mythology provided a **Narrative Order** (a story of the cosmos), a **Nomological Order** (clear rules/reality), and a **Normative Order** (a way to "level up" or become a better person).
- **The Symptom:** Modern life lacks this integration. Vervaeke often uses the **Zombie** as the myth of our time: a creature that is wandering, decaying, homeless, consuming "brains" (meaning-making organs), and unable to speak or connect communally.
- **The Trap:** In the absence of true meaning, we fall prey to **"Bullshit"** (a technical term he uses for speech that is unconcerned with truth, only salience) and **addiction** (e.g., video games, which are popular because they artificially provide the clear rules and progress markers real life lacks).

### **Structure of the Series**

The series is roughly divided into two halves:

Part 1: The Historical Genealogy (Episodes 1–25)

Vervaeke traces the evolution of human cognition to show how we got here. Key stops include:

- **The Upper Paleolithic Transition:** How early humans developed "psychotechnologies" (like shamanism and ritual) to alter their consciousness and solve problems.
- **The Axial Age:** The period (c. 800–200 BCE) where figures like Socrates, Buddha, and Confucius created the "two-worlds" mythology—the idea that the everyday world is illusory and we must train our minds to access a "realer" world of truth and wisdom.
- **The Collapse:** How the Scientific Revolution and the Protestant Reformation slowly dismantled this "two-worlds" view, trapping us in a cold, physical universe without a "wisdom tradition" to guide our inner lives.

Part 2: The Cognitive Science Solution (Episodes 26–50)

Vervaeke pivots to modern cognitive science to rebuild a worldview compatible with science.

- **Relevance Realization:** His central scientific concept. Intelligence isn't just "knowing facts"; it is the ability to realize what is *relevant* in a near-infinite world. Meaning is the feeling of being "fitted" to your environment (Agent-Arena relationship).
- **4E Cognition:** The idea that mind is not just in the brain, but is **E**mbodied, **E**mbedded, **E**nacted, and **E**xtended.
- **Ecology of Practices:** We cannot just "think" our way out of the crisis. We need a combination of practices (e.g., meditation, contemplation, martial arts, dialogue) to retrain our relevance realization and cultivate **Wisdom**.
- **Religion of No Religion:** The goal is to create a secular path to "enlightenment"—awakening to reality without necessarily adopting ancient dogmas.

### I. Introduction: Understanding the Meaning Crisis Through John Vervaeke's Lens

John Vervaeke, a professor whose work spans psychology, cognitive science, and Buddhist studies, has extensively explored what he terms the "meaning crisis." His foundational YouTube series, "Awakening from the Meaning Crisis," serves as a primary resource for understanding his perspective, delving into the loss of a sense of deep connection with oneself, with others, and with the world at large, as well as a decline in spiritual vitality 1. This concept of lost spiritual vitality suggests that the crisis extends beyond mere psychological distress, touching upon a fundamental disruption in how individuals relate to their existence and their sense of purpose within it. The interconnectedness of this disconnection across various levels of human experience highlights the systemic nature of the meaning crisis, indicating that it is not solely an individual issue but one with significant social and environmental dimensions. #meaning #crisis 

Vervaeke defines this pervasive modern-day feeling of despair, depression, and meaninglessness as being intrinsically linked to the disruption of a culture's fundamental "mythos," or worldview, that shapes how individuals relate to the world and find meaning 3. He argues that the particular mythos under pressure in our current meaning crisis is the "two-worlds mythos" inherited from the Axial Revolution. This historical perspective, tracing the roots of the crisis back to a period of significant philosophical and religious transformation, suggests that the contemporary experience of meaninglessness is not an entirely novel phenomenon but rather the result of long-term historical and cultural shifts. The association with despair and depression underscores the tangible psychological impact of this crisis, further emphasizing the urgency of finding effective ways to address it.

In clarifying the nature of "meaning" within this context, Vervaeke emphasizes "meaning in life," which he starkly defines as the sense that one's life is worth continuing despite its inherent frailties, failures, and frustrations 4. This definition underscores the profound role of meaning in sustaining human motivation and resilience. This sense of meaning in life involves feeling connected to the reality of one's own being, the reality of other people, and the reality of the world. This suggests that the meaning crisis involves a breakdown in these fundamental connections, pointing towards potential solutions that would focus on their strengthening.

The meaning crisis is also characterized as a widespread epidemic of meaninglessness, an existential crisis occurring at the societal level, marked by feelings of disconnection, nihilism, and a lack of purpose in the face of prevailing materialism, consumerism, and the erosion of traditional religious and philosophical frameworks 5. This broadens the scope of the issue, positioning it as a significant cultural and historical problem with far-reaching consequences. The linking of the meaning crisis to these societal trends identifies potential contributing factors in modern life, implying that solutions might necessitate a re-evaluation of these aspects. Furthermore, Vervaeke posits that this meaning crisis is deeply intertwined with other significant societal issues, such as the mental health crisis, environmental degradation, and political instability, suggesting a "metacrisis" where these problems mutually reinforce each other. This interdependence highlights that addressing the meaning crisis is not only important in its own right but also crucial for tackling these other major global challenges. The historical perspective is further reinforced by the observation that the meaning crisis is linked to the "great dis-embedding" that followed the scientific revolution and the Enlightenment, a period marked by a growing sense of separation from nature and a corresponding rise in nihilistic thought 7. This concept of "great dis-embedding" serves as a powerful metaphor for the alienation and disconnection at the core of the meaning crisis, suggesting a need to find ways to re-establish a meaningful relationship with the natural world and our broader environment.

### II. The Foundational Role of Relevance Realization in Vervaeke's Solution

At the heart of John Vervaeke's proposed solution to the meaning crisis lies the concept of **relevance realization**. He argues that this is a core function of both intelligence and consciousness, enabling individuals to effectively filter out irrelevant information and focus their cognitive resources on what truly matters 8. This process is described as a fundamental connectedness between individuals and the world, which is essential for agency and carries profound spiritual significance. This suggests that relevance realization is not merely a cognitive mechanism but also an existential one, and that cultivating this ability can lead to a deeper sense of connection with reality. The attribution of spiritual significance or even sacredness to relevance realization indicates a value that transcends mere information processing, connecting this cognitive function to the fundamental human need for meaning and purpose.

Vervaeke explicitly states that relevance realization (RR) serves as the cognitive machinery that allows us to determine what is relevant in any given situation, and it forms the very foundation of meaning-making itself 3. It is through this process that we connect with the world around us, co-construct our relationship with it, and ultimately make sense of our environment. The assertion that RR is the foundation of meaning-making is a key element of Vervaeke's proposed solution, suggesting that actively enhancing our relevance realization abilities is crucial for the process of finding meaning in life.

Multiple sources delve into the mechanisms and profound importance of relevance realization. It is highlighted as being critical for effective problem-solving and for navigating the overwhelming "combinatorial explosion" of information that we constantly face 9. The concept of combinatorial explosion effectively explains why relevance realization is absolutely necessary for intelligent behavior in a world brimming with complexity. Without this capacity to filter and prioritize information, individuals would be utterly overwhelmed by the sheer volume of possibilities and stimuli. Furthermore, Vervaeke grounds relevance realization in the concept of autopoiesis, which refers to the self-organization of living beings 12. This grounding suggests that the fundamental ability to find meaning is intrinsically linked to being a living, self-sustaining organism, providing a biological basis for the importance of meaning-making in human existence. It is also emphasized that relevance realization is not a passive process of detection but rather a dynamic and self-organizing one that involves caring, commitment, and a sense of deep connectedness 11. This highlights the affective and relational dimensions of meaning, indicating that meaning is not simply a cognitive construct but also involves our emotions and our relationships with the world and others.

Vervaeke argues that the meaning crisis in part stems from a confusion between merely "having" propositional knowledge, which consists of facts and beliefs, and truly "being" in an optimal relationship with ourselves, with other people, and with the wider world 3. He posits that modern ideologies and belief systems often attempt to generate meaning through this propositional knowledge, but they ultimately fail because meaning operates at a much deeper level, a level that is fundamentally facilitated by relevance realization. This distinction between "having" and "being" provides a valuable framework for understanding the inherent limitations of purely intellectual or belief-based approaches to finding meaning. True meaning, according to Vervaeke, involves a more profound and experiential engagement with the world than simply accumulating facts or adhering to a set of beliefs. He further explains that relevance realization is intricately linked to different kinds of knowing that extend beyond propositional knowledge. These include procedural knowing, which is knowing how to do something; perspectival knowing, which is knowing how to perceive the world from different viewpoints; and participatory knowing, which involves being in a state of flow and having an optimal sense of fitted-ness with one's environment 3. These forms of knowing are crucial as they contribute to a sense of deep connection and overall coherence, which are essential for experiencing life as meaningful. Therefore, Vervaeke suggests that the meaning crisis can be effectively addressed by consciously understanding and actively cultivating our inherent relevance realization abilities. By focusing on practices that enhance our capacity to connect and cohere with reality in a skillful and participatory manner, we can shift away from the limitations of the "having" mode of knowledge and move towards the richer and more meaningful "being" mode, thereby alleviating the pervasive sense of meaninglessness that characterizes the crisis.

### III. Wisdom and Virtue as Cornerstones of a Meaningful Life

John Vervaeke places significant emphasis on the importance of wisdom and virtue as essential components for navigating the inherent complexities of life and ultimately overcoming the pervasive meaning crisis. He suggests that wisdom is fundamentally needed to discern what truly matters in life and to understand how to align oneself with those priorities, establishing a direct and crucial link between the cultivation of wisdom and the resolution of the meaning crisis 4. This idea of "fitting ourselves" to what is truly important implies an active and engaged role in the creation of meaning in one's life. It is not simply about passively receiving meaning from external sources but rather about actively shaping our relationship with the world and our place within it.

Multiple sources within Vervaeke's work discuss wisdom as a form of cognitive improvement that fundamentally enhances the ability to live a good life. This improvement involves key aspects such as insight, the capacity for self-transformation, and the crucial ability to effectively deal with relevance in various situations 18. Furthermore, virtue is closely linked to wisdom in Vervaeke's framework, with the argument being made that wisdom is a necessary prerequisite for being truly virtuous. The definition of wisdom as a "cognitive improvement that affords the living of a good life" directly connects it to overall well-being and human flourishing. This suggests that the pursuit of wisdom is not merely an intellectual exercise but has tangible and practical benefits for how we experience and live our lives. Vervaeke also highlights the distinction between practical wisdom, or phronesis, and the awareness of underlying principles, or sophia, emphasizing that both theoretical understanding and the practical ability to apply that understanding are crucial for living a meaningful life 20. Knowing what is right or good is not sufficient; individuals also need the practical wisdom to act rightly and effectively in specific and often complex situations. The assertion that wisdom is the higher-order ability that deals with relevance further reinforces the central role of relevance realization within Vervaeke's overall framework. Wisdom, in this context, can be understood as the skillful and nuanced application of relevance realization in navigating the complexities of life.

Vervaeke also points out that virtues are not simply independent traits but are interconnected and can sometimes even come into conflict with one another 20. This recognition that virtues are not always mutually reinforcing highlights the complexity inherent in ethical decision-making and underscores the necessity of wisdom to effectively navigate these complexities. Simply attempting to maximize each virtue in isolation can lead to undesirable outcomes; wisdom is required to find balance and to apply virtues in a context-sensitive manner. Furthermore, Vervaeke suggests that the cultivation of virtue involves a process of self-correction and developing the ability to see through illusions into the underlying reality of situations 8. This process of seeing through illusion is what ultimately opens up an appreciation for the inherent sacredness of reality. This connection between virtue and a deeper, more accurate perception of reality aligns with the idea of wisdom as involving insight and a profound understanding of the world. Therefore, actively cultivating virtue can lead to a more meaningful and truthful experience of reality.

### IV. Mindfulness: A Key Practice for Enhancing Meaning and Insight

Mindfulness plays a significant role in John Vervaeke's framework for addressing the meaning crisis, primarily due to its capacity to foster cognitive flexibility, enhance insight, and cultivate a deeper connection with reality. The resurgence of interest in mindfulness practices is itself noted as a positive symptom of the meaning crisis, with individuals turning to these ancient techniques as a means of enhancing meaning in their lives and connecting with a more profound sense of reality 2. This widespread adoption suggests a broad recognition of mindfulness's potential to alleviate feelings of meaninglessness and disconnection that characterize the crisis.

Vervaeke presents mindfulness as a specific cognitive style that strategically foregrounds System 1 thinking, which is characterized by its intuitive and rapid nature, while simultaneously backgrounding System 2 thinking, which is more analytical and slow 21. This particular configuration of cognitive processing is believed to facilitate insight and promote cognitive flexibility. Importantly, mindfulness is not seen as operating in isolation but rather in an opponent yet coordinated relationship with active open-mindedness. This suggests that a balanced approach, incorporating both intuitive and analytical modes of thought, is necessary for navigating the complexities of life effectively. The explanation of mindfulness in terms of System 1 and System 2 processing provides a scientific basis for understanding its effectiveness in promoting insight. By intentionally shifting the balance between these two modes of thinking, mindfulness practices can assist individuals in overcoming ingrained cognitive biases and perceiving situations from novel and more insightful perspectives.

Furthermore, Vervaeke connects mindfulness directly to the core concepts of his framework, suggesting that it is a practice that enables individuals to ground themselves more firmly in reality and to discover relevance by actively deepening their relationship with the world around them 8. This idea that mindfulness can lead to a more profound connection with the world suggests its potential to counteract the pervasive feeling of disconnection that lies at the heart of the meaning crisis. By cultivating a more focused and non-judgmental awareness of their present experience, individuals can become more attuned to the subtle yet meaningful aspects of their lives and their environment.

### V. Reconnecting with Meaning, Purpose, and Belonging in a Disenchanted World

John Vervaeke emphasizes the fundamental human needs for meaning, purpose, and belonging, and his analysis of the meaning crisis highlights how these essential aspects of human experience have been significantly impacted in the modern world. He defines meaning in life as a sense that one's existence makes coherent sense and that individuals feel genuinely connected to the reality of their own being, as well as to others and the world at large 4. This underscores the importance of both narrative coherence and a sense of deep connection in experiencing life as meaningful. The emphasis on "making sense" suggests a fundamental human need for narrative and understanding in the construction of meaning within their lives. Individuals need to be able to integrate their diverse experiences into a cohesive and understandable story of who they are and their place in the world.

Vervaeke offers a nuanced understanding of purpose, describing it less as a fixed, ultimate goal and more as a guiding orientation or a "north star" that provides direction and organization to one's life narrative 11. This framing of purpose as an ongoing orientation, rather than a static endpoint, offers a more adaptable and sustainable approach to finding meaning, allowing for continued direction and significance even as specific goals are achieved or inevitably change. Beyond purpose, Vervaeke identifies other crucial dimensions of meaning, including significance, which refers to a sense of depth and realness in one's life, and mattering, which is the feeling of being connected to something larger than oneself and having a real impact on it. This concept of "mattering" highlights the fundamental human need to feel that their existence has a tangible effect and value beyond their own individual lives, suggesting the importance of contributing to something greater. Belonging is described by Vervaeke as a dynamic process of co-shaping between an organism and its environment 11. He argues that this sense of belonging has been significantly disrupted in modern culture, leading to a feeling of "cultural domicide," where individuals may have physical shelter but lack a genuine sense of feeling at home in their cultural surroundings. This concept powerfully captures the feeling of alienation and disconnection that can be a significant component of the meaning crisis, suggesting a critical need to rebuild a sense of belonging and connection to our cultural environment.

Vervaeke further links meaning in life and belonging to the concept of "religio," which he uses to signify a deep and fundamental sense of connectedness characterized by both care and commitment 8. His use of "religio" in a non-traditional sense emphasizes the basic human need for these qualities of connection and commitment, regardless of specific religious affiliations. This implies that fostering these attributes within our individual lives and our broader communities can be a powerful way to address the underlying issues of the meaning crisis. Finally, Vervaeke highlights the crucial role of community and meaningful interpersonal relationships in combating the meaning crisis and fostering a life that is richer and more purposeful 27. This underscores the vital social dimension of meaning and the fundamental importance of human connection for overall well-being. The emphasis on the quality of these connections, rather than merely the quantity, suggests that deep and authentic relationships are essential, as superficial connections may not be sufficient to address the profound sense of loneliness and disconnection associated with the meaning crisis.

### VI. An Interdisciplinary Approach to Meaning: The Synthesis of Cognitive Science, Philosophy, and Spirituality

John Vervaeke's approach to understanding and addressing the meaning crisis is notable for its deliberate integration of insights from a diverse range of disciplines, including cognitive science, philosophy, and spirituality 4. This interdisciplinary perspective allows for a more comprehensive and nuanced analysis of this complex human experience. The deliberate bridging of cognitive science with spirituality suggests an attempt to reconcile scientific understanding with the fundamental human need for meaning and transcendence, potentially offering a more holistic solution than approaches confined to a single field of study.

Vervaeke's work explicitly interweaves scientific findings, spiritual traditions, and philosophical inquiries to explore the historical origins and contemporary manifestations of the meaning crisis 7. By drawing upon these multiple perspectives, he aims to construct a richer and more comprehensive understanding of the multifaceted phenomenon. This allows for an explanation that considers biological, psychological, cultural, and existential factors that contribute to the crisis. Vervaeke's own academic interests, which include wisdom, mindfulness, meditation, relevance realization, general intelligence, and rationality, reflect this blend of cognitive science with aspects of Buddhist psychology and philosophy 8. The inclusion of Buddhist psychology indicates an engagement with ancient wisdom traditions, suggesting a belief that valuable insights into human flourishing and the alleviation of suffering can be found beyond the confines of modern Western thought.

In proposing a solution, Vervaeke advocates for re-legitimizing projects that actively enhance crucial human capacities such as insight, wisdom, self-transcendence, and the experience of meaning itself, all within a scientifically informed framework 3. He further suggests the need to re-engineer enlightenment, not as a singular mystical experience, but as a practical and accessible ecology of practices. This implies a deliberate and systematic approach to cultivating the cognitive, emotional, and relational skills necessary to overcome the meaning crisis. The concept of an "ecology of practices" suggests that meaning and well-being are not simply matters of chance but can be actively cultivated through a network of interconnected and mutually reinforcing practices. Vervaeke also proposes the development of a "Religio" that is distinct from traditional religious institutions. This "Religio" would prioritize different forms of knowing beyond purely propositional knowledge and would reinstate the importance of "being" as a fundamental aspect of human experience, all within a secular and scientifically grounded understanding. This concept acknowledges the challenges of finding shared meaning and purpose in an increasingly secular age and proposes a potential way forward by focusing on fundamental human needs for connection, coherence, and transcendence in a way that is compatible with a modern worldview.

### VII. Practical Pathways to Meaning: Recommended Steps and Practices

John Vervaeke offers several concrete and practical steps that individuals can incorporate into their lives to actively address the meaning crisis. These recommendations are designed to cultivate the capacities and connections that foster a greater sense of meaning and purpose. He suggests engaging in "cognitive exercises," drawing an analogy to mental gym sessions, which include practices like contemplation and meditation 27. Contemplation, in this context, involves actively and deeply pondering specific thoughts, ideas, or the larger questions of life. Recommended approaches include choosing a specific focus, finding a quiet environment conducive to reflection, considering the use of journaling to explore thoughts, and allowing the mind to wander freely within the chosen focus. Meditation, on the other hand, focuses on the practice of quieting the mind and seeking a state of deep inner peace, often through techniques such as focusing on the breath or the repetition of a mantra. Practical tips for meditation include maintaining focus on the breath, practicing patience with a wandering mind, and aiming for short, consistent daily sessions to build the practice. The analogy of "mental gym sessions" emphasizes the need for regular and dedicated practice in these cognitive exercises, suggesting that just like physical fitness, cultivating mental and existential well-being requires ongoing effort and engagement.

Vervaeke also highlights the importance of cultivating the state of "flow," which is characterized by a deep immersion in an activity where one's skills are optimally matched to the challenges at hand, leading to a sense of losing track of time and a profound sense of engagement 27. To achieve flow, he suggests intentionally challenging oneself, eliminating potential distractions, fully engaging in the chosen activity, and practicing patience as one develops the necessary skills. Furthermore, Vervaeke stresses the crucial role of fostering community by actively engaging in meaningful interactions and nurturing deep interpersonal relationships 27. Practical steps for building community include being intentional about seeking out connections with others, being open and vulnerable in interactions, practicing active listening when others are speaking, actively contributing to the well-being of the community, and consciously nurturing existing relationships. The emphasis on "deepening" relationships suggests that the quality and authenticity of these connections are paramount. Meaningful relationships require vulnerability, trust, and genuine engagement, and they can provide a powerful antidote to the loneliness and disconnection often associated with the meaning crisis. Vervaeke also suggests that deepening our relationship with the world and with others is a fundamental way to discover relevance and cultivate a sense of inner peace 8. This reinforces the importance of connection and active engagement with our environment and our social circles in the pursuit of meaning. Ultimately, Vervaeke emphasizes the need for an "ecology of practices" to effectively address the multifaceted challenges of the meaning crisis, providing examples such as mindfulness meditation for addressing modal confusion and community building for alleviating feelings of alienation 3. This concept of an interconnected network of practices suggests that a holistic and integrated approach, where different practices support and reinforce one another, is likely to be more effective than relying on a single technique in isolation.

### VIII. Critical Perspectives and Alternative Interpretations of Vervaeke's Ideas

While John Vervaeke's work on the meaning crisis has garnered significant attention and praise, it has also faced certain criticisms and alternative interpretations. Some commentators suggest that his core theory of Relevance Realization might not fully address fundamental questions of value, potentially focusing more on the process of identifying relevance than on the inherent worth or significance of what is deemed relevant 19. This raises important philosophical questions about whether the ability to discern relevance is sufficient to ground a robust sense of meaning, or if there are additional dimensions of value that need to be considered. Others have noted that some of Vervaeke's ideas can be intellectually demanding and complex, potentially making them less accessible to a broader audience 19. This concern about accessibility suggests that while his insights may be profound, their practical application might be limited if they are not easily understood or translatable into everyday practices for individuals seeking to address their own feelings of meaninglessness.

One critique mentioned that Vervaeke's extensive YouTube series, while initially engaging, appeared to lose momentum around the twentieth episode 19. This subjective observation could point to the challenges of sustaining audience engagement over a long and complex exploration of a multifaceted topic. Additionally, concerns have been raised regarding Vervaeke's engagement with certain individuals who have faced public criticism and have arguably lost credibility in their respective fields 19. This is a social and ethical consideration that some viewers might find problematic, potentially impacting the perceived authority or trustworthiness of Vervaeke's overall work for those who hold these concerns. Furthermore, one commenter specifically questioned whether relevance realization might inadvertently replace deeper questions of value with a focus on something more basic or universally applicable 19. This particular criticism suggests a potential for reductionism in solely emphasizing relevance realization as the primary basis for meaning, prompting a consideration of whether there are aspects of meaning that extend beyond the mere identification of what is relevant in a given context.

### IX. Conclusion: Towards an Ecology of Practices for Awakening from the Meaning Crisis

John Vervaeke's work offers a comprehensive and insightful framework for understanding and addressing the pervasive meaning crisis in contemporary society. He defines this crisis as a profound loss of spiritual vitality and a sense of disconnection from oneself, others, and the world, rooted in historical and cultural shifts, particularly the disruption of traditional worldviews. Central to Vervaeke's proposed solution is the concept of relevance realization, which he posits as the fundamental cognitive process underlying meaning-making. By effectively filtering information and focusing on what truly matters, individuals can begin to re-establish a sense of connection and coherence in their lives.

The cultivation of wisdom and virtue, facilitated by practices like mindfulness, are presented as crucial for navigating the complexities of existence and fostering a meaningful life. Wisdom enables individuals to discern what is truly important and to act accordingly, while virtue provides a moral compass guiding their interactions with the world. Mindfulness, by enhancing cognitive flexibility and promoting insight, allows for a deeper engagement with reality and a greater appreciation for the present moment. Vervaeke emphasizes the fundamental human needs for meaning, purpose, and belonging, highlighting how the meaning crisis has eroded these essential aspects of our lives. He advocates for reconnecting with these needs through intentional practices and the cultivation of strong community bonds.

A key strength of Vervaeke's approach lies in its interdisciplinary nature, drawing upon cognitive science, philosophy, and spirituality to provide a holistic understanding of the meaning crisis and its potential resolutions. He recommends practical steps such as contemplation, meditation, cultivating flow, and building community as actionable pathways for individuals seeking to enhance meaning in their own lives. While Vervaeke's work has been subject to some criticisms regarding the centrality of value in his framework, the accessibility of his ideas, and his engagement with certain individuals, his integrated approach offers a valuable perspective on a significant contemporary challenge. Ultimately, Vervaeke's emphasis on the development of an "ecology of practices" holds significant potential for fostering individual and societal well-being in the face of the meaning crisis, suggesting that a multifaceted and sustained effort is required to awaken from this pervasive sense of meaninglessness.
