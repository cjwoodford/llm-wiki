# Metamodernism

# Metamodernism: The Conceptual Architecture of Oscillation in Post-Postmodern Culture

## I. Introduction: The Necessity of a New Cultural Logic

### 1.1. Contextualizing the 'End' of Postmodernity

The theoretical architecture of Metamodernism emerges directly from the perceived intellectual and cultural exhaustion of the Postmodern condition. Spanning roughly from 1950 to 2000, Postmodernism was characterized by an aggressive skepticism toward all forms of universal truth, grand narratives, and utopian vision.1 Its aesthetic strategies relied heavily on irony, detachment, and critical deconstruction, often leading to a state described by Fredric Jameson as "depthlessness"—a collective inability to experience the objective reality beyond a superficial level.3 This pervasive attitude of cynicism and apathy ultimately proved limiting, creating cultural inertia.4

By the turn of the millennium, a consensus emerged among critics and theorists that the postmodern era had concluded. As noted by Linda Hutcheon, the condition was abandoned, prompting her definitive observation: "Let's just say it: it's over".6 However, agreement on the demise of Postmodernism was not matched by clarity on its replacement, leading to a period of theoretical ambiguity.6 #metamodernism #research/deep 

### 1.2. Response to Systemic Crises

The necessity for a new cultural logic was catalyzed by major global crises in the early 21st century. Events such as the 2008 financial crisis, mounting political instability, pervasive gender inequality, climate change, and the disruptive digital revolution demanded a response more proactive than cynical deconstruction.7 While Postmodernism offered powerful critical analytic strategies, particularly in identifying systemic faults, these methods "fall short in facilitating desired resolutions".7 Postmodernism demonstrated what not to believe, but offered no mechanism for collective belief or forward action.

Consequently, the cultural imperative shifted toward finding a framework that could accommodate the need for renewed agency and purpose without falling back into the pitfalls of ideological naïveté associated with Modernism. This intellectual predicament—how to sincerely engage with complex problems while retaining critical self-awareness—demanded a fundamental sociological adjustment. This adjustment suggests that the paradigm shift is not a superficial change in style but a fundamental cultural evolution designed to restore the capacity for productive action in the face of existential threats.

### 1.3. Defining Metamodernism

Metamodernism is the term used to define the emerging cultural discourse and paradigm that followed Postmodernism, specifically describing the dominant "structure of feeling" of the early 21st century.6 It is characterized by integrating and responding to both Modernism and Postmodernism, reflecting an oscillation between seemingly opposed concepts: modern idealism and postmodern skepticism, or modern sincerity and postmodern irony.7

This structure of feeling manifests as a "pragmatic idealism" or an "informed naivety".5 Proponents suggest that the metamodern sensibility allows for the pursuit of utopian action (a Modernist impulse) while simultaneously employing critical self-awareness (a Postmodern guardrail) to prevent the degeneration into ideological blindness.4 Metamodernism thus operates as a mechanism of survival, articulating a way to believe, commit, and build, even when knowing that all belief, commitment, and construction are inherently fragile.

## II. Genealogy and Foundational Texts

### 2.1. The Precursors and Early Usage of the Term

Although Metamodernism gained academic prominence in the 2010s, the term "metamodern" had earlier, less-related uses. It first appeared as early as 1975 when Mas'ud Zavarzadeh employed it to describe emerging American literature from the mid-1950s.7 Later, in 1999, Moyo Okediji used the term in the context of contemporary African-American art, framing it as an "extension of and challenge to modernism and postmodernism".7

However, the key proponents of the contemporary discourse, Timotheus Vermeulen and Robin van den Akker (V&vdA), stressed a crucial disjunction from these historical uses. They explicitly noted that their conception of Metamodernism is neither derived from nor aligned with these prior practices.9 They particularly sought to distinguish their work from earlier notions that intended a "programme" or read literature "normatively, as a prescription for another modernism preferable to the postmodern outlook".9 This careful distinction underscores the founders' initial intent to offer a descriptive framework rather than a normative mandate.

### 2.2. The Seminal Intervention: Notes on Metamodernism

The subject garnered broader academic attention following the publication of Timotheus Vermeulen and Robin van den Akker’s 2010 essay, Notes on Metamodernism, in the Journal of Aesthetics & Culture.6 This text established the foundation for the field, arguing that a new discourse was observable across contemporary architecture, art, and film.6

V&vdA contended that this new discourse could no longer be explained exclusively through postmodern terms but required a new label—Metamodernism—to capture its essence: an oscillation between a modern enthusiasm and a postmodern irony.6 Crucially, the authors positioned their term as a tool for periodization, seeking to articulate the developments in aesthetics, culture, and politics that transcended the capacity of the postmodern lens.9 Their initial aim was descriptive, aiming to articulate the current "structure of feeling" and think the present historically, rather than prescribing how art or society should function.9

The early scholarship established a foundation by observing the growing blend of irony and sincerity, often referred to as post-irony, in society.7 This analysis of the double-bind—the simultaneous acceptance of both modern and postmodern strategies—forms the bedrock of the movement.

### 2.3. Delimiting the Post-Postmodern Field

Metamodernism is but one of several theoretical frameworks proposed to describe the post-postmodern cultural state, reflecting the widespread intellectual recognition that the previous era had ended.8 However, Metamodernism has emerged as the most adopted term due to its structural clarity concerning affective negotiation.

One key competitor is Altermodernism, proposed by curator Nicolas Bourriaud.8 Bourriaud defined Altermodernism primarily as a "synthesis between modernism and post-colonialism".6 This synthesis is expressed through concepts like "heterochronicity," "archipelagraphy," nomadism, and a "globalized perspective".6 V&vdA, while acknowledging the evocativeness of Bourriaud’s concept, found its precise meaning "slippery and evasive" and its argumentation "vague".6 The Metamodern framework argues that the affective oscillation it describes is a more fundamental structural shift than the nomadic or globalized aesthetics highlighted by Altermodernism.

Another framework is Hypermodernism or Digimodernism, associated with Alan Kirby.9 This approach focuses heavily on technological change, emphasizing the role of digital media and participatory culture in shaping contemporary aesthetics.8 While Metamodernism recognizes the internet as a "game changer" 8, it maintains that the core shift lies in the affective relationship between sincerity and irony, defining the new "structure of feeling," whereas changes in technological consumption habits (Kirby’s focus) are secondary effects.

A key tension marks the theoretical trajectory of Metamodernism. While V&vdA initially positioned the term as descriptive and sought to avoid a normative "programme" 9, the necessity of articulating a post-postmodern condition implies a need to transcend the inertia of the preceding era.4 Consequently, the descriptive framework was quickly leveraged by cultural actors, such as Luke Turner and Hanzi Freinacht, who sought to articulate a plan-of-action.11 This suggests that defining a new structure of feeling following a period of critical paralysis inevitably assumes an implicit programmatic function, even against the founders' original intent.

## III. The Conceptual Architecture: Oscillation and Metaxis

Metamodernism’s conceptual architecture is anchored in two related concepts: the physical mechanism of oscillation and the philosophical state of metaxis. These concepts define the dynamic, unstable nature of the contemporary sensibility.

### 3.1. The Metaphor of the Pendulum: Dynamic Instability

To articulate this new "structure of feeling," V&vdA introduced the metaphor of the pendulum. This pendulum is defined by its continual oscillation between the sincere seriousness and utopian impulse of Modernism and the ironic playfulness and skepticism of Postmodernism.6 The term oscillation is crucial: it is not a stable synthesis or a static balance between the two poles, but rather a constant, dynamic swinging "between 2, 3, 5, 10, innumerable poles".5

This dynamic instability is self-regulating through metaphorical gravitational pull.5 The system avoids stagnation or collapse into ideological extremes: "Each time the metamodern enthusiasm swings toward fanaticism, gravity pulls it back toward irony; the moment its irony sways toward apathy, gravity pulls it back toward enthusiasm".5 This mechanism allows the metamodern subject to engage with high ideals without suffering from ideological naïveté and to critique without dissolving into cynical paralysis.4

### 3.2. Metaxis: Philosophical Grounding in 'In-Betweenness'

The prefix meta in Metamodernism is understood to derive from the Platonic concept of metaxis, which describes the simultaneous condition of "between and beyond" diametrically opposed poles.4 This philosophical grounding refines the idea of oscillation from a mere aesthetic preference into a structural condition of existence.5

The concept of metaxis implies a radical displacement in both temporal and spatial perception.6 While Modernism suggested a necessary temporal ordering (progress), and Postmodernism implied a spatial disordering (fragmentation and pastiche), the Metamodern condition is understood as a space-time that is "both—neither ordered and disordered".6 The individual is situated in a constant state of "simultaneously here, there, and nowhere".6 This defining feature highlights the double-bind of the metamodern experience: the individual is destined to pursue a "horizon that is forever receding".6 Action is compelled by sincere desire, even though critical awareness dictates that transcendence or perfect resolution is permanently out of reach.4

The sophistication of the metaxis concept serves as the primary theoretical defense against arguments that Metamodernism is simply a synthesis or a late, gentler phase of Postmodernism. By defining the condition as an institution of tension—the double-bind of both/neither 6—Metamodernism shifts the critique from the content being analyzed to the structure of engagement itself.

### 3.3. Key Sensibilities: Informed Naivety and Neo-Romanticism

The result of this dynamic structural oscillation is a characteristic disposition or sensibility. This is described as a "pragmatic idealism" or "informed naivety".5 This sensibility acknowledges the failures of modern utopianism but nonetheless insists on the necessity of striving for positive change.

The aesthetic expression of this sensibility is frequently described as the Neoromantic Turn.6 This resurgence of a Romantic sensibility, observed in architecture (Herzog & de Meuron) and the work of artists like Bas Jan Ader and Olafur Eliasson 7, is a key aesthetic marker. When these artists look back at the Romantic, they do so neither for simple mockery (parody) nor simple yearning (nostalgia).6 Instead, they engage in a serious attempt to perceive the Romantic ideal anew, utilizing contemporary critical tools to salvage meaningful feeling and aspiration from cynicism.6 This Neoromanticism is identified by V&vdA as the clearest expression of the metamodern cultural logic.6

## IV. The Aesthetics of Metamodernism and Cultural Resonance

The core principles of Metamodernism manifest across contemporary culture through distinct aesthetic strategies, primarily centered on the return of **genuine emotional engagement.**

### 4.1. The Affective Turn and New Sincerity

The shift away from Postmodern depthlessness required the rehabilitation of feeling, giving rise to what is termed the Affective Turn.12 Postmodern critical theory posited that affect had waned, but contemporary practices demonstrate a clear return to "humanist values such as empathy and mercifulness" in art.8

Central to this aesthetic movement is the concept of New Sincerity, a term popularized earlier by figures like American author David Foster Wallace in the 1990s.13 New Sincerity is closely related to post-postmodernism and serves as a key characteristic of Metamodernism.13 It involves a deliberate movement away from cynical, derisive detachment, acknowledging the destruction caused by irony alone.14 Crucially, New Sincerity does not require the avoidance of irony entirely. Instead, it employs a "particular brand of irony, which is sympathetic and warm, and allows its authors to remain committed to the ideals that they discuss, while also being somewhat ironic about this commitment".13 The metamodern aesthetic understands that sincerity and irony can coexist in the same moment, and that the critical awareness provided by irony does not necessarily diminish the genuine emotional connection.5

### 4.2. Depthiness vs. Depthlessness

To counter the established postmodern critique of cultural depthlessness (Fredric Jameson), scholar Alison Gibbons introduced the term depthiness.3 Depthiness describes a new cultural logic in which artworks imbue that which is fictional with a heightened "reality-effect".3

This is achieved through specific techniques, such as the frequent use of Present Tenses in narrative, which project a reader’s immediate, visceral immersion in the constructed fictional world.15 Rather than expanding fiction into reality, depthiness insists on the reality of the fiction.3 This technique is often coupled with a thematic engagement with real, acute contemporary crises—such as depression, bullying, or cultural alienation.3 By focusing on these issues, metamodernist art attempts to elicit strong empathy from the audience, shifting the subject from a nihilistic, detached sensibility to an engaged and interested subjectivity.12 This approach transforms art into a medium that "seeks political activism" through genuine emotional connection and affective resonance.12

### 4.3. Manifestations in Visual and Performing Arts

Metamodernism is readily observed across numerous media:

- Film and Television: The films of directors like Wes Anderson, Michel Gondry, and Spike Jonze 5 are often cited for their characteristic blend of meticulously curated irony (postmodern aesthetic) and deep, often fragile, sincerity (modernist emotional core). Similarly, television series such as Bojack Horseman, Community, and The Good Place utilize hyper-ironic, self-referential, or absurd premises (postmodern tools) to explore profound ethical dilemmas, emotional vulnerability, and the "flawed hero" trope.16 These narratives leverage humor and critique as a protective layer, allowing the audience to engage with difficult sincerity without succumbing to sentimentality.
- Architecture and Visual Art: The architecture of Herzog & de Meuron and the installations of artists like Bas Jan Ader embody the neoromantic sensibility, representing an "unsuccessful negotiation"—a simultaneous double-bind—that characterizes the metamodern search for meaning.6
- Digital Culture and Networked Aesthetics: The pervasiveness of the metamodern sensibility is particularly evident in participatory digital culture, suggesting that the oscillation model is structurally optimized for networked, decentralized creation and consumption.17
- Memes: Memes function as inherently metamodern artifacts, operating on layers of ironic detachment and visual pastiche combined with deeply sincere, shared affective communication and collective in-group understanding.17
- Fanfiction and Remixes: These creative acts embody "informed naivety".7 Participants understand that they are creating content based on existing material (a postmodern acceptance of pastiche) and acknowledge they may never "make History" (a critique of the modernist vision of progress). Yet, they are motivated by a sincere belief in the intrinsic meaning of creating something new and establishing genuine community.17

The fact that the oscillation model is so successfully adopted by mass digital culture demonstrates that the paradigm shift has moved beyond elite art critique and is now embedded in the functional logic of contemporary cultural production.

## V. Metamodernism as a Developmental and Political Paradigm

The theoretical framework, initially descriptive, quickly expanded into prescriptive and political spheres, addressing the inertia created by Postmodernism's critical paralysis.

### 5.1. The Role of the Manifesto: Prescriptive Romanticism

A crucial step in Metamodernism’s evolution was the publication of The Metamodernist Manifesto by Luke Turner in 2011.4 Turner explicitly framed Metamodernism as a quest for "elusive horizons" and a need to liberate society from the "cynical insincerity" resulting from the past century.4

The manifesto introduced the concept of pragmatic romanticism, which defines metamodernism as the "mercurial condition between and beyond irony and sincerity, naivety and knowingness, relativism and truth, optimism and doubt".4 This text functions as a decisive programmatic break, proposing movement through oscillation, where diametrically opposed ideas propel the world into action like the "pulsating polarities of a colossal electric machine".4

### 5.2. Hanzi Freinacht and Developmental Politics

The most comprehensive extension of Metamodernism into political theory is found in the work of Hanzi Freinacht (a writer utilizing a collective pseudonym). Freinacht defines Metamodernism not just as an aesthetic movement, but as a developmental stage and a comprehensive political ideology.18

Freinacht’s work integrates Metamodernism with Integral Theory, leading to a political philosophy centered on "vertical growth," emphasizing measurable developmental stages—a clear return to a type of Modernist faith in structural progress.2 This theoretical foundation is elaborated in his books, including The Listening Society and Nordic Ideology.7 The political goal is radical: promoting not merely happiness, but the "highest possible states of consciousness" for all citizens.18 This necessitates government policy focused on environmental sustainability, economic liberalism, and substantial spending on social programs, reflective of the green-social-liberal mainstream found in Nordic countries.7

The political model attempts to operationalize the pendulum metaphor, using the awareness derived from postmodern critique to guide modernist progress. Freinacht addresses the core political tension of increasing personal freedom while simultaneously requiring intimacy of control necessary for development.20 This relationship is managed through a complex cycle of individuation (leading to higher levels of personal development), alienation, integration, potential oppression/suffocation, and subsequent resistance/emancipation.20 The model recognizes that working for global improvement requires a genuine sense of tragedy—"loving until it hurts"—driven by a perceived "depth as beauty, mystery and tragedy".19

### 5.3. The Return of the Grand Narrative

A primary political divergence from Postmodernism is Metamodernism’s acceptance of the need for grand narratives and unifying political purpose.2 Postmodernism nullified any sense of unifying purpose.1 Metamodernism, conversely, accepts "progress, hierarchy, sincerity, spirituality, development, grand narratives, party politics".2

The ideology espouses a utopian vision, yet maintains the critical knowledge that "no vision will ever be the 'right one'".18 This pursuit of a receding, elusive horizon 6 is the fundamental political manifestation of metaxis. It justifies the intense effort required for political transformation—a necessary commitment to idealism (Modernist) that is ethically guarded by the certainty of its own limitations (Postmodernist).

## VI. Critical Assessments and Theoretical Limitations

While Metamodernism has provided a robust framework for interpreting contemporary culture, it faces significant academic and ethical scrutiny regarding its theoretical necessity and its capacity to address the negative consequences of the Affective Turn.

### 6.1. Challenges to Theoretical Necessity

Metamodernism often draws criticism that it fails to adequately differentiate itself from other "post-postmodern" phenomena or that it is merely a stylistic variation of late Postmodernism.10 This critique challenges the necessity of creating an entirely new paradigm label.

Furthermore, competitors such as Alan Kirby's Hypermodernism (or Digimodernism) argue that the fundamental shift is driven by technology and user interaction, not by affective negotiation.8 If the oscillation is viewed as a mere side effect of digital media that fosters both ironic detachment (memes) and sincere emotional sharing (vlogging), then the metamodern claim of a distinct structural shift in the "structure of feeling" is weakened. Metamodern scholars must continuously demonstrate that the negotiation of affect is the primary cultural logic, and that the digital landscape is merely the medium enabling that specific structure.

### 6.2. Critique of Prescriptivism and Theoretical Purity

An internal tension exists between the founders’ initial descriptive project and the subsequent programmatic applications. V&vdA expressly intended the term to be descriptive, periodizing the present rather than offering a normative roadmap.9 However, the enthusiastic adoption of Luke Turner’s Manifesto and the comprehensive political philosophy of Hanzi Freinacht confirm the programmatic drift.9

This shift poses a danger: if Metamodernism is adopted as a "programme," it risks succumbing to the "ideological naivety" characteristic of early Modernism, the very pitfall it sought to avoid.4 The transition from aesthetic theory to political action raises fundamental questions about whether the complex, dynamic balance of oscillation can be successfully translated into stable institutional policy without sacrificing the critical checks provided by irony.

### 6.3. The Ethical Dilemma: The Dark Side of Sincerity

The most profound critique of the Metamodern framework relates to the ethical implications of the Affective Turn. The return of depth and sincere commitment is inherently ambiguous; it is not intrinsically good or progressive.12

The framework’s focus on intensified affect proves problematic when confronted with forms of renewed commitment that are actively destructive, such as "White Nationalism and rampant far-right ideologies".12 These movements gain prominence through strong, sincere communal ties and an intensified commitment to deeply felt, if toxic, grand narratives.12 The new sincerity of the metamodern subject can, in this context, be "altogether scarier than the nihilistic sensibilities of the postmodern" because it provides the motivating force for fanaticism.12 The possibility of the pendulum swinging toward "moderate fanaticism" 5 demonstrates that the simple oscillation structure is insufficient as a critical tool; it merely describes the structural capacity for intense belief, regardless of the belief's ethical valence.

Furthermore, early metamodern scholarship has been criticized for its insufficient engagement with crucial recent socio-political upheavals, such as the realities surrounding Brexit, the rise of Donald Trump, and deeper analyses of social media consumption post-2016.12 These events, marked by sincere ideological commitment often perceived as irrational or regressive, highlight a sociological blind spot that mandates a more robust critical framework for differentiating between constructive and destructive sincerity. The challenge, therefore, is for Metamodernism to evolve from a descriptive aesthetic framework into a comprehensive ethical and political philosophy that can provide criteria for judging the merit of the sincere commitments it observes.

## VII. Conclusion: Trajectories and Future Research

Metamodernism provides a critical and timely lens through which to understand the cultural and political shifts of the 21st century. It successfully articulates the cultural impasse left by the exhaustion of Postmodernism and offers a structural solution—the dynamic tension of metaxis—that enables action without demanding ideological certainty.7 Its strength lies in its ability to reconcile the necessity of hope and enthusiasm with the critical awareness of ideological fragility, fostering a complex "informed naivety" that permeates art, media, and politics.5

However, the analysis of critical assessments reveals that the Metamodern project cannot rest solely on aesthetic observation. The structural observation of oscillation is insufficient to address the ethical dangers posed by the dark side of sincerity, where intensified affect fuels harmful ideologies.12

Future research must therefore prioritize the following avenues:

1. Ethical and Critical Philosophy: Developing robust criteria for distinguishing between forms of positive, pragmatic idealism and forms of destructive, intensified commitment. This requires moving beyond merely describing the mechanism of oscillation to defining the ethical quality of the poles and the trajectory of the swing.
2. Political and Developmental Science: Rigorously analyzing the feasibility and potential authoritarian risks of developmental political models, such as Hanzi Freinacht’s framework, when applied to diverse, non-Nordic political systems. This involves evaluating how the complex cycles of alienation and integration 20 can be managed institutionally to genuinely promote freedom and vertical growth.
3. Advanced Media Studies: Expanding the analysis of the oscillation dynamic within immersive, interactive media (e.g., video games and virtual reality), which inherently blend aesthetic surface (pastiche, spectacle) with depthy, affective engagement (moral choices, intense narrative commitment), further testing the boundaries of depthiness and the reality-effect in constructed worlds.

# Follow-Up Questions

> Metamodernism seems fairly Apollonian, no?

That is a very insightful observation. Metamodernism certainly presents characteristics that align strongly with the Apollonian principle, though the defining feature of the paradigm is its constant, dynamic negotiation with both the Apollonian and the Dionysian.

You are correct that Metamodernism often appears Apollonian in its *structure* and *intent*. Friedrich Nietzsche's distinction defines the **Apollonian** as the impulse toward light, reason, harmony, balance, and prophesy.1

Metamodernism reflects this Apollonian spirit in several key ways:

1. **The Quest for Order and Balance:** The core mechanism of Metamodernism is the principle of **oscillation**—a pendulum swinging between the sincere enthusiasm of Modernism and the ironic skepticism of Postmodernism.21This deliberate, self-regulating motion is inherently Apollonian, as it is designed to achieve a precarious, dynamic **balance** that prevents a collapse into ideological extremes like fanaticism (too much enthusiasm) or apathy (too much irony).31
2. **The Integration of Reason:** The prefix *meta* derives from *metaxis*, the Platonic concept of 'in-betweenness.' This condition is structurally related to *taxis*, which means **ordering** or arrangement.3 Metamodernism is explicitly described as an effort to achieve an "integration of reason and sensibility," valuing order and critical self-awareness (reason) even as it pursues heartfelt goals (sensibility).
3. **The Pursuit of the Horizon:** The Metamodern subject is compelled to pursue an "elusive horizon" 4 or "utopian action" 2, which reflects a deep-seated belief in progress, hierarchy, and grand narratives—all concepts rejected by Postmodernism but central to the Modernist, ordered, Apollonian worldview.5

### The Role of the Dionysian

However, Metamodernism is not *purely* Apollonian; it is the *negotiation* of the two forces that defines it. The **Dionysian**represents the chaotic, ecstatic, emotional, and tragic impulse.4

Metamodernism consciously incorporates the Dionysian element as one of its necessary poles:

- **Sincerity and Affect:** Metamodernism's shift is defined by the **Affective Turn** and the rehabilitation of *sincerity* and *genuine emotional connection* 6, which align with the passionate, non-rational energies of the Dionysian. The political movement described by Hanzi Freinacht, for example, is driven by a genuine sense of tragedy—"loving until it hurts".7
- **The Continuum of Energies:** The oscillation is described as movement on a "multidimensional continuum of energies and intensities," swinging between *hardness* (akin to Apollonian firmness/ruthlessness) and *softness* (akin to Dionysian sensitivity/empathy).

Ultimately, Metamodernism attempts to harness the structural order of the Apollonian to manage the passionate, sometimes chaotic, drive of the Dionysian. The constant shedding of "yesterday's certainties" to make room for new meaning is a "practice of becoming" that uses deconstruction (Dionysian dissolution) not out of cynicism, but to allow for new form (Apollonian creation). The paradigm is therefore best understood as the **dynamic tension** between both forces, continually reshaped by the structural demands of the oscillation.5

> Thank you for that clarification. Are there metamodern practices or praxis? 

That is an excellent follow-up question. While Metamodernism originated as a descriptive framework to articulate the new "structure of feeling" after Postmodernism, it has quickly evolved into a distinct set of #**praxis**—or intentional, actionable practices—across cultural, political, and personal domains.

This shift from describing cultural aesthetics to advocating for a “plan-of-action” is a crucial point of departure from Postmodernism, which often dissolved the capacity for sincere action.

Metamodern praxis can be broken down into three main categories:

### I. Aesthetic and Cultural Methods

In the arts, metamodern practices are methods employed to intentionally create the dynamic tension between sincerity and irony. The fundamental goal of this aesthetic praxis is to protect the "solidity of felt experience" from postmodern irony or modernist reductionism.

Key aesthetic practices include:

- **Oscillation:** The core practice of consciously swinging between seemingly opposed cultural logics, such as sincerity and irony, or apathy and intense affect, often within the same work of art or cultural product.1
- **Ironesty:** A blend of irony and sincerity that allows an artist to remain committed to their ideals while retaining critical self-awareness about that commitment.2
- **Constructive Pastiche:** Unlike the postmodern pastiche that merely juxtaposed previous forms cynically, metamodern pastiche uses existing material (like in remixes or fanfiction) to genuinely build something new and meaningful, demonstrating a belief in the "intrinsic meaning of creating something new".2
- **Performatism and Quirky:** These methods involve the use of highly contrived or stylized elements (like the "quirky" aesthetic found in Wes Anderson films) to create a heightened “reality-effect” that encourages the viewer’s immediate, visceral immersion and emotional engagement.2
- **The Tiny and The Epic:** A frequent practice is the oscillation between metamodern minimalism (The Tiny) and metamodern maximalism (The Epic), reflecting a sensibility that is both modest in scope and ambitious in feeling.2

### II. Political and Societal Praxis (The Listening Society)

The most robust example of metamodern political praxis is the theory of the "Listening Society," primarily articulated by Hanzi Freinacht. This work moves from aesthetic theory to a comprehensive political ideology that advocates for a systemic plan of action,.

The political praxis focuses on promoting “vertical growth” and the "highest possible states of consciousness" for citizens, requiring practical interventions:

1. **Systemic Psychological Support:** The practice of ensuring deep human welfare beyond material needs. Specific policy recommendations include ensuring that every person benefits from talking to a professional therapist while growing up.
2. **Inner Work and Consciousness Training:** Implementing public programs that teach meditation, including mindfulness and other techniques, to help people handle stress and connect with their own emotions.
3. **Developmental Policy:** Centering policy around the measured developmental stages of society, which is a return to a Modernist acceptance of progress, hierarchy, and grand narratives, but informed by Postmodern critique.5
4. **Crowdsourced and Process-Oriented Politics:** This approach is exemplified by the Danish political party *The Alternative*, which incorporates metamodern elements. This party was formed not around a readymade political platform, but around principles for good discourse, with specific policies being crowdsourced in "political laboratories" in cooperation with the people,. This emphasizes the *how* (the process) over the *what* (the program).

### III. Personal and Developmental Praxis

On an individual level, Metamodernism is understood as a **stage of personal development**. The praxis here is focused on the ongoing internal work required to maintain the dynamic tension of *metaxis*:

- **The Art of Becoming:** This involves a conscious "practice of becoming" through the constant deconstruction of "yesterday's certainties" to make room for new meaning. It is not deconstruction for the sake of cynicism, but to allow for sincere re-creation.
- **Informed Naivety:** The personal practice of committing to idealistic goals and emotional sincerity, while remaining fully aware—and using—the critical tools of postmodernism to avoid dogmatism.1
- **Inhabiting Dualities:** The capacity to inhabit "multiple selves and truths with both suspicion and sincerity", recognizing that inner-personal growth is integral to political and cultural understanding.5

## Gesturing Toward a Transcendent Metaphysics?

> Does metamodernism or its related ideologies gesture toward a transcendent metaphysics?

Metamodernism and the "Liminal Web" certainly gesture toward a transcendent metaphysics, but they do so in a way that is distinctly different from traditional religion or modern New Age spirituality. Because metamodernism is defined by **oscillation**, it treats metaphysics not as a static set of dogmas, but as a "necessary myth" or a "functional truth."

Given your background in philosophy, cosmology, and consciousness (CIIS) and your work on an idealist alternative to transhumanism, this is likely where the most fertile ground for your critique lies.

Here is how these movements engage with the transcendent:

---

### 1. The Return of the "Grand Narrative" (Metanarrative)

Postmodernism’s defining feature was "incredulity toward metanarratives" (Lyotard).1 Metamodernism, however, suggests that humans *cannot live* without a sense of overarching purpose or "Big Story."

- **The Gesture:** It gestures toward a transcendent "telos"—a direction or purpose to evolution and human history.
- **The Nuance:** It doesn't claim to have the *absolute* truth.2 Instead, it engages in **"Informed Naiveté."** It says, "We know that all stories are constructed, but we choose to act *as if* there is a transcendent meaning to prevent nihilism."3

### 2. The "Both/And" of Immanence and Transcendence

Metamodernism often adopts a **Panentheistic** or **Non-dual** flavor, frequently influenced by the thinkers you follow (Kastrup, McGilchrist, and Vervaeke).

- **Materialism vs. Spirit:** Rather than choosing between a dead, materialist universe (Modernism) or a purely subjective, linguistic one (Postmodernism), metamodernism explores the idea that **Consciousness or Mind** is fundamental.
- **The Liminal Web Influence:** Figures like John Vervaeke discuss "Sacredness" without necessarily requiring a supernatural deity.4 They point toward a *transcendent function*—the ability of the human mind to go beyond itself and connect with the "Moreness" of reality.5

### 3. Developmental Metaphysics (Integral Influence)

Much of the Liminal Web is rooted in **Integral Theory** (Ken Wilber), which is explicitly metaphysical.

- **The Great Chain of Being:** It views the universe as a series of nested "holons" (parts that are also wholes), moving toward greater complexity and consciousness.
- **Evolutionary Spirituality:** This framework suggests that the universe itself is "waking up" through us.6 This is a transcendent gesture because it implies that our subjective experience is not an accident of biology, but a manifestation of a cosmic process.

### 4. Metaphysical "Serious Play"

In metamodern art and philosophy, there is a concept called **"The Sublime."** * **The Gesture:** It acknowledges that there are aspects of reality (the infinite, the void, the divine) that exceed human categorization.

- **The Critique:** Unlike traditional metaphysics, which tries to define the transcendent, metamodernism *gestures* toward it through irony and sincerity combined. It uses "serious play" to point at the divine while simultaneously acknowledging the limitations of our language to describe it.

## Idealist Metaphysics vs. Oscillating Metaphysics

Mapping the distinction between these two frameworks is crucial for your book, as it highlights the difference between a **functional strategy for living** (Metamodernism) and a **fundamental claim about reality** (Ontological Idealism).

While the Liminal Web often uses Idealist language, its philosophical "commitment" is usually quite different from the rigorous Analytic Idealism of someone like Bernardo Kastrup.

---

### The Comparison: Firm Idealism vs. Oscillating Metamodernism

| **Feature**            | **Firm Ontological Idealism (e.g., Kastrup / CIIS)**                                                                 | **Metamodern "Oscillating" Metaphysics**                                                                                              |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Status of Reality**  | **Literal.** Reality is fundamentally mental (Mind-at-Large). Matter is the *appearance* of localized consciousness. | **Pragmatic.** It doesn't claim to know what reality *is*, but recognizes that acting "as if" it has meaning is better than nihilism. |
| **The Transcendent**   | **Ontological.** Transcendence is a return to the underlying unity of Mind. It is a fact of nature.                  | **Functional.** It is a "Necessary Myth" or a "Sacred Canopy" used to coordinate human behavior and sensemaking.                      |
| **Truth Claim**        | **Propositional.** Claims that Physicalism is logically and empirically incoherent.                                  | **Perspectival.** Claims that we need multiple perspectives (modern, postmodern, indigenous) to "triangulate" truth.                  |
| **View of the "Self"** | A "dissociated alter" of a universal mind. De-complexifying leads to the Whole.                                      | A "holon" in a developmental stack. Growing/Waking up leads to higher complexity.                                                     |
| **Role of Irony**      | Secondary. Logic and phenomenology lead to a sincere realization of unity.                                           | **Central.** Irony is the "failsafe" that prevents the metamodernist from becoming a **fundamentalist**.                              |

### 1. The "Truth" vs. The "Tool"

For a "firm" Idealist, the mental nature of reality is a discovery about the world, akin to a scientist discovering the laws of thermodynamics. It provides a stable, bedrock foundation from which to view technology, AI, and the human soul.

In contrast, Metamodernism—and by extension, much of the Liminal Web—is often **post-metaphysical**. They are weary of making "Big Truth" claims because they’ve inherited the postmodern fear of totalizing systems. Instead, they use metaphysics as a **tool for sensemaking**. They treat the transcendent as a "hyper-object" or a "liminal space" that we can point to but never fully inhabit.

### 2. The Danger of "Metaphysical LARPing"

This leads to a primary critique you might explore: **Is the Liminal Web actually religious, or is it "Metaphysical LARPing" (Live Action Role Playing)?**

- **Firm Idealism** demands a shift in identity: You are not a "user" of consciousness; you *are* a localized segment of it.
- **Metamodernism** often keeps the "Ego" in the driver's seat, choosing which metaphysical "lens" to wear based on the current context (e.g., "I'll be a Buddhist for this meditation, a Scientist for this code, and a Romantic for this sunset").

### 3. Application to your Anti-Transhumanist Critique

This distinction is vital for your alternative to transhumanism. Transhumanism is built on a **firm Physicalist metaphysics** (the mind is software, the brain is hardware).

- A **Metamodern** critique might say: "Transhumanism is a limited narrative; we should oscillate toward a more organic narrative." (This is a weak defense because it treats both as equally 'valid' stories).
- A **Firm Idealist** (Kastrupian) critique says: "Transhumanism is based on a category error. You cannot 'upload' consciousness because consciousness is the medium, not the data. Silicon is just another image in the mind."

One is a **stylistic** preference for the "human"; the other is an **ontological** argument that the transhumanist project is a literal delusion based on a misunderstanding of what matter is.
