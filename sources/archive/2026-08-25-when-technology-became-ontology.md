# When Technology Became Ontology

> When I think of Descartes gazing at the water-driven automata at Saint-Germain-en-Laye and thinking that must be how nature and the body is, or all his successors gazing at other man-made technologies and making similar metaphorical leaps, it makes these vaunted thinkers seem almost daft for being so easily influenced by man-made objects in that way

It does feel absurd in hindsight—watching a philosopher stare at a hydraulic statue popping out of a French royal fountain and immediately declare that human neurology is essentially fancy plumbing.

Yet that historical leap wasn't pure naivety; it was driven by a desperate attempt to escape medieval mysticism, which eventually calcified into a rigid intellectual habit.

**Overthrowing Occult Essences** In the early 17th century, Aristotelian scholasticism explained the natural world through invisible "forms," "essences," and teleological purposes—claiming, for example, that fire rises because it desires the heavens, or that opium causes sleep due to its innate "dormitive virtue". To Descartes, the water-driven automata at Saint-Germain-en-Laye were revolutionary because their causal mechanics were **visible, physical, and mathematically measurable**. If water pushing through hidden valves could make a mechanical Diana draw a bow, then complex bodily behavior could be explained without invoking mystical souls or occult forces.

**The Illusion of Maker's Knowledge** Humans consistently model what they *cannot* explain using the most complex technology they *can* build.

- **The Epistemic Crutch:** Because we design and assemble our own machines, we understand their causal pathways from the inside out.
- **The Projection:** When faced with the baffling complexity of biological organisms, thinkers instinctively borrow the blueprints of their latest inventions—watermills, clocks, telephone switchboards, or transformer models—to create an illusion of comprehension.

**When the Heuristic Became Dogma** The real intellectual misstep was not using the machine as a temporary heuristic to study biology, but mistaking the synthetic tool for the living reality. As Herbert Simon observed, studying our own engineered artifacts is a "science of the artificial," not a discovery of natural law.

By forgetting that the machine was merely an explanatory crutch, centuries of thinkers committed what Alfred North Whitehead called the *Fallacy of Misplaced Concreteness*: they projected man-made engineering onto the living world and then marveled at how "mechanical" nature appeared.

Do you think this tendency to mistake our tools for ourselves is an inevitable feature of human cognition, or is it unique to Western scientific materialism?

## Other Examples of Technology as Mirror

> I'm reminded of Vallor's book AI Mirror here. What other examples from the Western tradition are relevant between Descartes and Vallor?

Between Descartes' grottos and Shannon Vallor’s *The AI Mirror*, Western intellectual history formed an unbroken loop of polishing the latest engineered artifact, projecting it onto human nature, and mistaking the synthetic reflection for living reality. #mirror #technology #metaphors 

**18th-Century Clockwork Automata and Mechanical Materialism**

- **Julien Offray de La Mettrie (*L'Homme Machine*, 1747):** Collapsed Descartes’ separation of mind and matter by declaring that the conscious soul is simply a complex arrangement of mechanical springs, gears, and clockwork cogs.
- **David Hartley’s Physiological Associationism (1749):** Modeled thought and memory as physical vibrations (*vibratiuncles*) in nerve cords, directly importing the physics of musical strings and pendulum harmonics to explain the association of ideas.

**19th-Century Steam, Telegraphy, and Industrial Engines**

- **Charles Babbage and Ada Lovelace (1840s):** Modeled formal human reasoning on the Jacquard loom and mechanical brass gears, structuring the Analytical Engine into "The Mill" (processing core) and "The Store" (memory).
- **Thermodynamic Brains and Epiphenomenalism (Helmholtz & Huxley):** Following Hermann von Helmholtz's measurements of nerve conduction and energy conservation, Thomas Henry Huxley cast human consciousness as the passive "steam whistle on a locomotive engine"—a byproduct of thermodynamic mechanics with zero causal power.
- **Early Psychoanalysis (Freud):** Borrowed directly from 19th-century steam-engine physics, framing the psyche as a closed hydraulic system governed by "pressure," "repression," and "discharge."

**Mid-20th-Century Cybernetics and Telecommunications**

- **The Telephone Switchboard:** Neurophysiologists and behavioral psychologists recast the brain as a central relay exchange, explaining human reflex arcs and attention as physical wire routing.
- **The Cybernetics Movement (McCulloch, Pitts, & Wiener, 1940s–50s):** The Macy Conferences formally mapped biological neurons onto electrical vacuum tubes and binary logic gates, asserting that nervous systems are literal electronic feedback circuits.

**Late-20th-Century Computational Functionalism**

- **Software and Hardware Separation (Putnam & Fodor, 1960s–70s):** Hilary Putnam and Jerry Fodor decoupled the mind from biological wetware, framing cognition as abstract, substrate-independent software ("Mentalese") executing formal syntactic rules.
- **Blackboards to Global Workspaces (Baars, 1980s):** Cognitive science borrowed 1970s multi-agent AI "blackboard" data buffers to formulate Global Workspace Theory, turning a software routing solution into the baseline model for human conscious awareness.
