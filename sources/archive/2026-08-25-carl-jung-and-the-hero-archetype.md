# Carl Jung and the Hero Archetype

> Is it accurate to say that Carl Jung was critiquing the hero's journey or monomyth in his Red Book? Not in direct response to the theories of Joseph Campbell (because it predates Campbell's work) but the larger concept of a hero's journey

Yes, it is highly accurate to say that Carl Jung critiques the "hero's journey"—or more specifically, the **dominance of the Hero archetype**—in *The Red Book*. #herojourney #monomyth #shadow 

In fact, one of the most pivotal and shocking scenes in the entire book is the **murder of the Hero**, which Jung enacts as a necessary psychological sacrifice.

While Joseph Campbell later synthesized the "Hero's Journey" (Monomyth) as a universal cycle of transformation, Jung’s *Red Book* suggests that the Hero is not the pinnacle of human development but a stage that must be transcended (and even "killed") to achieve true wholeness (Individuation).

Here is a breakdown of how this critique manifests in *The Red Book* and why it aligns with your book's critique of transhumanism.

### 1. The Murder of Siegfried

In a visionary chapter titled "Murder of the Hero," Jung dreams that he and a savage youth ambush and shoot **Siegfried**, the legendary dragon-slaying hero of Germanic mythology.

- **Symbolism:** Siegfried represents the "Spirit of the Times" (rationalism, willful conquest, the perfect ego, the "sun" consciousness). He is the figure who imposes his will upon nature and chaos.
- **The Critique:** Jung realizes that Siegfried had to die because he represented an "attitude" that was no longer viable. The Hero's way is to conquer the unconscious (the dragon), but Jung’s new path required *submission* to the unconscious to integrate it. As long as the Hero (the conquering ego) rules, the Soul (the deeper Self) cannot speak.

### 2. The Hero as a Limitation

Jung’s critique is that the Hero archetype is essentially an **Ego-ideal**. The Hero fights, strives, achieves, and overcomes. However, to become fully human (individuated), one must eventually stop fighting and learn to listen, suffer, and accept.

- **Campbell’s View:** The Hero returns from the journey with a boon to save society. The cycle often glorifies the active principle.
- **Jung’s *Red Book* View:** The Hero is a valid stage for the first half of life (building the ego, establishing oneself in the world). But in the second half of life—the phase Jung was entering during his "confrontation with the unconscious"—maintaining the Hero pose becomes a pathology. It blocks the "Spirit of the Depths."

### 3. "Force" vs. "Acceptance"

This distinction is vital for your book on transhumanism:

- **The Heroic/Transhumanist Path:** Seeks to overcome limits, conquer death, and optimize the human condition through will and technology. It is the Siegfried archetype on steroids—"I will slay the dragon of biology."
- **The Jungian/Humane Path:** Acknowledges that true psychological maturity comes from accepting limits and integrating the "shadow"—the parts of us that are weak, mortal, and irrational.

> "The highest truth is one and the same with the absurd... If the hero in you is slain, then the sun of the depths rises in you."

> — Carl Jung, The Red Book

### Summary for your Book

You can accurately argue that while Campbell mapped the *cycle* of the Hero, Jung (in his private, deeper work) identified the **limit** of the Hero. Jung warns that if we remain identified with the Hero too long, we become rigid, disconnected from our souls, and potentially destructive—a perfect parallel to the hubris of modern transhumanism.
