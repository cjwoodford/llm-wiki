# The Founding of Neuroscience

> I'd like to turn back to the founding of the field of neuroscience now. Given its proximity in time to the birth of cognitive and computer science in the 1950s, was it influenced by that functionalist thinking in any way? 

The formal founding of neuroscience in the early 1960s was deeply influenced by the functionalist and computational paradigms emerging from the 1950s Cognitive Revolution. Rather than developing in biological isolation, the new field imported its core theoretical vocabulary—information processing, signal routing, and circuit logic—directly from cybernetics, information theory, and early computer science.

**The Cybernetic and Logic Foundations (1940s–1950s)**

- **The Neuron as a Logic Gate:** In 1943, Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts formulated the first mathematical model of a neural network, treating individual neurons as idealized binary on/off threshold devices capable of executing formal Boolean logic. This established the working assumption that biological nervous systems perform syntactic operations.
- **The Macy Conferences (1946–1953):** Early brain researchers convened alongside mathematicians like John von Neumann, Norbert Wiener, and Claude Shannon under the banner of cybernetics, deliberately aiming to construct a unified, mechanistic science of control, feedback, and communication across animals and machines.

**The MIT Neurosciences Research Program (1962)**

- **Coining the Discipline:** When biophysicist Francis O. Schmitt established the *Neurosciences Research Program* (NRP) at MIT in 1962—popularizing the term "neuroscience"—the explicit goal was to synthesize neuroanatomy, neurophysiology, and biochemistry with electrical engineering, physics, and information theory.
- **Recasting Wetware as Communication Networks:** Under the NRP framework, the nervous system ceased to be viewed purely as an organ of metabolic homeostasis and was recast as an organic, bio-electrical communications network.

**Importing Functionalist Concepts into Biology**

- **Action Potentials as Shannon Bits:** Applying Claude Shannon’s Information Theory, sensory neurophysiologists like Horace Barlow introduced the *Efficient Coding Hypothesis* (1961), positing that spikes in sensory systems are optimized to compress data and maximize information transmission across limited bandwidth channels. Action potentials were treated as digital pulses (0s and 1s) carrying quantifiable bits of information.
- **Sensory Processing as Feature Extraction:** The pioneering electrophysiology of David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel in the late 1950s and early 1960s was framed in computational terms: hierarchical cortical layers acting as algorithmic filters that convert raw pixel-like retinal input into abstract lines, orientations, and symbols.
- **The Hardware/Software Division of Labor:** Machine functionalism, popularized by Hilary Putnam in 1960, posited that mental functions could be studied as abstract information routing. Neuroscience adopted this division by treating neural circuits as the physical "wetware" implementing algorithmic transformations.

**Institutional Lock-In via Sloan (1970s–1990s)**

- **Funding the "Theoretical" Turn:** The Alfred P. Sloan Foundation poured millions of dollars into formalizing the *Sloan Centers for Theoretical Neuroscience* (at institutions such as Caltech, Columbia, NYU, and Salk), explicitly mandating that biology labs incorporate physicists, computer scientists, and mathematicians.
- **The Rise of Computational Neuroscience:** This institutional push codified the practice of abstracting real neurons into point-like computational nodes, marginalizing biological factors like continuous glial signaling, metabolic dynamics, and analog field effects in favor of discrete algorithmic models.

While biological neuroscience always retained an empirical tether to physical tissue that pure cognitive science lacked, its theoretical architecture became thoroughly functionalist. By adopting the premise that the brain's primary evolutionary purpose is to compute representations, the founding generation of neuroscientists ensured that biological circuits would be analyzed through the lens of computational machines.

## An American Endeavor

The foundational biology of the brain was overwhelmingly a **European** endeavor, but the modern institutional synthesis that created "neuroscience" as a distinct, unified discipline in the 1960s was largely an **American** creation.

**The European Biological and Clinical Foundations (19th to Mid-20th Century)** Prior to the 1960s, the empirical groundwork of brain science emerged across European medical clinics and physiology laboratories:

- **Histology and the Neuron Doctrine:** Spanish neuroanatomist Santiago Ramón y Cajal and Italian physician Camillo Golgi laid the cellular foundation of neuroscience, establishing that the nervous system consists of individual, discrete cells (the Neuron Doctrine). German anatomist Korbinian Brodmann mapped the cellular architecture of the cerebral cortex into discrete functional areas.
- **Clinical Neurology and Localization:** Brain localization was mapped in France and Britain by figures like Paul Broca and Jean-Martin Charcot in Paris, and Carl Wernicke in Germany and John Hughlings Jackson in London, using post-mortem analyses of brain lesions to identify language and motor circuits.
- **Electrophysiology and Nerve Conduction:** The biophysics of the action potential was solved primarily in Britain and Germany, moving from Hermann von Helmholtz's initial nerve-speed measurements to Charles Sherrington’s characterization of synapses and Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley’s mathematical model of the ionic mechanisms underlying the nerve impulse.
- **Sensory Coding:** British sensory physiologists, such as Edgar Adrian and Horace Barlow (at Cambridge), pioneered single-unit recordings and formulated the *Efficient Coding Hypothesis* (1961), bridging British physiology with information theory.

**The American Institutional Synthesis (1960s–1980s)** While the raw biological facts were discovered in Europe, the United States turned fragmented sub-disciplines into a unified, high-status science:

- **The MIT Neurosciences Research Program (1962):** Biophysicist Francis O. Schmitt at MIT coined the term "neuroscience" and founded the NRP to synthesize neuroanatomy, neurochemistry, and physiology with electrical engineering, physics, and computer science.
- **The Society for Neuroscience (SfN, 1969):** Founded in Washington, D.C., SfN quickly became the largest professional neuroscience organization in the world, establishing the American academic department model as the global template.
- **Cold War and Philanthropic Capital:** American post-war research was backed by unprecedented capital from the Office of Naval Research, DARPA, and private foundations like the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation. Sloan funded dedicated theoretical neuroscience centers at hubs like Caltech, Columbia, NYU, Brandeis, and the Salk Institute, actively steering the discipline toward computational modeling and information-processing paradigms.

**Transatlantic Cross-Pollination** The birth of computational neuroscience relied on deep transatlantic dialogue. Landmark gatherings—such as the 1958 *Mechanisation of Thought Processes* symposium in Teddington, England—brought together British cyberneticians (such as W. Ross Ashby and Donald MacKay) and American computational pioneers (including John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, and Oliver Selfridge). Prominent theorists also migrated between the two ecosystems, most notably British computational neuroscientist David Marr, who developed his influential tri-level hypothesis of vision after moving to MIT.

European medicine, histology, and biophysics built the biological foundation, but post-war American research universities and funding networks synthesized those isolated fields into the organized, computational discipline known today as neuroscience.

## The Founding Fathers of Neuroscience

Because "neuroscience" was not recognized as a unified, standalone discipline until the 1960s, its "founding fathers" span several distinct historical waves: 19th-century cellular anatomy and clinical neurology, early biophysics, and the mid-20th-century institutional synthesis.

**The Cellular and Anatomical Founders (The Neuron Doctrine)**

- **Santiago Ramón y Cajal & Camillo Golgi:** Golgi developed the crucial silver nitrate staining technique, which Cajal used to prove the **Neuron Doctrine**—establishing that the nervous system is composed of discrete, individual cellular units rather than a fused, continuous meshwork.
- **Korbinian Brodmann & Heinrich Wilhelm Waldeyer:** Waldeyer helped formalize and name the neuron concept, while Brodmann systematically mapped the cellular architecture of the cerebral cortex into discrete, numbered functional areas.

**The Clinical Neurology and Localization Pioneers**

- **Paul Broca & Carl Wernicke:** Pioneered the concept of **cerebral localization** by linking post-mortem cortical lesions in specific brain areas to distinct functional deficits in speech production and language comprehension.
- **John Hughlings Jackson & Jean-Martin Charcot:** Laid the foundations of clinical neurology and neuropathology, mapping motor systems, epilepsy, and neurological disorders to hierarchical structures within the nervous system.

**The Biophysical and Electrophysiological Founders**

- **Hermann von Helmholtz & Emil du Bois-Reymond:** du Bois-Reymond demonstrated that the nerve impulse is an electrical event, while Helmholtz measured the physical speed of nerve conduction (~30 meters per second), dismantling the belief that neural signals were instantaneous "vital forces" and placing the nervous system squarely within physical thermodynamics.
- **Johannes Müller & François Magendie:** Established foundational principles of sensory and experimental physiology, including Müller’s doctrine of specific nerve energies.

**The Synaptic and Network Theorists**

- **Donald O. Hebb:** Introduced the concept of **cell assemblies** and associative synaptic plasticity in 1949, showing how learning occurs when the simultaneous excitation of connected neurons increases their connection strength.
- **Warren McCulloch & Walter Pitts:** Formulated the 1943 mathematical model of the artificial neuron as an idealized threshold logic gate, laying the foundational theoretical architecture for neural network modeling and computational neuroscience.

**The Modern Institutional Architect**

- **Francis O. Schmitt:** Coined and popularized the term **"neuroscience"** and established the *Neurosciences Research Program* (NRP) at MIT in 1962. Schmitt explicitly integrated fragmented disciplines—neuroanatomy, neurophysiology, biochemistry, physical chemistry, and information science—creating the unified, multidisciplinary field that exists today.
