# The Epistemology of Evasion: The Emergent Superintelligence Myth in Tort, Antitrust, and Regulatory Strategy

> **Archivist Note**: Ingested on September 28, 2026 from the published monograph analyzing the intersection of frontier foundation model narratives, common law doctrines, statutory regimes, and federal agency enforcement postures. This text details how tech-messianic and catastrophic risk rhetoric functions as a deliberate, structural legal strategy across four core arenas: torts/products liability, fair use/copyright, administrative audits, and antitrust neutralization.

---

## Introduction: Ontological Mystification as Corporate Strategy

The public discourse surrounding frontier artificial intelligence is dominated by declarations of emergent superintelligence, non-human reasoning capabilities, and approaching machine sentience. While casual observers often interpret these narratives as Silicon Valley hyperbole or tech-messianic marketing, their primary function within the political economy of advanced computing is structural and defensive. The myth of the autonomous machine mind performs legal and regulatory work by orchestrating epistemological confusion and temporal displacement, diverting established legal doctrines while insulating corporate balance sheets from traditional forms of legal liability.

Rather than relying solely on conventional lobbying against state intervention, frontier developers deploy the narrative of emergent agency to reshape how judicial and administrative institutions classify their technologies. By presenting current models as autonomous reasoning entities rather than deterministic software products or statistical pattern matching engines, developers obscure the mechanical realities of data ingestion, optimization, and commercial deployment. This framing establishes an ontological break with existing common law and statutory frameworks, convincing policymakers that legacy legal tools are obsolete and that immediate state enforcement must yield to prospective governance regimes.

This strategic mystification operates across four primary doctrinal arenas: products liability torts, intellectual property and fair use, administrative audits, and antitrust enforcement. In each arena, developers pair the discourse of miraculous cognitive emergence with the rhetoric of catastrophic existential risk to deflect immediate accountability while building durable competitive moats.

## Tort Doctrine and the Liability Gambit: From Product Defects to Abnormally Dangerous Activities

### Non-Deterministic Agency and Causation Arbitrage

In private civil litigation, foundation model developers confront the established framework of strict products liability. Under the Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability, commercial sellers and distributors are held strictly liable for physical, reputational, or financial harm caused by manufacturing defects, design defects, or inadequate instructions and warnings. Historically, software developers avoided strict products liability by exploiting doctrinal ambiguities over whether intangible code constitutes a "product" or a "service."

In *Winter v. G.P. Putnam’s Sons* (938 F.2d 1033, 9th Cir. 1991), the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit declined to apply strict products liability to misrepresentations in an encyclopedia of mushrooms that caused severe liver damage, holding that products liability is confined to the tangible world and does not encompass abstract ideas and expressions. While courts have historically extended this logic to computer software that operates primarily as information dissemination, modern courts increasingly treat interactive, consumer-facing software applications as functional products amenable to products liability scrutiny.

To defeat the application of design-defect doctrine, frontier laboratories rely on the narrative of non-deterministic, emergent reasoning. Under standard products liability law, a design defect exists when the foreseeable risks of harm could have been reduced or avoided by adopting a reasonable alternative design. When an artificial intelligence model generates defamatory falsehoods, dispenses lethal medical guidance, or outputs toxic chemical formulations, plaintiffs characterize these outputs as predictable manifestations of defective training data or inadequate guardrails.

In response, defense counsel argue that large language models do not simply execute deterministic retrieval, but rather exhibit emergent cognitive properties that cannot be fully anticipated. By characterizing hallucinations as an inevitable byproduct of complex reasoning rather than an engineering defect, laboratories attempt to remove their products from design-defect scrutiny entirely.

Furthermore, developers leverage this concept of autonomous emergence to construct defenses based on intervening and superseding causation. In common law tort, a defendant's liability is severed if an unforeseeable, independent force intervenes between the original act and the ultimate injury. In cases such as *Walters v. OpenAI* (where ChatGPT hallucinated that a radio host had embezzled funds from a Second Amendment advocacy group), OpenAI successfully insulated itself by arguing that the journalist's specific, iterative prompting technique constituted the operative causal act that elicited the false output.

By framing the model as an open-ended cognitive partner whose outputs are non-deterministic and heavily co-created by the prompter, developers shift the legal inquiry toward comparative fault and intervening user causation, disclaiming direct responsibility for the resulting injury.

### The Doctrinal Boomerang: Abnormally Dangerous Activities

The gambit of framing artificial intelligence as an unpredictable, semi-autonomous force introduces significant vulnerability under common law tort principles. If developers successfully argue that frontier systems possess emergent unpredictability that cannot be eliminated through reasonable care, they walk directly into the common law doctrine of strict liability for abnormally dangerous (ultrahazardous) activities, codified in Sections 519 and 520 of the Restatement (Second) of Torts.

Under Section 520 of the Restatement, courts assess six distinct factors to determine whether an activity is abnormally dangerous:
1. The existence of a high degree of risk of harm;
2. The likelihood that the harm will be great;
3. The inability to eliminate the risk through the exercise of reasonable care;
4. The extent to which the activity is not a matter of common usage;
5. The inappropriateness of the activity to the place where it is carried on; and
6. The extent to which the activity's value to the community is outweighed by its dangerous attributes.

The third factor—the inability to eliminate risk by exercising reasonable care—functions as the definitive boundary separating fault-based negligence from strict liability. If a risk can be managed through ordinary due care, negligence remains the governing standard; if an activity generates unavoidable, severe risks regardless of precautions taken, strict liability attaches to ensure that the enterprise internalizes its true social costs.

Frontier laboratories, in their public declarations and corporate governance documents, regularly assert that scaling neural parameters poses severe systemic and existential hazards. Through published "Preparedness Frameworks" and "Responsible Scaling Policies," developers warn that advanced foundation models could lower technical barriers to the synthesis of chemical, biological, radiological, or nuclear (CBRN) weapons, facilitate catastrophic autonomous cyber-attacks, or escape human containment.

By arguing that hallucinations and catastrophic failure modes are emergent properties that cannot be eliminated through contemporary alignment techniques, developers establish the factual predicates of Section 520. If a catastrophic harm occurs, an injured plaintiff does not need to identify a specific programming defect or prove negligence; the public statements and voluntary safety disclosures of the developers function as evidence that the technology poses an ultrahazardous risk that cannot be neutralized by due care, triggering strict liability for all proximate harms.

### The Price-Anderson Solution

Frontier laboratories are aware of this liability dilemma. The strategic objective is not to operate under common law tort rules, but to force a crisis of insurability that necessitates a bespoke federal statutory regime modeled on the **Price-Anderson Nuclear Industries Indemnity Act of 1957 (42 U.S.C. § 2210)**.

The Price-Anderson Act was designed to solve a private insurance market failure that threatened to halt the commercial nuclear power industry. Because the catastrophic tail risks of a reactor meltdown could not be underwritten by private insurers, commercial entities refused to build reactors without statutory protections.

Congress addressed this by creating a two-tiered statutory compensation system: commercial operators were mandated to purchase the maximum available private liability coverage, and an industry-wide retrospective pooling fund was established to absorb secondary losses. Above this pooled ceiling, private civil liability was preempted and capped by federal statute, funneling all state tort litigation into federal district courts and shifting uncompensated public damages to discretionary congressional relief.

A parallel market failure is emerging in frontier artificial intelligence. Generative AI systems fail traditional actuarial standards regarding maximum possible loss, historical claim frequency, and information asymmetry, prompting commercial underwriters to add broad AI exclusions to commercial general liability and cyber insurance policies. Incumbent laboratories, facing balance-sheet exposure to correlated downstream failures across thousands of enterprise software integrations, cannot rely on private reinsurance markets to absorb their long-tail exposure.

By elevating the rhetoric of catastrophic existential risk, laboratories position their systems alongside commercial nuclear power: technologies deemed essential to national power, but accompanied by catastrophic risks that private insurance cannot absorb. The industry’s ultimate legislative goal is a federal compact exchanging compliance with government safety benchmarks for statutory liability caps, mandatory insurance pools, and total preemption of state-level tort actions.

## Copyright Moats: Cognitive Anthropomorphism and Weight Enclosure

### The Anthropomorphic Fair Use Defense

In intellectual property litigation, the narrative of emergent machine minds serves as an effective defense against mass copyright infringement claims. In consolidated class actions and high-stakes corporate disputes—such as *The New York Times v. OpenAI* and parallel litigations brought by visual artists and authors—frontier developers rely on cognitive anthropomorphism to support their fair use defenses under 17 U.S.C. § 107.

Under 17 U.S.C. § 107(1), courts evaluate the purpose and character of the use, focusing primarily on whether the secondary use is transformative. In legal briefs, executive declarations, and policy submissions, developers describe neural network training through human cognitive metaphors: the systems do not merely copy data, but rather "read," "learn," "comprehend," and "synthesize" information. Counsel argue that training an artificial neural network on copyrighted books, articles, and code is functionally identical to a human student reading texts in a library, abstracting underlying concepts, and applying those insights to create original expressions.

By equating model training with human learning, developers obscure the underlying computational realities: the unauthorized mass scraping, vectorization, and statistical compression of expressive works to populate commercial generative tools. Under this anthropomorphic framework, scraping copyrighted expression is framed as non-infringing cognitive acquisition, and the resulting statistical associations are framed as transformative fair use. When plaintiffs present evidence of verbatim regurgitation of copyrighted text, developers classify these outputs as aberrant bugs—analogous to occasional human rote memorization—rather than structural consequences of objective loss minimization.

### Existential Threat Rhetoric and the Enclosure of Open Weights

While cognitive anthropomorphism provides legal cover for data ingestion during the pre-training phase, developers reverse their rhetoric once training is complete and downstream distribution begins. Having secured uncompensated access to creative works by characterizing their systems as benign students, incumbents pivot to existential threat narratives to restrict access to model weights.

Incumbent laboratories argue that fully trained foundation weights represent dual-use technologies capable of catastrophic misuse if released openly. They claim that open-weight architectures allow bad actors to remove fine-tuned safety filters, unlocking dangerous instructions for cyber-attacks, synthetic bio-weapons, and large-scale disinformation. This threat model is then translated into proposed regulatory frameworks, including the compute-threshold reporting mandates under the EU AI Act and California’s proposed SB 1047, which impose strict compliance standards, safety audits, and potential civil liability on models trained using compute in excess of $10^{26}$ floating-point operations.

This dynamic creates an asymmetric competitive moat:
- In the upstream training phase, incumbents rely on anthropomorphic fair use defenses to extract creative and factual content from the public commons without licensing costs.
- In the downstream deployment phase, incumbents assert existential risk doctrines to impose severe regulatory hurdles, licensing requirements, and cybersecurity mandates that effectively restrict open-weight alternatives.
- As a result, open-source initiatives and smaller academic developers lack the capital and legal resources needed to satisfy these compliance standards, leaving the proprietary cloud infrastructures of established incumbents as the sole authorized providers of frontier foundation models.

## Regulatory Audits: Misdirection and the Dual-Use Security Cloak

### Audit Substitution: Speculative Doom vs. Mundane Compliance

The narrative of emergent superintelligence provides frontier laboratories with a mechanism for regulatory audit substitution. By elevating low-probability, high-consequence scenarios—such as autonomous model escape, non-human strategic deception, and artificial proliferation of biological weapons—incumbents steer federal and global regulators toward assessing abstract alignment methodologies and voluntary red-teaming frameworks.

Frontier laboratories design internal risk management structures, such as Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP) with its tiered AI Safety Levels (ASL-1 through ASL-4) and OpenAI’s Preparedness Framework. These frameworks define the evaluation benchmarks, threat vectors, and safety thresholds required to progress to higher training runs. Regulators are invited to collaborate on inspecting model behavior within these synthetic, industry-designed testing environments.

This hyper-focus on hypothetical catastrophic futures displaces rigorous, enforceable government audits of clear legal violations occurring in the present:
- Algorithmic disparate impact and employment discrimination under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act.
- Consumer credit scoring violations under the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA).
- Data collection practices violating the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and state privacy regimes such as the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA).
- Labor exploitation and trauma endured by shadow workforces in the Global South tasked with data annotation and content moderation.

When regulatory oversight is dominated by the technical challenges of speculative superintelligent alignment, administrative agencies fail to deploy established audit powers against commercial fraud, deceptive design, and anti-competitive lock-in.

### The "Security" Cloak and Transparency Preemption

Beyond diverting the substantive focus of administrative audits, the superintelligence myth serves as a shield against external scrutiny. By defining frontier models as potent, dual-use national security artifacts, laboratories shield their operations from independent academic research, judicial discovery, and open auditing.

When independent academic researchers, consumer advocacy groups, or investigative journalists attempt to audit foundation models for security vulnerabilities, hallucinations, or training bias via systematic scraping or behavioral testing, laboratories restrict API access, citing security risks and anti-jailbreak safety commitments.

Requests for algorithmic transparency, system architecture disclosures, training-data manifests, and model weight access are rejected on the twin grounds of protecting proprietary trade secrets and preventing catastrophic geopolitical proliferation. The dual-use framing allows commercial enterprises to maintain closed-box obscurity, avoiding public scrutiny while casting opacity as responsible civilizational stewardship.

## Antitrust Neutralization: The "National Champion" Paradigm and Compute Monopolies

### The "Manhattan Project" Defense

The narrative of emergent superintelligence provides an effective rhetorical and strategic counter to antitrust enforcement. In traditional competition law under the Sherman and Clayton Acts, multibillion-dollar investments, exclusive infrastructure agreements, and horizontal capital tie-ups attract intensive scrutiny for anti-competitive entrenchment and market foreclosing effects.

However, by framing the development of frontier artificial intelligence as an existential, winner-take-all technological race against geopolitical rivals—chiefly the People's Republic of China—the corporate development of AGI is elevated from commercial commerce to a matter of state survival.

Under this "Manhattan Project" framework, attempts by antitrust authorities to break up Big Tech firms, unwind joint ventures, or block infrastructure investments are portrayed as unilateral economic disarmament. Incumbents assert that any structural remedy that fragments domestic capital, restricts compute acquisition, or introduces regulatory friction will impair national competitive speed, conceding technological superiority to foreign adversaries.

This geopolitical rationale shifts the focus of antitrust enforcers from consumer welfare and open market competition toward preserving the consolidated scale of American "national champions."

### The Natural Monopoly of Containment and Cloud Tie-Ups

To reinforce this geopolitical posture, laboratories argue that frontier AI represents a natural monopoly necessitated by engineering and safety parameters. Under this theory, the development and containment of superintelligence requires unprecedented financial capital, massive data center capacity, and gigawatt-scale energy resources. Incumbents argue that only centralized, trillion-dollar infrastructure operators possess the capital resilience, sophisticated cybersecurity infrastructure, and physical containment capabilities needed to train frontier models safely without risking weight theft or accidental release.

This logic flips conventional antitrust theory: extreme capital concentration is reframed as an ethical mandate for containment. This framework shields the massive joint ventures between frontier AI startups and cloud computing service providers (CSPs)—most notably Microsoft’s partnership with OpenAI and Amazon and Alphabet’s investments into Anthropic.

Historically, acquisitions of this magnitude would trigger mandatory premerger notification and substantive review under the Hart-Scott-Rodino (HSR) Antitrust Improvements Act. Instead, parties structured these deals as non-controlling equity stakes, multi-year cloud compute credit arrangements, and intellectual property access covenants, deliberately testing the limits of traditional HSR reporting thresholds.

In response, the Federal Trade Commission executed a compulsory inquiry under Section 6(b) of the FTC Act into large AI partnerships and investments. The FTC's investigation revealed that these arrangements create systemic vertical and horizontal dependencies:
- CSPs secure guaranteed, captive consumption of proprietary cloud infrastructure via multi-billion-dollar compute commitments.
- Early-stage laboratories secure specialized compute capacity unavailable on open commercial markets.
- Big Tech hyperscalers acquire operational control over cutting-edge model research, early IP access, and exclusive commercialization rights without executing formal mergers that trigger antitrust enforcement under Section 7 of the Clayton Act.

The superintelligence narrative legitimizes these infrastructure alliances, framing them not as collusive entry barriers, but as the only industrial structures capable of financing the computational demands of safe AGI.

## The Systemic Strategic Underpinnings: Temporal Displacement and Priestly Capture

### Temporal Displacement of Settled Law

The broader strategic benefit of the superintelligence myth is its capacity to induce temporal displacement within the legal system. By characterizing artificial intelligence as an entirely unprecedented, ontological breakthrough that transcends existing categories of law, corporate actors convince legislators that contemporary statutes are conceptually obsolete. The narrative suggests that legacy laws governing common law negligence, fraud, product defects, civil rights, and collective bargaining were designed for industrial tools and cannot govern autonomous, non-deterministic reasoning agents.

Consequently, legislative bodies defer immediate regulatory and statutory enforcement, embarking on protracted efforts to draft novel, comprehensive "AI Acts." While legislatures debate existential definitions, threshold metrics, and post-human governance over multiple congressional cycles, the enforcement of existing legal machinery is held in stasis.

Against this temporal displacement, former Federal Trade Commission leadership and federal civil rights enforcement agencies established a contrary enforcement posture. In April 2023, the FTC, the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC), the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), and the Department of Justice’s Civil Rights Division issued a Joint Statement on Enforcement Efforts Against Discrimination and Bias in Automated Systems.

In that statement and accompanying regulatory actions, agency heads asserted that there is "no AI exemption to the laws on the books." They emphasized that existing statutory grants—including Section 5 of the FTC Act prohibiting unfair and deceptive practices, Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, and the Fair Credit Reporting Act—grant comprehensive legal authority to penalize algorithmic discrimination, commercial fraud, and unfair methods of competition today, without requiring new congressional enactments.

The emergent superintelligence myth serves precisely to counter this interagency posture: by arguing that the real risks are future and existential, laboratories attempt to stall enforcement until market consolidation is permanent and irreversible.

### Priestly Regulatory Capture and the "Terrified Sorcerer" Posture

The political implementation of the superintelligence myth relies on what can be described as priestly regulatory capture. Rather than engaging in traditional corporate lobbying—which reflexively opposes government regulation to maximize short-term profits—frontier AI leadership adopts the posture of the "terrified sorcerer." In public hearings, media appearances, and academic declarations, executives warn that the technological spirits they are summoning could surpass human control and extinguish civilization if deployed carelessly.

This posture achieves several strategic objectives:
- **Epistemic Monopolization**: By claiming that they are building systems of unprecedented danger, executives position themselves as the sole priesthood technically capable of understanding, interpreting, and controlling those systems. Regulators and politicians, lacking technical literacy in deep learning architectures, naturally defer to this self-selected clerical cadre for guidance on legislative drafting.
- **Architecture of Entry Barriers**: In Senate Judiciary hearings, congressional roundtables, and international safety summits, industry leaders advocate for mandatory government licensing regimes, independent safety oversight boards, and stringent capability thresholds. When corporate-backed legislative proposals emerge—such as the Blumenthal-Hawley Bipartisan Framework for a U.S. AI Act or California’s SB 1047—they mandate specific compliance obligations (third-party red-teaming, full shutdown capabilities, advanced cybersecurity controls, and extensive reporting) tied to computational thresholds such as $10^{26}$ cumulative floating-point operations.
- **Institutionalizing Incumbent Advantages**: While multimillion-dollar capitalization enables frontier laboratories to navigate bespoke licensing bureaucracy, hire regulatory compliance suites, and conduct expensive safety auditing, these compliance costs are prohibitive for open-source initiatives, academic developers, and early-stage startups. The terrified sorcerer willingly invites regulation, provided the state entrusts the sorcerer with drafting the protective runes—ensuring that prospective competitors are barred from building competitive infrastructure.

## Conclusion: De-Mythologizing Frontier AI for Legal Accountability

The framing of artificial intelligence as an emergent superintelligence is an effective regulatory strategy. By presenting software as non-deterministic agency, it seeks to evade traditional products liability while pursuing a federal Price-Anderson indemnification architecture. In intellectual property litigation, it advances anthropomorphic metaphors of human learning to defend unauthorized data scraping under fair use, while simultaneously invoking existential security risks to restrict open weights and enclose computational moats.

In administrative oversight, it substitutes industry-defined catastrophic alignment benchmarks for enforceable civil rights, labor, and privacy compliance. In competition policy, it reframes capital concentration and anticompetitive infrastructure joint ventures as an essential geopolitical and technical firewall against catastrophic proliferation.

The ultimate vulnerability of this corporate posture lies in its fundamental doctrinal contradictions:
1. **The Anthropomorphism vs. WMD Duality**: The cognitive anthropomorphism relied upon to establish fair use directly conflicts with the dual-use hazardous profile asserted to justify closing down open weights and securing national security immunities.
2. **The Non-Determinism vs. Ultrahazardous Trap**: The claim of non-deterministic, unpreventable hallucination intended to defeat ordinary negligence directly satisfies the common law criteria for strict liability for abnormally dangerous activities.
3. **The Legislative Stasis vs. Statutory Reality Paradox**: The assertion that emergent machine minds require entirely new legislative regimes is increasingly challenged by federal and state regulators applying existing statutory authorities to prosecute unfair trade practices, algorithmic bias, and market consolidation today.

Resolving these regulatory tensions requires stripping away the speculative myth of emergent superintelligence. Regulators and courts must evaluate foundation models not through the mystifying lens of artificial consciousness, but as industrial software products: computational infrastructure assembled through industrial capital, trained on human-authored data, and operated by corporate entities fully subject to established legal accountability.
