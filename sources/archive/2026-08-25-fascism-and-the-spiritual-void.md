# Fascism and the Spiritual Void

> Could one argue that fascism is a uniquely modern phenomenon arising from a loss of transcendent worldviews?

Yes, this is a formidable and widely respected philosophical argument, one that moves beyond economic or political explanations to address the "spiritual pathology" of the 20th century.

Many thinkers argue that fascism is not a relapse into barbarism, but rather a toxic byproduct of modernity itself—specifically the vacuum created when traditional religious frameworks (transcendence) collapsed and were replaced by secular ideologies that claimed absolute truth (immanence). #spirituality #fascism 

Here is how this argument is structured across philosophy, theology, and depth psychology.

### 1. The Thesis of "Political Religions" (Eric Voegelin)

The political philosopher Eric Voegelin is perhaps the most significant proponent of this view. In his 1938 book *The Political Religions*, he argued that humans have an innate need for spiritual order and meaning.

- **Immanentizing the Eschaton:** Voegelin famously argued that when modernity severed the connection to the *transcendent* (God, the divine, a higher moral law beyond human control), that religious impulse didn't disappear—it was redirected toward the *immanent* (the material world).
- **The State as Church:** Fascism (and Communism) stepped into this void, functioning as a "secular religion." It offered all the trappings of faith: a messiah (the Leader), a holy book (ideology), rituals (rallies), a chosen people (the master race), and a path to salvation (the Thousand Year Reich).
- **The Consequence:** Because there was no longer a "God" above the State to limit its power, the State became the absolute arbiter of reality, morality, and life itself.

### 2. The Archetypal Possession (Carl Jung)

From the perspective of depth psychology, Carl Jung argued that the "loss of transcendence" created a dangerous psychological vulnerability.

- **Wotan:** In his 1936 essay *Wotan*, Jung suggested that the Germanic people had not simply "gone crazy," but were possessed by an ancient archetype. He argued that when the conscious mind (rationality/modernity) rejects the religious container, the unconscious forces (the "shadow") are not tamed; they simply burst out in primitive, destructive forms.
- **Mass Psychosis:** Jung viewed fascism as a "psychic epidemic." Without a transcendent worldview to integrate these dark impulses, the collective unconscious flooded the rational mind, leading to a state of mass possession where the "Führer" acted as a magnet for the collective projection of the "Savior."

### 3. The Collapse of Authority and Tradition (Hannah Arendt)

Hannah Arendt, in *The Origins of Totalitarianism*, connected the rise of fascism to the specific atomization of modern man.

- **Loneliness:** She argued that modernity destroyed the traditional bonds of community, religion, and family, leaving individuals isolated and "lonely" (in an existential sense).
- **The Hunger for Logic:** This atomized mass was desperate for a consistent narrative to explain their suffering. Fascism provided a "key" (racism/struggle) that explained *everything*. The loss of a transcendent, complex reality made people susceptible to the "terrible consistency" of totalitarian logic, which promised to build a perfect world if only they followed the rules of the ideology.

### 4. The "Technique" of Modernity (Jacques Ellul / Heidegger)

Another layer of the argument is that fascism is the ultimate expression of modern *technique* (efficiency/technology) unmoored from moral constraints.

- **Instrumental Rationality:** The Frankfurt School (Adorno, Horkheimer) and Martin Heidegger argued that the Enlightenment focused on "how" to do things (reason/science) but lost the "why" (transcendence/teleology).
- **Industrialized Murder:** Without a transcendent belief in the sanctity of the individual soul (imago Dei), humans became mere data points or raw material. The Holocaust, in this view, was not a break from modern civilization but a manifestation of it: a factory system applied to the "problem" of human beings, optimized for efficiency.

### Summary of the Argument

If a transcendent worldview places the ultimate good *outside* of human history (in heaven or the divine), then human power is limited. No leader can claim to be God.

When that view is lost, the "ultimate good" must be achieved *inside* human history (the perfect society, the pure race). This justifies any means necessary to achieve it. The "heaven" must be built here, and anyone standing in the way of this utopia is not just a political opponent, but an impediment to salvation who must be removed.

In this light, fascism is uniquely modern because it requires both the **spiritual void** (to be filled by the State) and the **technological means** (to implement total control) that only the 20th century could provide.
