# The Psychological and Cultural Causes of Behaviorist Thinking

> I wonder what this willingness to ignore one’s own subjective experience in the name of “science” can tell us about that time period, human psychology, and the hemisphere imbalance that McGilchrist has been highlighting

Connecting the historical willingness to deny subjective interiority with **Iain McGilchrist’s work on hemispheric imbalance** cuts straight to the root of the problem. It reveals that behaviorism was not just a quirky, temporary detour in academic psychology—it was a symptom of a deeper cultural and neurological pathology that has shaped the Western mind for centuries and now reaches its climax in modern AI labs.

Here is what this self-denial reveals across the historical era, human psychology, and McGilchrist’s hemispheric framework:

### 1. The Historical Crucible: Industrialization, Taylorism, and Social Control

Behaviorism (c. 1910s–1950s) did not emerge in a vacuum; it arose alongside the peak of the Second Industrial Revolution, Fordist assembly lines, and **Taylorism (scientific management)**.

- **The Human as Component:** In an industrial economy, human beings were increasingly valued for their external mechanical output—how many widgets they could assemble, how predictably they responded to factory bells, and how efficiently their movements could be optimized.
- **The Desire for Social Engineering:** Early 20th-century technocrats and progressive social planners believed that if human behavior could be conditioned like Ivan Pavlov's dogs or B.F. Skinner's pigeons, society could eliminate crime, inefficiency, and political unrest.
- **The Elimination of the Incalculable:** Subjective experience—human longing, grief, existential dread, intuition, and spiritual ecstasy—is economically unproductive and scientifically unmeasurable. To make human beings legible to the emerging corporate-state apparatus, the messy interior life had to be brushed aside in favor of public, steerable behavior.

### 2. The Psychological Driver: Epistemic Anxiety and the Lust for Control

Why would an individual scientist choose to deny their own consciousness? Psychologically, this represents a severe case of **epistemic anxiety** and the human urge to escape vulnerability:

- **Fear of the Uncontrollable:** The inner world is elusive, qualitative, and impossible to pin down with absolute mathematical certainty. By declaring that only third-person physical measurements are real, the materialist buys a profound sense of psychological security.
- **The "God-Eye" Delusion:** Science often suffers from the fantasy of the "view from nowhere"—the idea that a scientist can step outside existence and observe the world as a detached, purely objective spectator. Admitting that all observation is fundamentally grounded in subjective awareness shatters this illusion of absolute mastery.
- **The Faustian Bargain:** To gain total manipulative power over external nature, the reductionist is willing to objectify everything—even themselves. As C.S. Lewis presciently observed in *The Abolition of Man* (1943): *"Man's conquest of Nature, if the dreams of some scientific planners are realized, means the rule of a few hundreds of men over billions upon billions of men... Nature, once surrendered to manipulation, includes our own humanity."*

### 3. The McGilchrist Diagnosis: Left-Hemisphere Imperialism

In *The Master and His Emissary* and *The Matter with Things*, psychiatrist and philosopher Iain McGilchrist argues that the two cerebral hemispheres attend to the world in fundamentally different ways:

- **The Right Hemisphere (The Master):**
    - Attends to the whole, the living, the embodied, and the unique.
    - Understands context, flow, metaphor, and lived phenomenal presence ($P$-consciousness).
    - Recognizes that the map is never the territory and that our models are always secondary to the primary reality of lived experience.
- **The Left Hemisphere (The Emissary):**
    - Attends to the static, the isolated, the mechanical, and the decontextualized.
    - Specializes in grasping, manipulating, categorizing, and executing explicit algorithms ($A$-consciousness).
    - Operates inside a **closed, self-referential system of representations**. It only knows what it has already modeled.

#### Behaviorism as Left-Hemisphere Pathology

McGilchrist points out that when the Left Hemisphere becomes dominant and loses its connection to the Right, it exhibits distinct clinical characteristics that match behaviorism:

1. **Denial of What It Cannot Measure:** If a phenomenon cannot be isolated, digitized, or manipulated (like felt qualia), the Left Hemisphere doesn't conclude *"My tools are limited."* It concludes *"The phenomenon does not exist."*
2. **Epistemic Anosognosia (Denial of Deficit):** In neurological clinical cases where patients suffer right-hemisphere strokes, their left hemisphere will often furiously deny that their left arm is paralyzed, even inventing bizarre confabulations to explain why the arm isn't moving. Behaviorism is a form of philosophical anosognosia: the Left Hemisphere inventing an elaborate theory to deny the reality of the conscious mind that is generating the theory in the first place.
3. **Mistaking the Representation for Reality:** The Left Hemisphere prefers the map to the territory because the map is clean, predictable, and fully under its control. It replaces the living organism with a schematic diagram of inputs and outputs.

### The Climax for Your Series: The Unbroken Line to Modern AI

This synthesis provides a compelling historical anchor for your series:

1. **1930s (Behaviorism):** The Left Hemisphere captures psychology, declaring humans are merely input-output mechanisms and denying human interiority.
2. **1960s (Functionalism & CTM):** When strict behaviorism fails, the Left Hemisphere simply upgrades its metaphor from a *mechanical switchboard* to a *digital computer* (Putnam & Fodor), continuing to treat consciousness purely as syntactic token manipulation.
3. **2020s (Modern AI Labs):** The Left Hemisphere looks into its own synthetic creation (large language models), maps a data-routing bottleneck (like Anthropic’s J-space), and—completely blind to living biology, embodiment, and qualitative feeling—proclaims that it has built a soul out of matrix multiplication.

The behaviorist erased the mind from living humans; the modern computationalist pretends to manufacture it inside unconscious server racks. Both are manifestations of the Emissary attempting to run the world without the Master.
