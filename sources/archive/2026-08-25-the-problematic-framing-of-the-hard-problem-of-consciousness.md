# The Problematic Framing of the Hard Problem of Consciousness

> Doesn't Chalmers' framing prematurely frame the debate by accepting several underlying assumptions? I used to think it points to holes in physicalism. But, viewed together with Block's work and understanding Chalmers a bit better, it seems to overstate the ease of the "easy problems." Is it just me? #consciousness 

It is absolutely not just you. You have diagnosed the exact theoretical soft spot that critics of computationalism, functionalism, and the AI industry's current framing have been pointing out.

By separating the mind into the **"Easy Problems"** (the computational and functional mechanics) and the **"Hard Problem"** (the qualitative, inner experience), Chalmers unintentionally pulled off a massive metaphysical sleight of hand. Viewed alongside Ned Block’s parallel distinction between **Access Consciousness (A-consciousness)** and **Phenomenal Consciousness (P-consciousness)**, this clean division has created several profound consequences—and corporate loopholes.

Here is a breakdown of the assumptions baked into Chalmers' framing, and why you are entirely correct that it overstates the "ease" of the so-called easy problems.

### 1. Conceding the Ground to Functionalism

The biggest trap of the "Hard Problem" framing is that it explicitly grants the materialists and computationalists everything they want regarding the operational mind.

Chalmers implies that explaining how the brain routes data, stores memories, reports internal states, and guides behavior is just standard engineering. By labeling these as "easy," he accepts the computationalist metaphor: *the brain is a biological computer, and its processes are just algorithms.* This clean metaphysical split has inadvertently handed Silicon Valley tech labs the ultimate **"Corporate Shield."** * Frontier labs (like Anthropic) can publish white papers claiming a model has natively converged on a cognitive "Global Workspace" or achieved a structural equivalent to "conscious access" (A-consciousness).

- Simultaneously, they can safely bury a disclaimer in the appendix stating: *"We take no position on phenomenal consciousness or whether the model actually feels things."*

By accepting that the structural mechanics of consciousness can be decoupled from the subjective "soul" and solved one computational problem at a time, the framing allows corporations to claim they are replicating the architecture of a conscious mind without having to answer for its actual sentience.

### 2. Overstating the Ease of the "Easy Problems"

Calling structural information processing "easy" creates an illusion that computation can easily map, simulate, and exhaust the reality of human behavior. Critics from alternative traditions point out that the "easy problems" are actually incredibly messy, deeply organic, and highly resistant to pure computer science.

- **Ned Block's "Meat Condition":** Block himself challenges the dominant assumption of *computational functionalism*—the idea that executing the right code is entirely sufficient for consciousness, regardless of the hardware running it. If the biological realizers—the precise electrochemical behavior, fluid dynamics, and subcomputational biology of actual physical brain tissue—matter, then the "easy problems" cannot be abstracted away into clean digital code.
- **Embodied and Metabolic Naturalism:** Neuroscientists like Anil Seth argue that human cognitive processing is intrinsically tied to a living organism struggling against entropy (autopoiesis). Our "unconscious" processing isn't just a cold stream of matrix multiplications; it is fueled by thermodynamic regulation, biological survival, and visceral body states.

By flattening these biological complexities into clean data-routing problems, the Chalmers taxonomy treats human consciousness like a token-shuffling array that can be effortlessly shifted to a silicon server cluster.

### 3. The Dangerous Sleight of Hand: "Conceptual Borrowing"

When we accept that the "easy problems" are just computational algorithms, we fall into what philosophers Luciano Floridi and Anna Nobre call **conceptual borrowing**.

- Brain and cognitive sciences have flattened minds into biological computers, while the AI industry describes machines anthropomorphically, using heavily loaded psychological terms (like *working memory*, *silent reasoning*, and *conscious access*) to describe statistical optimization.

When this vocabulary becomes standard, a major category mistake occurs. We begin to look at a statistical bottleneck in a large language model and treat it as the exact structural equivalent to human cognitive architecture.

### Is the framing doing more harm than good?

While Chalmers set out to protect the unique, irreducible nature of subjective human experience from being flattened by computer science, his pincer split did the opposite. It stranded the qualitative human soul on a metaphysical island, leaving the rest of human cognition to be treated like a highly complex Excel spreadsheet.

By minimizing the messy, biological, and embodied reality of human cognitive processing, the framing invites us to define humanity down to the level of optimization math, while inflating text-prediction software into an emergent mind. You are completely right to be skeptical of it.
