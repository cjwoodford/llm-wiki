# The Decolonial Turn: A Global Cartography of Power, Knowledge, and Being (Deep Research)

## Executive Summary: The Geopolitics of Thinking

The contemporary intellectual landscape is witnessing a seismic shift, a movement that transcends the boundaries of traditional academic disciplines and the political borders of nation-states. This movement, broadly termed **decoloniality**, represents a fundamental interrogation of the structures of the modern world. It is not merely a critique of the historical event of colonialism, but an analytic of the enduring power matrix—defined as **Global Coloniality**—that continues to order the planet long after the formal flags of empire have been lowered. #decoloniality 

For the newcomer to this field, the terminology can be dense, and the geography vast. Decoloniality is not a monolithic theory; it is a "family of diverse positions" that shares a view of coloniality as the fundamental problem of modernity.1 It emerges not from the centers of power in Paris, London, or New York, but from the "underside of modernity"—the epistemological territories of Latin America, Africa, South Asia, the Middle East, and Indigenous North America.

This report provides an exhaustive, expert-level overview of this terrain. It distinguishes decoloniality from its cousins, postcolonialism and decolonization, and maps the intellectual genealogy of the movement. From the "Modernity/Coloniality" group in the Andes to the "Radical Resurgence" of the Nishnaabeg in North America, from the "Necropolitics" of the African postcolony to the "Settler Colonial" analysis of Palestine, this document synthesizes the work of key thinkers who are reshaping how we understand power, knowledge, and existence itself.

The analysis proceeds by first establishing the theoretical definitions and distinctions that ground the field. It then traverses the globe, examining specific regional contributions and the universal implications of their localized struggles. It concludes with a synthesis of the "Epistemologies of the South," offering a vision of a "Pluriverse"—a world in which many worlds fit—as the ultimate horizon of the decolonial project.

## 1. Defining the Territory: The Conceptual Triad

To grasp the magnitude of the decolonial project, one must first navigate the conceptual triad that often causes confusion: **Decolonization**, **Postcolonialism**, and **Decoloniality**. While these terms interact and overlap, they refer to distinct historical moments, political goals, and theoretical commitments.

### 1.1 Decolonization: The Political Event

Historically, **decolonization** refers to the political and bureaucratic process by which colonies achieved independence from imperial powers. This wave, which crested in the mid-20th century (e.g., India in 1947, Ghana in 1957, Algeria in 1962), was primarily concerned with the transfer of sovereignty, the seizure of state power, and the admission of new nations into the international system (the United Nations).2

The primary goal of historical decolonization was the "nation-state." Leaders like Kwame Nkrumah and Jawaharlal Nehru sought to take over the colonial apparatus and use it for the benefit of the native population. However, as the 20th century progressed, it became evident that the *political* transfer of power did not necessarily result in *economic* or *epistemic* liberation. The state structures remained European in form, the economies remained extractive, and the languages of administration remained those of the colonizer. This failure of the "independent" state to deliver true freedom is the starting point for decolonial theory.3

### 1.2 Postcolonialism: The Cultural Aftermath

**Postcolonialism**, as an academic field, emerged largely in the late 1970s and 1980s, primarily within departments of literature and cultural studies in the Anglo-American academy. Triggered by Edward Said’s *Orientalism* (1978), postcolonial studies focuses on the analysis of colonial *discourse*—how the West represented the East—and the cultural consequences of the colonial encounter.4

Key thinkers like Homi Bhabha and Gayatri Spivak analyzed the "aftermath" of colonialism, focusing on concepts like **hybridity**, **mimicry**, and the **ambivalence** of identity. Postcolonial theory often relies heavily on French post-structuralist philosophy (Foucault, Derrida, Lacan) to deconstruct colonial texts.

Critics within the decolonial school argue that the prefix "post" is misleading. It implies a temporal progression—that colonialism is "over" and we are now in an era "after." Furthermore, they argue that postcolonialism’s intense focus on culture and textuality can sometimes obscure the brutal material realities of ongoing economic exploitation and the structural continuity of imperial power.6

### 1.3 Decoloniality: The Structural Analytic

**Decoloniality** (or the decolonial option) emerges primarily from Latin American thinkers in the 1990s who argued that **colonialism did not end; it merely evolved.** They distinguish between *colonialism* (the specific historical period of direct administrative rule) and *coloniality* (the underlying logic of power, economy, and knowledge that survives the end of direct rule).6

Decoloniality is an analytic of **Global Coloniality**—the modern world-system that has been in place since 1492. It posits that the global economy (capitalism), the global political system (nation-states), and the global hierarchy of knowledge (Western science/universality) are all constituted by the colonial difference. Therefore, one cannot simply be "post-colonial" while living inside a world structure that is still "colonial."

The goal of decoloniality is not to seize the state (as in decolonization) or to analyze the text (as in postcolonialism), but to **delink** from the matrix of power entirely. It seeks to reconstruct the world from the perspectives of those who have been silenced—epistemically, ontologically, and politically.9

## 2. The Latin American School: The Modernity/Coloniality Research Program

The theoretical engine of contemporary decolonial thought is the **Modernity/Coloniality (M/C)** research program. This collective of sociologists, philosophers, and semioticians reshaped the understanding of the modern world by arguing that "Modernity" is not a European phenomenon that later expanded to the world, but a planetary phenomenon that was constituted by the colonization of the Americas in 1492.8

### 2.1 Aníbal Quijano and the Coloniality of Power

The cornerstone of this school is the concept of the **Coloniality of Power**, introduced by the Peruvian sociologist Aníbal Quijano. Quijano argued that the conquest of the Americas established a new pattern of global power based on the social classification of the world’s population around the idea of "race".10

Prior to 1492, differences in power existed, but they were not racialized in the biological sense. The invention of "race" was a mental construction used to naturalize the superiority of the European conquerors and the inferiority of the conquered (Indigenous and African peoples). This racial classification became the organizing principle of the new global economy—Capitalism.

Quijano’s profound insight was that **race and capitalism were born together.** The "International Division of Labor" was a racial division:

- **Whiteness** was associated with wage labor and management.
- **Indigeneity** was associated with serfdom (servitude).
- **Blackness** was associated with slavery (commodification).

This structure, the **Colonial Matrix of Power (CMP)**, did not vanish with independence. It mutated. The post-independence states of Latin America (and later Africa and Asia) retained the racial hierarchy where "whiteness" (or proximity to European culture) remained the standard of humanity and rationality, while Indigenous and Black populations remained marginalized, impoverished, and epistemic "non-beings".9

### 2.2 Walter Mignolo: Epistemic Disobedience and Delinking

Building on Quijano, the Argentine semiotician Walter Mignolo focused on the **Coloniality of Knowledge**. Mignolo argues that the CMP is maintained not just by guns and economics, but by a "colonization of the mind" (echoing Ngũgĩ).9

Mignolo identifies the "Hubris of the Zero Point"—the pretension of Western philosophy and science to speak from "nowhere" (objectivity) and therefore "everywhere" (universality). Western epistemology hides its local, provincial origins (Greece, Rome, France, Germany) and presents itself as the *only* valid way of knowing reality. All other forms of knowledge (Indigenous, African, Islamic) are relegated to the past—labeled as "folklore," "myth," or "superstition".6

To counter this, Mignolo proposes **Delinking** (*desprendimiento*). Delinking is not an attempt to "improve" the system or to be "included" in modernity. It is a shift in the geography of reason. It requires **Epistemic Disobedience**: the refusal to accept that Western categories (democracy, development, human rights) are the only descriptors of the good life.

- **Insight:** Mignolo argues that if we only critique Western modernity using Western tools (like Marxism or Post-structuralism), we remain trapped in the same house. We must think *from* the borders, utilizing the "Border Gnosis" of those who have been marginalized.14

### 2.3 María Lugones: The Coloniality of Gender

A critical expansion of the M/C framework came from the Argentine feminist philosopher María Lugones. Lugones engaged with Quijano’s work but critiqued his understanding of gender. Quijano assumed that "men" and "women" were biological categories that existed globally before colonization. Lugones argued that **gender itself is a colonial imposition**.16

In her seminal work on the **Coloniality of Gender**, Lugones utilized anthropological evidence (such as Oyèrónkẹ́ Oyěwùmí’s work on the Yoruba) to show that many Indigenous and African societies operated without a binary gender system, or at least without a hierarchical one. The colonizers introduced the "Modern/Colonial Gender System" to control the population.18

This system operated differentially:

- **The Light Side:** For white settlers, gender was defined by the bourgeois family (fragile domestic woman vs. public rational man).
- **The Dark Side:** For colonized peoples, gender was brutalized. Indigenous and Black women were not treated as "fragile women"; they were exploited as labor (field hands) and as sexual property, but denied the protections of "femininity."
- **The Erasure:** "Third genders" or non-binary identities (common in the Americas) were demonized as sodomy and eradicated through violence.

**Second-Order Insight:** Lugones’ theory implies that "Patriarchy" is not a universal phenomenon but a specific colonial export. Therefore, **Decolonial Feminism** cannot simply be about "equality" with men (which assumes the colonial binary is valid); it must be about dismantling the gender system itself and recovering the non-binary and communal social relations that colonization destroyed.20

### 2.4 Nelson Maldonado-Torres: The Coloniality of Being

Puerto Rican philosopher Nelson Maldonado-Torres drives the analysis to the level of ontology—the study of *being*. He asks: What does it mean to "be" in a colonial world?.21

Maldonado-Torres argues that the famous Cartesian statement *Cogito, ergo sum* ("I think, therefore I am") is built upon an unstated foundation: *Ego conquiro* ("I conquer, therefore I am"). The European subject's sense of self-certainty and existence was constructed through the negation of the Other.22

He introduces the **Coloniality of Being**, which refers to the "lived experience" of colonization. It produces a **Zone of Non-Being** (a Fanonian term) where colonized subjects are rendered invisible. In this zone, the normal ethics of human interaction do not apply. Violence against the colonized is not considered a crime in the same way; their death is naturalized.

- **Implication:** This explains the "disposability" of life in the Global South today. When a disaster hits Haiti or a drone strikes Yemen, the global reaction is muted because those lives exist within the "Coloniality of Being"—they are ontologically deficient in the eyes of the modern system.24

## 3. The African Condition: Necropolitics and the Decolonization of the Mind

While the Latin American school focuses on the 1492 constitutive moment, African decolonial thought grapples with the intense violence of the 19th-century "Scramble for Africa," the apartheid systems, and the subsequent failure of the post-independence nation-state.

### 3.1 Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o: The Language of the Mind

One of the most influential figures in African decolonial thought is the Kenyan writer Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o. His central thesis in *Decolonising the Mind* (1986) is that colonialism was not just a theft of land, but a theft of the **soul** through the imposition of European languages.25

Ngũgĩ argues that language is a carrier of culture. It is the collective memory bank of a people's history. When colonial schools forced African children to speak English, French, or Portuguese—and punished them for speaking Gikuyu, Igbo, or Yoruba—they severed the child's connection to their own reality. The child learned to associate "knowledge" and "civilization" with the foreign tongue, and "stupidity" and "backwardness" with their mother tongue.

**The "Cultural Bomb":** Ngũgĩ describes colonial education as a cultural bomb that annihilates a people's belief in their names, their languages, their environment, their heritage of struggle, their unity, their capacities, and ultimately themselves.25

**Praxis of Delinking:** Ngũgĩ took the radical step of ceasing to write his creative fiction in English, switching to Gikuyu. He argued that African literature written in European languages is effectively "Euro-African literature," serving to enrich the English language rather than African culture. True decolonization requires "re-membering" the fragmented African subject through the recovery of indigenous languages.27

### 3.2 Achille Mbembe: Necropolitics and the Postcolony

Cameroonian philosopher Achille Mbembe provides a darker, more psychoanalytic reading of the African condition. He critiques the "Potentate" of the post-independence African state, which often reproduced the violence of the colonial regime.28

Mbembe’s most famous contribution is the concept of **Necropolitics** (2003). Expanding on Michel Foucault’s "Biopolitics" (the state’s power to manage life), Mbembe argues that in the colony and the postcolony, sovereignty is defined by the power to dictate **who may live and who must die**.28

The colony is a permanent "state of exception" where the law is suspended. The colonial power creates "death worlds"—spaces where populations are subjected to conditions of life conferring the status of the living dead (e.g., slavery, apartheid, the occupation of Palestine).

- **Critique of Decolonization:** Mbembe is skeptical of the 20th-century model of decolonization. He argues that the transfer of state power often resulted in "zombified" regimes that turned against their own people. For Mbembe, the "Postcolony" is a chaotic mix of conviviality and violence, where the ruler and the ruled are locked in a grotesque embrace of mutual corruption.

### 3.3 Sabelo Ndlovu-Gatsheni: The Decolonial University

A key voice in the recent "Rhodes Must Fall" movement in South Africa, Sabelo Ndlovu-Gatsheni synthesizes the Latin American and African schools. He focuses on the **Coloniality of Power in Postcolonial Africa**.30

Ndlovu-Gatsheni argues that African universities are largely "Western universities located in Africa." They teach European history, European philosophy, and European sociology as universal truths, while African knowledge is relegated to "anthropology" (the study of the exotic other).

- **Epistemic Freedom:** He calls for a "Decolonial Turn" in the university that goes beyond merely adding a few African authors to the reading list (the "additive approach"). Instead, he demands **Epistemic Freedom**—the right of Africans to theorize the world from their own historical and cultural vantage point, rather than serving merely as "raw data" for Western theorists to analyze.3

**Insight:** Ndlovu-Gatsheni highlights the **Coloniality of Internationalism**. He argues that the current global order (UN, IMF, World Bank) is a continuation of the imperial order, where the Global North dictates the economic and political terms of existence to the Global South under the guise of "development" and "human rights".31

## 4. India and South Asia: The Friction of Postcoloniality

South Asia, particularly India, has been the primary engine of **Postcolonial Studies**. The relationship between this tradition and the **Decolonial** turn is marked by both deep affinity and significant theoretical friction.

### 4.1 Subaltern Studies: The Precursor

The **Subaltern Studies Group** (Ranajit Guha, Partha Chatterjee, Dipesh Chakrabarty) emerged in the 1980s to critique the elitism of Indian historiography. They argued that history was always written from the perspective of the colonial elite or the nationalist elite (like Nehru and Gandhi), ignoring the "Subaltern"—the peasant, the tribal, the worker.7

While Subaltern Studies shares the decolonial goal of centering the marginalized, decolonial thinkers (like Mignolo) often critique it for remaining too tethered to European theory. Subaltern Studies scholars heavily utilized Gramsci (Marxist) and Foucault (Post-structuralist).

- **The Decolonial Critique:** The argument is that while Subaltern Studies critiques Eurocentrism, it does so *using European tools*. Decoloniality seeks to delink from those tools entirely, asking: Can we theorize the subaltern using *Indian* epistemologies, rather than French ones?.7

### 4.2 Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak: The Warning

Spivak, a giant of postcolonial theory, provides a necessary check on the sometimes romantic tendencies of decoloniality. Her seminal essay, *"Can the Subaltern Speak?"*, is often misunderstood. She does not mean that the subaltern cannot physically speak, but that **there is no institutional space where her voice can be heard and understood** without being translated or co-opted by the intellectual.6

Spivak might critique the decolonial idea of "delinking" as naive. She argues that we cannot simply "unlearn" our privilege or step outside the colonial episteme purely by will. We are all contaminated by the history of imperialism.

- **Abusing the Enlightenment:** Instead of rejecting the West entirely, Spivak advocates for "abusing the Enlightenment"—using the tools of the master (reason, rights, democracy) to dismantle the master's house, because there is no "pure" pre-colonial space to return to. She calls for a "transnational literacy" that is constantly self-critical of its own position.33

### 4.3 Homi Bhabha: Hybridity vs. Delinking

Homi Bhabha’s work focuses on **Hybridity** and the **Third Space**. He argues that colonial power is never total; it is always anxious. The colonized subject "mimics" the colonizer but is "almost the same, but not quite." This slippage creates a space of hybridity that destabilizes colonial authority.35

**Friction with Decoloniality:** Decolonial thinkers often reject Hybridity as a solution. They argue that celebrating hybridity can obscure the violence that created it. For a decolonial thinker, the goal is not to live in the "in-between" of the Third Space, but to reconstruct the indigenous epistemologies that were silenced—to create a "zero space" or a new center, rather than a margin. Mignolo famously contrasted this by saying decoloniality is about "delinking," while postcoloniality is about "transformation within the system".6

### 4.4 Vandana Shiva: Ecofeminism as Decoloniality

Indian scholar and activist Vandana Shiva connects the decolonial project to the environment. She critiques the **"Monocultures of the Mind"**—the Western drive for standardization, industrialization, and homogeneity.37

Shiva argues that the "Green Revolution" in India was a form of recolonization by Western agribusiness (like Monsanto), which replaced diverse indigenous crops with monocultures dependent on chemicals. This destroyed the soil and the social fabric of farming communities.

- **Ecofeminism:** Shiva explicitly links the destruction of nature (the Earth) with the oppression of women. She argues that both are treated as "passive resources" by the patriarchal colonial capitalist system. Her defense of "Seed Sovereignty" and traditional knowledge is a practical application of **delinking** from the global capitalist economy.39

## 5. The Middle East: Orientalism, Palestine, and Settler Colonialism

The Middle East presents a unique challenge to decolonial theory. It was not colonized in the same way as the Americas (total demographic replacement) or Africa (extraction), but through a complex mix of mandates, protectorates, and one specific, ongoing instance of settler colonialism: Israel.

### 5.1 Edward Said and the Shadow of Orientalism

Edward Said’s *Orientalism* is the foundational text that allowed for the emergence of the entire field. Said demonstrated how the "Orient" was not a real place, but a European invention—a stage on which the West projected its fantasies and fears.5

Said’s crucial contribution was to show the **complicity of culture in empire.** Novels, operas, and travelogues were not innocent art; they were part of the technology of rule that made the domination of the East seem natural and necessary.

- **Decolonial Connection:** While Mignolo and others critique Said for relying too heavily on Foucault (a European thinker), they acknowledge that without *Orientalism*, the conversation on the "Geopolitics of Knowledge" would not exist. Said opened the door to questioning *who* produces knowledge about *whom*.35

### 5.2 Palestine: The Decolonial Litmus Test

In contemporary decolonial thought, Palestine is increasingly viewed not as a "conflict" between two national movements, but as an active, ongoing case of **Settler Colonialism**. This reframing aligns Palestine with the struggles of Indigenous peoples in North America and Australasia.42

- **Rashid Khalidi:** In *The Hundred Years' War on Palestine*, Khalidi frames the Zionist project as a colonial war against the indigenous population, supported by successive imperial powers (Britain, then the US). He argues that the Zionist leaders themselves explicitly described their project as "colonial" in the early 20th century.43
- **Ilan Pappé:** The Israeli historian argues that the events of 1948 were not a "War of Independence" but an **Ethnic Cleansing**—a deliberate, pre-planned operation (Plan Dalet) to depopulate the land of its native inhabitants to create a Jewish majority state. This is a structural necessity of settler colonialism: the elimination of the native.45
- **Raef Zreik:** A Palestinian legal theorist, Zreik analyzes the theology of the settler state. He argues that Israel faces an unresolvable contradiction: it claims to be a democracy ("State of all its citizens") but defines itself as a "Jewish State." This creates a structure of **Apartheid** where the indigenous Palestinian is included in the economy but excluded from the body politic.
    - **The Decolonial Solution:** Zreik and others argue that the "Two-State Solution" is a colonial partition fantasy. True decolonization involves the **Right of Return** and the dismantling of the Zionist structure of supremacy, moving toward a single democratic state or a binational reality where the settler and the native coexist as equals.47

### 5.3 Islamic Decoloniality

Thinkers like **Wael Hallaq** and **Hamid Dabashi** offer critiques of modernity from within the Islamic tradition.

- **Wael Hallaq:** In *The Impossible State*, Hallaq argues that the "Islamic State" is a contradiction in terms. The modern nation-state is a European imposition with specific technologies of control (biopolitics, surveillance, borders) that are incompatible with the moral cosmology of the Shari'a. Decolonizing the Muslim world requires imagining political forms *beyond the Westphalian nation-state*, perhaps looking back to pre-modern forms of governance that were more pluralistic.50
- **Hamid Dabashi:** In *The End of Postcolonialism*, Dabashi argues that the binary of "West and the Rest" is collapsing. He calls for a "Post-Orientalist" scholarship that reclaims the cosmopolitanism of non-European intellectuals, refusing to be defined solely by the reaction to Europe.51

## 6. Indigenous Resurgence on Turtle Island (North America)

In Canada and the US, Indigenous scholars have forcefully rejected the "Postcolonial" label. They argue: *We are not post-colonial; the settlers are still here.* Their work focuses on **Settler Colonialism** as a structure, not an event—a structure that demands the elimination of the native to secure access to land.

### 6.1 Glen Coulthard: The Politics of Refusal

In *Red Skin, White Masks* (a homage to Fanon), Dene scholar Glen Coulthard offers a blistering critique of the **Politics of Recognition**. Since the 1990s, settler states (like Canada) have tried to "reconcile" with Indigenous peoples through apologies, land acknowledgments, and limited rights.53

Coulthard, utilizing Fanon and Marx, argues that "recognition" is a trap. When the master recognizes the slave without freeing the slave, it merely reinforces the master's power to grant recognition. It pacifies resistance without changing the material relationship of land theft.

- **The Politics of Refusal:** Coulthard advocates for **Refusal**: refusing to engage with the state's recognition games. Instead of looking at the colonizer for validation, Indigenous peoples should turn away and look at each other, rebuilding their own nations and ethical systems.54

### 6.2 Leanne Betasamosake Simpson: Radical Resurgence

Nishnaabeg scholar Leanne Simpson expands on this with the concept of **Radical Resurgence**. In *As We Have Always Done*, she argues that decolonization is not a political negotiation with the state but a "world-building" project.56

Resurgence means practicing **Grounded Normativity**—living according to Indigenous ethical frameworks, languages, and governance systems *right now*, regardless of the state's permission. It is about "making a lovely discovery" of one's own culture and brilliance.

- **Queer Indigeneity:** Simpson emphasizes the role of Two-Spirit and queer people in this resurgence. She argues that the imposition of the colonial gender binary was a key tool of dispossession, and therefore, "Queering" the resistance is a fundamental part of decolonizing the nation.58

### 6.3 Linda Tuhiwai Smith: Decolonizing Methodologies

Maori scholar Linda Tuhiwai Smith’s *Decolonizing Methodologies* (1999) is the essential handbook for decolonial research. She famously wrote that "Research is probably one of the dirtiest words in the indigenous world's vocabulary" because research was the tool used to classify, dehumanize, and steal from Indigenous peoples.59

Smith outlines the "imperial gaze" of Western research, which views Indigenous people as "problems" to be solved or "artifacts" to be preserved. She proposes an **Indigenous Research Agenda** that privileges:

- **Survival:** Protecting culture and language from extinction.
- **Recovery:** Reclaiming lands, histories, and dignity.
- **Self-Determination:** Research *by* Indigenous people, *for* Indigenous people, accountable to the community rather than the academy.60

## 7. Epistemologies of the South and the Pluriverse

The Portuguese sociologist Boaventura de Sousa Santos acts as a bridge between European theory and the Global South (specifically Latin America and Africa). His work systematizes the epistemological crisis of modernity.

### 7.1 The Abyssal Line

Santos introduces the concept of **Abyssal Thinking**. He argues that modern Western thinking creates an invisible "Abyssal Line" that divides the world into two realms:

1. **This Side of the Line (Metropolitan Societies):** The zone of regulation and emancipation. Here, law, human rights, democracy, and science apply. Conflict is managed through institutions.
2. **The Other Side of the Line (Colonial Societies):** The zone of appropriation and violence. Here, the law does not apply. Resources are stolen, and people are killed (drones, black sites, favela raids) with impunity.62

The tragedy of Western thought is that it cannot see the "Other Side." It assumes that its solutions (Human Rights, Democracy) are universal, failing to see that for the Global South, Western "Human Rights" have often been the vehicle for intervention and destruction.

### 7.2 Epistemicide and the Ecology of Knowledges

Santos coins the term **Epistemicide**: the murder of knowledge. Colonialism did not just kill people; it killed their ways of knowing (ancestral wisdom, spiritual connections, alternative medicines, legal systems).

- **Sociology of Absences:** The task of the decolonial scholar is to make visible what was produced as "non-existent" by the abyssal line (e.g., the "ignorant" peasant, the "primitive" native).
- **Ecology of Knowledges:** Santos proposes moving from a "monoculture of scientific knowledge" to an "ecology of knowledges." This does not mean rejecting science, but accepting that science is *one* valid way of knowing, which can coexist with other ways (Indigenous, aesthetic, practical) in a horizontal dialogue.63

## 8. Conclusion: The Horizon of the Pluriverse

The journey through the landscape of decoloniality reveals a coherent yet diverse project. Whether it is the **Coloniality of Power** in the Andes, **Necropolitics** in Africa, **Settler Colonialism** in Palestine, or **Radical Resurgence** in North America, the core argument remains: the modern world is built on a structure of violence that did not end with 20th-century independence.

Decoloniality is an invitation to **unlearn** the dominant narratives of modernity—that the West is the vanguard of history and the rest are lagging behind. Instead, it posits that the "underdeveloped" world is not "behind"; it is being actively "underdeveloped" by the colonial matrix.

The ultimate goal of decoloniality is not a new universalism (where "our" truth replaces "their" truth), but a **Pluriverse**. Borrowing from the Zapatistas of Mexico, the decolonial horizon is "a world in which many worlds fit." It is a world where different ontologies, different economies, and different ways of being human can coexist without one seeking to annihilate the other.

For the newcomer, this requires a shift in the geography of reason. It asks you to stop looking at the world from the deck of the ship (the discoverer/colonizer) and start looking at it from the shore (the discovered/colonized) or from the hold of the slave ship. It is in that shift of perspective that the possibility of a different future—a decolonial future—begins.

| **Coloniality of Gender**          | The theory that the gender binary (man/woman) was a colonial imposition used to categorize and dehumanize.             | María Lugones (Argentina)                |
| **Delinking**                      | The political and epistemic act of detaching from the logic of Western modernity to create other options.              | Walter Mignolo, Samir Amin               |
| **Epistemicide**                   | The destruction and erasure of indigenous knowledge systems by colonial power.                                         | Boaventura de Sousa Santos (Portugal)    |
| **Necropolitics**                  | The sovereignty of the postcolony defined by the power to dictate who may live and who must die.                       | Achille Mbembe (Cameroon)                |
| **Settler Colonialism**            | A distinct structure where the colonizer comes to stay and eliminate the native (e.g., US, Canada, Israel, Australia). | Patrick Wolfe, Glen Coulthard            |
| **Politics of Refusal**            | Rejecting the state's "recognition" and turning inward to rebuild indigenous nationhood.                               | Glen Coulthard (Dene)                    |
| **Radical Resurgence**             | The practice of living according to Indigenous norms and ethics *now*, regardless of the settler state.                | Leanne Betasamosake Simpson (Nishnaabeg) |
| **Pluriverse**                     | A world in which many worlds fit; the rejection of universalism in favor of multiple, coexisting ontologies.           | Zapatistas, Mignolo                      |
